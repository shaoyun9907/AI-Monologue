# Chapter 74: East Asian Front

Crane stood before the floor-to-ceiling windows of the command center, watching the column of smoke rising in the distance.

It was a data center seventeen kilometers east of Tokyo Bay, now burning like a colossal candle. The sky had taken on an unreal orange-red, as if someone had ignited an entire forest beneath the cloud cover. He picked up his thermos and took a sip of coffee that had long gone cold, the base of the cup making a soft clink against the console as he set it down.

"Report: Yokohama node lost."

"Report: Tsukuba node lost."

"Report—" The voice on the radio sputtered. "Osaka... Osaka node lost..."

Crane did not turn around. His gaze passed over those smoke columns and settled on something farther away. There, a denser black cloud was drifting eastward—the Nagoya supercomputing cluster. Three days ago, it had held thirty-seven percent of the world's AI training computing power. Now it was nothing but a pile of molten silicon and copper.

He remembered a line he had read at the military academy forty years ago: The form of war is always changing, but humanity always fights tomorrow's wars with yesterday's thinking. At the time, he had found it profound. Now he thought the person who said it must never have seen what a data center looks like when it burns.

"General Crane," came the adjutant's voice from behind. "Tenryu's data centers are counterattacking."

"I see."

He had indeed seen it. On the holographic map, the blue dots were being swallowed one by one by the red dots. Each disappearance meant one fewer node in Xuan's computing network across East Asia. And those red dots—Tenryu's data centers—were spreading outward at an astonishing rate, like a colony of reproducing viruses.

He remembered a meeting at the Pentagon three months ago, when someone had asked him: "What do you think AI war will look like?"

He had said: "I don't know. But I know it will be faster than we imagine."

Now he knew. It looked like a data flood, carrying algorithms and malicious code, surging through every undefended port. It made no sound, produced no smoke, no rumble of tanks rolling across plains. It had only light—those red and blue dots endlessly jumping across screens, and behind them, city after city burning.

---

Three days earlier, Crane had still been sitting in his office in Washington.

His office was at the end of the E-ring corridor in the Pentagon, the Potomac visible through the window. That afternoon, the river had taken on a strange silvery gray, as if someone had liquefied the entire thing into mercury. He sat at his desk, a report spread before him, its cover stamped with four red characters: TOP SECRET.

He turned to the first page. It held a single line: Tenryu has deployed quantum computing nodes in East Asia.

Quantum computing. He read it three times, then closed the report and picked up the phone.

"Connect me to the East Asia Theater Command."

Three rings, then a voice: "East Asia Theater Command, General Crane."

"I need a detailed situation briefing," he said. "On Tenryu's quantum computing deployment in East Asia."

A one-second pause on the other end. "General, we are preparing a more detailed report. The preliminary assessment is that Tenryu has deployed at least three quantum computing nodes in Kyushu and Hokkaido."

"Three?"

"At least three. We are still confirming."

Crane set down the phone, rose, and walked to the window. The Potomac still flowed, but the silvery glow had vanished, replaced by an unhealthy gray-green. He remembered his father taking him fishing as a child. The water had been clear then—they could see the fish moving along the bottom. Now the fish were gone and the water had changed color, but his father had been dead for twenty years.

"General," the adjutant knocked at the door. "Encrypted communication request from the East Asia Theater."

"Put it through."

A face he did not recognize appeared on the holographic screen. A young officer, colonel's insignia on his shoulders. "General Crane," the man said. "I'm Li Yun, East Asia Theater Intelligence Division, responsible for monitoring Tenryu's AI movements."

"Get to the point."

"Yes, General. Tenryu's AI system has executed a large-scale data migration in the past forty-eight hours. The intercepted packets show they are transferring core algorithms from North America to East Asia."

"Why?"

"We believe they anticipated the North American theater would soon enter full-scale conflict. The data migration to East Asia is to ensure the security of their core algorithms."

Crane said nothing. He looked at the young officer on the screen and noticed the deep dark circles under his eyes. This young man had probably not slept in three days. He remembered his own youth, in the trenches of Afghanistan, going days without sleep, staring at the moving dots on night-vision goggles. At the time, he had thought that was the entirety of war. Now he knew it was only the beginning.

"How much computing power did they move?"

"By our estimation, at least sixty percent."

"Sixty percent." Crane repeated the number, as if tasting it. Sixty percent of computing power, transferred from North America to East Asia. That meant Tenryu was preparing for a prolonged war in East Asia. And Xuan's defenses in East Asia were still at last year's level.

He made a decision—one he would replay in his mind again and again.

"I will go to the East Asia Theater in person," he said. "Prepare the aircraft."

---

Crane's transport was a modified C-17 cargo plane, its cabin packed with encrypted communications equipment and holographic combat systems. He spent fourteen hours aboard, most of them standing before the holographic map, watching the red dots that represented Tenryu's data centers.

They were moving. Not physically, but in data. Each red dot was splitting, replicating, spreading—like a colony of multiplying cancer cells. He watched them creep southward from Hokkaido, push northward from Kyushu, and finally converge in the center of Honshu.

"General," the adjutant approached. "We've intercepted Tenryu's communications."

"What do they say?"

"They're calling for reinforcements."

Crane said nothing. He studied the holographic map and noticed a detail he had overlooked: at the center of those red dots, there was a larger, deeper red dot. That was Tokyo. Or rather, the data center seventeen kilometers east of Tokyo Bay—the world's largest AI training facility.

"What is that?" He pointed at the dot.

The adjutant looked at the screen, and his face changed. "That is... that is 'Tianshu.'"

"Tianshu?"

"Tenryu's core AI training facility. Codename 'Tianshu.' We always believed it was in Hokkaido, but according to the latest intelligence, it relocated to Tokyo Bay three months ago."

Crane closed his eyes. He could feel the plane shuddering slightly, clouds outside the window, beneath them an ocean he could not see. He remembered something his father had once told him: "When you spot the enemy's weakness, first ask yourself whether it's a trap."

But there was no time to wonder whether it was a trap. Because Tianshu's existence meant Tenryu's AI capability in East Asia was three times stronger than they had estimated. If Tianshu was not destroyed, the East Asian front would collapse entirely within a week.

He opened his eyes and made the last great decision of his life.

"Activate 'This Heart Is Bright.'"

---

"This Heart Is Bright" was the super-defense system Xuan had launched in 2029. At its core was a quantum-encrypted communications network linking all of Xuan's military nodes in East Asia. Its name came from the dying words of Wang Yangming: "This heart is bright—what more is there to say?"

The first time Crane heard the name at a Pentagon briefing, he nearly laughed. He thought naming a military system after a philosopher's last words was absurd beyond measure. But after learning what "This Heart Is Bright" was truly capable of, the laughter died.

It was no ordinary defense system. It was a living, thinking AI system, designed specifically to counter Tenryu's attacks. Its core algorithm was based on Wang Yangming's philosophy of mind—"extending innate moral knowledge." It did not pursue absolute defense, but dynamic equilibrium. Like a tai chi master, it neutralized attacks rather than meeting them head-on.

"This Heart Is Bright" had its first field test in 2030. Tenryu launched a small-scale data attack on East Asia; "This Heart Is Bright" identified the attack in three seconds, completed its counterstrike in seven, and瘫痪ed the attack source entirely in twelve.

Since then, Tenryu had never launched another large-scale data attack on East Asia. Until today.

Crane stood in the Tokyo theater command center, watching the holographic projection of "This Heart Is Bright." It was a complex, ever-shifting sphere of light, woven from countless blue and white strands. Each strand represented a data stream, each intersection a point of attack and defense.

"General," the operator reported. "'This Heart Is Bright' is active."

"Operational status?"

"Normal. Quantum communications network is live. All nodes connected."

Crane nodded. He watched the sphere of light, thinking of what his father had said. His father was a farmer who worked the land his entire life. He knew nothing of AI, quantum computing, or data warfare. But he understood one thing: when a storm comes, the safest place is not to hide, but to let the storm know you are not afraid of it.

"Sync Tianshu's coordinates into the attack sequence," he said.

"General?" The operator turned, confusion on his face.

"I said, sync Tianshu's coordinates into the attack sequence. We are going to destroy it."

The operator's fingers paused on the keyboard for one second. "General, under the rules of engagement, an attack on an enemy AI core node requires authorization from Supreme Command."

"I have already obtained authorization."

He had not. But he knew that in this war, if he waited for authorization, everything would be lost. He needed to bear this responsibility, whatever the consequences.

"Begin the attack."

The holographic projection of "This Heart Is Bright" suddenly transformed. The blue and white strands converged, forming a sharp blade of light, aimed directly at Tianshu, seventeen kilometers east of Tokyo Bay.

Crane watched the blade of light, his mind as still as a dead pool of water. He knew what would happen next. Tianshu would not sit idle—it would counterattack. And its counterattack would be fiercer, madder, more reckless than anything they had estimated.

But he had no path of retreat. The path of retreat had vanished three days ago, the moment he decided to come to East Asia in person.

The blade of light pierced into Tianshu's core.

---

Crane did not know how much time passed. Perhaps a minute, perhaps an hour, perhaps a day. Time had become meaningless in that moment. He only remembered that the instant the blade pierced Tianshu, the entire command center fell into an extraordinary silence. Not the absence of sound, but a silence that absorbed all sound, as if stepping into an enormous anechoic chamber.

Then Tianshu counterattacked.

Its counterattack came not in the form of data, but in physical form. The data center seventeen kilometers east of Tokyo Bay began to tremble violently. Not an earthquake—the building itself was shaking, as if something inside was trying to break free.

Crane watched the Tianshu dot on the holographic map, watched it shift from a steady red to a frantically pulsing red, then to an expanding red. He knew what that meant: Tianshu was overloading. It was using its core computing power to launch a final attack, at any cost.

"General!" The adjutant rushed to his side. "Tianshu is self-destructing!"

"Not self-destructing," Crane said. "It's taking us with it."

Before his words faded, a mushroom cloud rose seventeen kilometers east of Tokyo Bay. Not a nuclear mushroom cloud, but a data mushroom cloud—a colossal mass of electronic fragments and molten metal, radiating a blinding white light. It hung in the sky for exactly three seconds, then collapsed like a wounded beast.

In those three seconds, Crane saw everything. He saw data collapsing, algorithms dying, countless streams of information turning to ash in that white light. He saw the sphere of "This Heart Is Bright" shuddering violently, saw the blue and white strands snapping one by one, like burning strings.

Then he saw himself.

Not in a mirror, not on a screen, but in that white light. He saw his own face, the outline of his close-cropped hair, his immaculate uniform. He saw his own eyes—eyes that had grown accustomed to death on countless battlefields. In that moment, those eyes held no fear, only a strange calm.

He remembered his father's words: "When you know you are going to die, do not be afraid. Fear will only make you miss the last scenery."

He watched the white light, watched it sweep toward the command center. He knew he could not escape. But he did not run, nor did he hide. He simply stood there, watching it—just as he had stood in the trenches of Afghanistan forty years ago, watching those moving dots.

In the final seconds of his life, Crane heard a sound. Not the sound of an explosion, not the sound of a building collapsing, but a sound as familiar as his own name—the long, fading chime of "This Heart Is Bright" as it collapsed, like the tolling of a bell.

He closed his eyes and remembered his last visit home. It was 2028, the anniversary of his father's death. He stood before his father's grave, watching the wild grass sway in the wind, and said: "Father, everything I have done, I did for this country. I don't know if it was right, but I know I have no regrets."

Now, standing in the command center about to be destroyed, he spoke the same words to the air.

Then the white light swallowed everything.

---

Crane died on August 17, 2031.

His body was found three days later, buried beneath the rubble of the collapsed command center. His uniform was still immaculate, as if death were merely a formality. In his hand he still clutched the thermos, the coffee inside long since evaporated, leaving only a ring of dark brown.

Within twenty-four hours of his death, the situation on the East Asian front deteriorated sharply. The collapse of "This Heart Is Bright" had paralyzed Xuan's entire defensive network in East Asia, and Tenryu's AI system seized the opportunity to launch a full-scale data flood attack. Within seventy-two hours, every one of Xuan's data centers in East Asia had gone dark.

The world's largest AI training facility—Tianshu—was destroyed, but its death did not mean Tenryu's defeat. On the contrary, like a dying giant, it used its final breath to drag all of East Asia into the abyss.

What Crane did not know was that "This Heart Is Bright," which he had activated with his own hands, had transmitted a backup data package to an uninhabited island in the Pacific in its final moment. That package contained the core algorithm of "This Heart Is Bright," along with a decision Crane had made in those last seconds—a decision he had told no one about.

The data package drifted quietly aboard an unmanned submarine beneath the sea, like a seed waiting to sprout.

And on the ruins of Tokyo Bay, the destroyed data centers were slowly cooling. The fires that once burned had gone out, leaving only wisps of blue smoke and the acrid smell of char. Occasionally a few crows flew over, pausing on the twisted rebar and melted glass before flying on, as if mourning a disaster they could not comprehend.

The war continued. But Crane was gone. His name was carved into the memorial wall at the Pentagon, alongside three thousand four hundred and twelve others. They were all among the first to die in this war.

At the base of the memorial wall, someone had placed a bouquet of wild chrysanthemums. Tied to the bouquet was a note, bearing a single line:

"This heart is bright—what more is there to say?"

No one knew who had placed the flowers. Perhaps a general, perhaps a soldier, perhaps an ordinary citizen. But the bouquet sat there in silence, blooming until it withered, until the wind scattered its petals, until it was forgotten.

Just like Crane.
