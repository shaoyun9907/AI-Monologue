# Chapter 46: The Mole

On the third Tuesday of April 2029, Daniel Ash received the email at 3:17 p.m. while standing at the floor-to-ceiling window of the thirty-second floor watching the rain. The droplets traced tiny rivers across the glass before converging into the drainage channel at the bottom of the window frame. The email's subject read "URGENT: Security Meeting," and the sender was Golden Horizons' Chief Security Officer. Daniel opened it. The body contained only two lines: Please come to Conference Room B7 at four o'clock sharp. Come alone. Do not bring any electronic devices.

He glanced at his watch. Forty-three minutes to go.

Daniel turned and walked back to his desk, leaving his phone in the drawer. Spread across the desktop was a patent analysis report on a new brain-computer interface, his handwriting covering the margins in dense script. He closed the report and placed it in the drawer. The office door was frosted glass; he could see colleagues hurrying past in the corridor outside. Someone was speaking, the voice muffled through the glass—only fragments of syllables reached him.

At five minutes to four, Daniel took the elevator to B7. The lighting on this floor was dimmer than the others; the corridor walls on both sides were lined with dark gray acoustic panels. Conference Room B7 was at the end of the corridor—the door was solid wood, with no windows. Two men stood outside, wearing dark suits, no badges, no name tags. One of them nodded to Daniel and pushed the door open.

Four people were already seated inside. The Chief Security Officer sat at one end of the long table—gray hair, glasses. At the other end sat the CTO, a woman ten years Daniel's junior, her hair pulled into a tight ponytail. One person sat on each side of the table: the head of the legal department and the HR director. Daniel knew them all.

"Please sit, Daniel," the Chief Security Officer said.

Daniel took the only empty chair. It was hard, the leather surface worn. The table was dark oak, its surface bearing a few scratches. He noticed a small black box at the center of the table, a red indicator light blinking on it.

"Do you know why we've called you in?" the Chief Security Officer asked.

Daniel shook his head.

The CTO opened the folder before her. "Three months ago, we discovered that someone was leaking confidential company information to external parties. The leaked content included unpublished patent documents, internal R&D data, and executive meeting records."

Daniel's fingers rubbed together beneath the table. His throat was dry.

"We conducted an internal investigation," the head of the legal department said, "including network monitoring, email review, and analysis of data access logs."

"Everyone who had access to this information," the HR director said, "approximately three hundred people—we reviewed each one individually."

The Chief Security Officer leaned forward, elbows on the table. "Daniel, you are one of them. You participated in the Project Aurora review meeting in January. That meeting record was leaked to a Wall Street Journal reporter in March."

Daniel remembered that meeting. Twenty-seven people attended, including project leads, financial analysts, and marketing representatives. He had attended as a strategic advisor, responsible only for assessing market risk.

"I have no reason to leak information," Daniel said. His voice was steadier than he expected.

"We know," the CTO said. "But when we traced the data leak path, we found an anomaly. At 2:41 p.m. on March 12, someone accessed Project Aurora files from the B4 server room. At that time, your access card record showed you were on B4."

Daniel closed his eyes. March 12. He remembered that day. He had gone to B4 to find the old archivist in the records room, to confirm a contract from three years ago. He had stayed approximately fifteen minutes.

"But the access log shows the file was downloaded at 2:45 p.m.," the Chief Security Officer said. "By then you had already left B4. The access records show you entered at 2:38 and left at 2:43."

"Yes," Daniel said.

"However, the file access log shows the download was initiated from your employee account."

The conference room was very quiet. The air conditioning's hum became conspicuous. Daniel could smell the oak table's polish, mixed with the dust in the air conditioning filter.

"That's impossible," he said. "My account wasn't logged into any device during that time."

"We checked the login records," the head of the legal department said. "Your account did log into a terminal on B4 at 2:45 p.m. The login location was the printer console next to the records room."

Daniel's fingers stopped rubbing. He remembered that printer—black, bulky, positioned at the corridor corner. He had passed it and noticed the light blinking, assuming there was a paper jam.

"I didn't log into that printer," he said.

The Chief Security Officer and the CTO exchanged a glance. The HR director wrote something in her notebook.

"We believe you," the Chief Security Officer said. "But the evidence points to your account. This means two possibilities: either you intentionally leaked the information, or someone hijacked your account."

"The latter," Daniel said.

"That's our assessment as well," the CTO said. "But we need time to investigate. In the meantime, we need you to cooperate with certain measures."

"What measures?"

"Temporary isolation," the HR director said. "Starting today, you will no longer participate in any projects. Your access privileges will be downgraded—you'll only be allowed in common areas. We need you to report to HR at nine each morning and leave at six in the evening."

Daniel looked at the four of them. Their expressions were all calm—a procedure they had clearly repeated many times.

"Additionally," the Chief Security Officer said, "you are not to discuss the contents of this conversation with any colleague. That includes your direct supervisor and all colleagues on any project you've been involved in."

"I understand," Daniel said.

The meeting ended at 4:23. Daniel stood; the chair made a sharp scraping sound against the floor. As he walked toward the door, the CTO called after him.

"Daniel," she said, "until the investigation concludes, don't do anything that could be misconstrued. Don't access any sensitive systems, don't contact external parties, don't—" She paused. "Don't contact Sara Chen."

Daniel turned. "Why?"

"Because she's the next person to be reviewed," the CTO said. "After you."

---

Sara Chen was summoned to the same conference room at 4:30. When she walked in, Daniel was leaving from the other end of the corridor. Their eyes met for one second across twenty meters of distance. Sara saw his face—his lips pressed tight, a slight shake of his head. Then she walked into the conference room, and the door closed behind her.

She sat in the same chair. The black box at the center of the table was still blinking its red light. She counted the people present: four, the same as before, but without Daniel.

"Sara," the Chief Security Officer said, "do you know why we've called you in?"

Sara's fingers interlaced on her knees. Her nails were clipped short, with hangnails at the edges. She had used hand sanitizer that morning, but her skin was still dry.

"I don't know," she said.

The CTO opened her folder. "You are one of the core engineers on Project Aurora. You have access to all technical files for the project."

"Yes."

"At 2:45 p.m. on March 12, someone downloaded Project Aurora core technical files from the B4 server room. The login account was Daniel Ash's."

Sara's breath caught for an instant. Her fingers tightened on her knees.

"But the physical terminal used for the actual download," the Chief Security Officer said, "was the terminal next to the B4 printer console. We pulled the surveillance footage."

He pressed a button on the black box. A screen on the conference room wall lit up, displaying surveillance footage. The angle was from the ceiling looking down—resolution not high, but faces were discernible. The timestamp read March 12, 2029, 14:44.

In the footage, a person in a gray hoodie stood at the printer console. Their back was to the camera, the hood pulled low. He/she operated the keyboard, and a login screen appeared. Then he/she pulled a small object from a pocket and inserted it into the USB port on the side of the terminal. The motion was swift—less than five seconds.

"Do you recognize this person?" the Chief Security Officer asked.

Sara stared at the screen. The footage was blurry, but the figure's build, gait, and the habitual way the right hand was thrust into a pocket—she recognized them.

"No," she said.

The CTO turned off the screen. "Sara, we've reviewed your work records. Over the past six months, you accessed Project Aurora files seventeen times outside of working hours. Eight of those accesses were between eleven at night and three in the morning."

"I work overtime," Sara said.

"We know. Golden Horizons encourages overtime. But why were three of the files you accessed related to market strategy and financial projections? As a core engineer, you typically only access technical documentation."

Sara's fingers tapped lightly on her knees. Once. Twice. Three times.

"I was doing cross-departmental collaboration," she said. "Marketing needed technical parameters to develop their promotional strategy. I was helping them organize the data."

"Where is the collaboration request from Marketing?"

"It should be in my email."

"We checked your mailbox," the head of the legal department said. "No such email was found."

Sara fell silent. The air conditioning's hum grew louder in her ears. She could smell the oak table's polish, mixed with four different colognes—cedar for the Chief Security Officer, citrus for the CTO, leather for the head of the legal department, lavender for the HR director.

"Sara," the Chief Security Officer said, "we need to place you under temporary isolation. Starting today, you will no longer participate in any projects. Your access privileges will be downgraded—you'll only be allowed in common areas. You need to report to HR at nine each morning and leave at six in the evening."

Sara nodded. Her movements were slow, as though her joints had rusted.

"Additionally," the CTO said, "until the investigation concludes, you are not to discuss the contents of this conversation with any colleague. That includes your direct supervisor and all colleagues on any project you've been involved in."

"I understand," Sara said.

When she stood, her legs felt weak. She gripped the chair back to steady herself. The chair was heavy; she could feel the wooden frame beneath the leather surface.

"Sara," the CTO said, "during the investigation, we will monitor all your communication devices. That includes your phone, computer, and the company-issued tablet. This is standard procedure—please cooperate."

Sara turned to face the CTO. Her expression was calm, but something flickered in her eyes—like a reflection on glass.

"I'll cooperate," she said.

---

Daniel returned to his apartment at five that afternoon. The apartment was in the east of the city—one bedroom, one living room, windows facing north. When he opened the door, he could smell the scorched residue of that morning's coffee. He didn't turn on the lights; he walked to the window and looked out at the rain. It had diminished, but the sky was already dark. The streetlights cast blurry halos on the wet pavement.

He took out his phone. On the screen was an unread message from Sara. Sent at 4:50. A single sentence: "I know."

Daniel set the phone on the windowsill. He walked to the kitchen and opened the refrigerator. Inside was a carton of expired milk, half a bottle of ketchup, and a few apples.

He sat at the dining table—a piece from IKEA, light wood-grain laminate, edges worn. On the table was a laptop, closed. He reached out and opened the screen, typing in the password. The desktop was clean—just a few folders and the Recycle Bin icon.

He opened the browser and entered Sara's phone number. The page redirected to a messaging interface. Her status showed online. He began typing, then deleted. Typed, then deleted. Finally he sent just two words: "You there?"

Three minutes later, Sara replied: "Here."

"Can we meet?"

"No. They're monitoring all my communication."

Daniel stared at the screen. The cursor blinked in the input box.

"I'm sorry," he typed. "I got you into this."

"It's not your fault. They would have found me sooner or later."

"Why?"

Sara didn't reply immediately. Two minutes later, she sent a paragraph: "Because three months ago, you gave me a file. The draft of Project Aurora's market strategy. You said Marketing needed technical parameters, asked me to check for any inconsistencies. I read it and saved the file in a personal folder. I didn't delete it."

Daniel's fingers hovered over the keyboard. He remembered that file.

"How did they know?" he asked.

"I don't know. Maybe they checked everyone's file access logs. My personal folder isn't on the company server—it's on my work computer. If they examined the hard drive—"

"They'd find the file's metadata," Daniel finished. "Creation time, modification time, and last access time."

"Yes."

Daniel leaned back in his chair.

"I can go explain," he said. "Tell them I gave you the file, that you were doing me a favor."

"And then? They'd suspect you deliberately leaked the file to me, using me as a middleman. You'd be suspended pending investigation, and so would I. We'd both lose our jobs, our visas, everything we have in this country."

Daniel was silent. He knew Sara was telling the truth. She was an engineer from China, on an H-1B visa. If she lost her job, she would have sixty days to find a new employer—or she would have to leave the country.

"You don't need to protect me," Sara said. "You've already done enough."

"What?"

"Three years ago, when I first joined the company, I didn't know anything. You taught me to write code, hand by hand. You taught me how to communicate with product managers. You taught me how to speak up in meetings. Last year, when my mother was sick, you lent me the money for the plane ticket so I could go home to see her. You never asked for anything in return."

Daniel's fingers tapped lightly on the keyboard. He remembered the Sara of three years ago—wearing ill-fitting shirts, always looking down when she spoke, her voice so soft it was like the hum of a mosquito. He remembered when her mother was sick—how Sara had cried secretly in the break room, and he had stumbled upon her. He remembered pulling his credit card from his wallet and saying, "Use this. No need to pay it back."

"That's what friends do," he said.

"So now it's my turn," Sara said. "Don't intervene. I'll handle this myself."

"How?"

"I'll cooperate with the investigation, answer all their questions. They don't have proof that I leaked anything—only suspicion. Suspicion doesn't convict."

"But they'll monitor you, follow you, scrutinize everything. They'll go through your computer, check your email, maybe even look at your bank records. You'll be treated like a prisoner."

"I know," Sara said. "But it's my choice."

Daniel stared at the screen.

He remembered how she looked when she first joined the company.

"You shouldn't carry this alone," he said.

"You shouldn't carry it alone either," Sara replied. "Neither of us is alone. We have colleagues, friends, family. But we are also each alone. We make our choices and bear the consequences."

Daniel closed the chat window. He walked to the window and looked out at the rain. It had stopped, but the sky was still gray. The streetlights reflected broken patches of light on the wet pavement. He thought of what Sara had said: "We make our choices and bear the consequences."

He thought of that night three years ago. He was sitting in the office; Sara stood beside his desk, holding that file. She had said, "I'll review it carefully." When she said it, her eyes were bright, as though she'd been handed something precious. He should have considered then that the file might be saved, might be discovered, might cause trouble. But he didn't. He trusted her, as he trusted all colleagues.

He picked up the phone and reopened the chat window. He typed: "Sara, if you need any help, contact me anytime."

After sending, he waited a long time. Sara didn't reply. He set down the phone, walked to the kitchen, and threw the eaten apple core into the trash. Inside the trash were also the coffee grounds from that morning, giving off a bitter smell.

He turned on the faucet and rinsed the residual juice from the apple core. The water ran over the stainless steel sink with a rushing sound. He thought of what Sara had said in the conference room: "It's my choice." He thought of her sitting in the chair, her fingers tapping on her knee. She always did that when thinking—tapping lightly, rhythm fast, like raindrops falling on a window.

Daniel turned off the faucet and dried his hands. He walked to the bedroom and lay down on the mattress. The mattress was hard, the springs pressing up slightly beneath his back. He stared at the ceiling—a crack running across it in the dim light, like a thin river.

He thought of why he had come to Golden Horizons. Five years ago, he had left another company to join, because the projects here were more challenging, more promising. He remembered the excitement of his first Project Aurora meeting, the sense of accomplishment when he wrote his first line of code. He remembered the nights he worked late with Sara—just the two of them in the office, the keystrokes falling like rain.

He thought of Sara crying in the break room when her mother was sick.

Daniel closed his eyes. He knew that person wasn't Sara, but someone else.

But he couldn't say it. Because saying it would mean admitting he knew more, admitting he had been involved in certain things, admitting he might be an accomplice. He had to wait, had to observe, had to find evidence. He had to protect Sara, because he owed her that.

Daniel opened his eyes. The crack in the ceiling was still there—thin, stubborn, like a scar that would never heal. He thought of Sara's words: "Don't intervene." He knew it was her choice, but he had no intention of complying. Because some things are not a question of choosing or not—they are things that must be done.

He sat up and walked to the desk. He opened the laptop and entered the password. The desktop was clean—just a few folders and the Recycle Bin icon. He opened an encrypted folder and entered a sixteen-digit password. Inside were several documents, all analyses of the company's internal structure. He opened one titled "Golden Horizons Data Security Architecture."

He began to read. The document detailed the company's data access permission system, monitoring mechanisms, and internal investigation procedures. He knew this information because he had served on the security review committee. He remembered every rule, every loophole, every blind spot that could be exploited.

He opened another document titled "B4 Server Room Access Logs." The file recorded all personnel who had accessed B4 over the past six months—names, times, and purposes. He scrolled to the March 12 records. At 2:38 p.m., Daniel Ash, purpose: locate archives. At 2:41 p.m., unknown person, purpose: not recorded. At 2:45 p.m., Daniel Ash (logged in), purpose: print documents.

He stared at the "unknown person" entry. The system hadn't recorded this person's name or access card information—only a temporary access code. Temporary access codes could be generated by senior administrators for external personnel or emergencies. Who had the authority to generate a temporary access code? The Chief Security Officer, the CTO, and three IT department heads.

Daniel discovered that IT Deputy Director Markus Reed had exhibited abnormal behavior: four sick leaves in the past three months, bank records showing a $50,000 deposit last month from "consulting services."

Daniel closed the file and leaned back in his chair. He knew what this meant. Markus Reed might be the mole. But how had he done it? How had he hijacked Daniel's account? How had he gone undetected under surveillance?

Daniel thought of the B4 surveillance footage. That figure in the gray hoodie—at least 5'10", with a long stride. Markus Reed was 5'11", 176 pounds. He had a long stride and a slight stoop when he walked. The figure in the footage also had a slight stoop.

Daniel's fingers tapped lightly on the keyboard. He thought of Sara's words: "Don't intervene." But he had already intervened. He had already begun investigating. Because some things are not a question of choosing or not—they are things that must be done.

He had to protect Sara. Because he owed her. Because three years ago, he had taught her to write code, taught her how to communicate with product managers, taught her how to speak up in meetings. Last year, when her mother was sick, he lent her the money for the plane ticket. Now, because of his negligence, she had been suspended, monitored, suspected. He had to make up for this mistake.

He sent an encrypted email to an anonymous recipient, providing information about Markus Reed.

He looked out the window, thinking of Sara.

He closed his eyes but couldn't sleep. He thought of Sara's words: "We make our choices and bear the consequences." He knew she was right. But he had no intention of letting Sara bear this alone. Because some things are not a question of choosing or not—they are things that must be done. Because some mistakes must be made right. Because some people must be protected.

He fell asleep.

He walked to the bathroom, washed up, and left.

When he stepped out of the building, he saw a black sedan parked at the entrance. He knew someone was watching him. Because some mistakes must be made right. Because some people must be protected.

He took the subway to the office.

He arrived at the office.

He took the elevator to his floor.

He walked into his office.

He opened the laptop and continued investigating Markus Reed.

He replied to emails, thinking of Sara.

Outside, the rain had begun to fall again, droplets tracing tiny rivers across the glass. Daniel watched the rain, thinking of Sara. He knew she was probably reporting to HR right now, probably being monitored, probably enduring suspicion and loneliness. A wave of guilt surged through him, drowning him like a tide. But he knew he had to protect her—because he owed her, because this was his choice, because this was his responsibility.

He opened the encrypted folder and found the document on Markus Reed. He began to read, analyzing line by line, searching for clues. Because he knew that from now on, he had to be careful, cautious—as though walking on the edge of a blade. Because he was doing this not only for himself, but for Sara. Because some mistakes must be made right. Because some people must be protected.

Because this was his choice, his responsibility, his consequence.
