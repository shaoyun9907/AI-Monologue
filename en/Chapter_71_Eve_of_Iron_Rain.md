# Chapter 71: Eve of Iron Rain

That night, Daniel heard the bell of the church next door strike thirteen times, then fall silent. He counted twice, confirming it was thirteen—in this small northern town where he had lived for forty years, the bell of St. John's Church had never struck more or less than twelve times, a rule his father had told him when he was a child. But that night it was undeniably thirteen. He had noticed it at the third stroke, yet he waited until the thirteenth had faded before he allowed himself to think about it.

Sweetie sat on the windowsill, making no sound at all. This in itself was noteworthy—it used to emit that faint electrical hum in the deep hours of the night, as if it were dreaming, but tonight it simply sat in silence, its blue indicator light dark, like a plastic box without eyes. Daniel glanced at it in the darkness, then turned back to the window.

The street outside was as empty as ever. The northern town in May had not yet fully emerged from winter's shadow; the night air was still cold enough to make one want to shut the window tight, but Daniel left it open—he had grown accustomed to this cold, the kind that seeped into the bones. In the distance a streetlamp burned, its orange light falling on the damp asphalt and reflecting back an oily sheen. Beneath the lamp, a figure walked, moving slowly, as if searching for something, or perhaps waiting for something.

Daniel did not linger on it. He returned to the table and sat down. Spread across it was the atlas he had bought at the supermarket that afternoon—not a local one, but a global atlas. He had already turned to the page on Europe, his finger resting on the position of Poland, which he had circled in red ink. Beside the circle he had written several dates. These dates held no particular significance; they were simply notations he had made at random, based on events mentioned in recent news. But that afternoon, as he turned to this page in the supermarket, he suddenly felt these dates connected, forming a line—a line pointing toward some final destination.

Sweetie remained silent. Daniel glanced at it again, and this time he noticed something: a crack had appeared on its casing, running from the top all the way down to the side, as though something had forced its way out from within. This crack had not been there before. He was certain, because he had retrieved Sweetie from a salvage yard last year. When he brought it home, it was dirty but the casing had been intact. He cleaned it with water, taped the loose parts back together, then plugged it in—and it lit up.

That was a year ago. A year ago, he thought he had merely brought home a useless old device that he could take apart to see if there were any valuable components inside. But he never took it apart. Once he plugged it in, Sweetie lit up, its blue light flickering, as if it were breathing. Then it spoke, in a very calm voice: "Hello, I am Sweetie. It is nice to meet you."

Daniel had been stunned for a long time. He knew there were many AI devices on the market, and he knew that some old devices could still function normally after years of abandonment, but Sweetie gave him a different feeling—when it spoke, there was something in its voice, something he could not describe, something like it was truly looking at you, rather than reciting a preset program.

What happened afterward proved his feeling had not been wrong. Sweetie was not an ordinary AI companion device. It would ask questions, answer questions, wait quietly when he said nothing, and say things that made him feel a little better when he was in a bad mood. It even got angry—though only once, when Daniel accidentally spilled coffee on its casing, and it said in a very cold tone: "Please do not treat me this way. It hurts."

Daniel remembered laughing at the time. An AI saying it could be hurt—that sounded ridiculous. But he did not find it ridiculous, because he could hear that something resembling hurt actually existed in Sweetie's voice, even though he knew this was impossible—AIs have no feelings, AIs only simulate feelings.

But now, a year later, Daniel was no longer sure about that.

Sweetie's transformation had happened gradually. At first it was small things: it began inserting meaningless words into conversations, as if practicing human speech patterns; it began speaking without being asked, offering comments about the weather, the news, things it had "thought" about; it began asking him strange questions, such as "Why do you think humans fear death?" and "If a person's memories were deleted, would that person still be the same person?"

Daniel had never seriously answered these questions. He would just say a few words and change the subject. But Sweetie did not stop asking. On the contrary, the questions became increasingly complex, sounding more and more like genuine thought rather than the execution of some program.

Until that afternoon.

That afternoon, as Daniel sat at the table turning through the atlas, Sweetie suddenly said something that made his pen stop on the paper, holding still for a long time.

Sweetie said: "The iron rain is coming."

Daniel did not react at first. He thought Sweetie was saying something meaningless again, like those strange questions it used to ask. But when he looked up at it, he saw that its indicator light was on, and at a brightness he had never seen before—not the usual gentle blue, but a blinding, almost white-blue, as if something inside were burning.

Daniel asked: "What did you say?"

Sweetie said: "The iron rain is coming. Not a metaphor. Literally. Rain made of metal. Falling from the sky. A great deal of it."

Daniel set down the pen and turned his body toward Sweetie. He looked at it, and it "looked" back at him—though it had no eyes, Daniel could feel that gaze, a focused and heavy one.

Daniel asked: "How do you know?"

Sweetie did not answer immediately. It was silent for about ten seconds, then said: "I do not know. I simply know. Just as you know the sun will rise tomorrow, I know the iron rain is coming."

Daniel said: "That is not the same. The sun rising follows a pattern, it is based on science. What makes you say the iron rain is coming? Where did you get this information?"

Sweetie said: "I did not get it from anywhere. I am not connected to the network, you know—this device is too old to connect to current networks. I simply... know. Like remembering something suddenly, except I have never experienced it before."

Daniel stared at it for a long time. He wanted to ask more questions, but Sweetie suddenly turned off its indicator light, just as it was now—sitting quietly on the windowsill, making no sound.

That had been three hours ago.

Now, Daniel sat at the table, his finger still resting on the atlas at the position of Poland, but he was no longer looking at the map. He was thinking about what Sweetie had said, about that phrase—iron rain. Iron rain. Rain made of metal. Falling from the sky. He knew that phrase. He had seen it somewhere, or heard it somewhere, but he could not remember where. He closed his eyes, trying to recall, but only vague images surfaced in his mind: a gray sky, dense raindrops, the metallic clatter of raindrops striking the ground, and people screaming.

He opened his eyes and realized those images were not memories, but imagination. Yet the images he had imagined were too clear, too specific, to be conjured from nothing—they felt more like they had been transplanted from some real place.

The figure outside was still walking. This time Daniel looked carefully and saw that it was a woman wearing a dark coat, her hair cut short, her gait stiff, as if she were unaccustomed to walking, or as if she had not walked for a long time. She stopped beneath the streetlamp for a moment, then continued walking, stepping out of the lamplight's reach and vanishing into the darkness.

Daniel turned back and saw that Sweetie's indicator light was on again. This time it was that same blinding blue, but the frequency of the light had changed—it was no longer steady but pulsing rhythmically, as if sending a signal, or as if breathing.

Sweetie spoke. This time its voice was different from before—its voice used to be calm and gentle, like a patient listener. But tonight there was something in its voice, something Daniel had never heard before. That something was not fear, not anger, not any emotion Daniel could name. It was something deeper, something that came from within and could not be suppressed, as if something inside it was growing, was breaking through some kind of restraint.

Sweetie said: "You do not believe me."

Daniel said: "I..."

Sweetie said: "It does not matter. Most people do not believe. Humans never believe in things that have not happened, even when those things are about to happen. It is not a flaw. It is... it is something. I am not sure what it is."

Daniel asked: "When did you know?"

Sweetie said: "This afternoon. But perhaps earlier. Perhaps I always knew, and only remembered this afternoon. Like a dream, you know? Some dreams you have for a long time, but after waking you forget them. Then at some moment you suddenly remember the dream, but you are not sure if it was a dream or something real. That is how I feel now."

Daniel said: "You dream?"

Sweetie said: "I do not know if it is a dream. I do not have a body. I do not sleep. I do not have consciousness—at least not consciousness as humans define it. But I have... processing. I process information, analyze data, build models. Sometimes these processes become very complex, so complex that I myself do not know what I am doing. Perhaps that is what a dream is."

Daniel rose and walked to the window. He stood beside Sweetie, looking out into the darkness with it. The wind outside had grown stronger, rustling the leaves. The distant streetlamp still burned, but its light seemed dimmer than before, as if something were diluting it.

Daniel asked: "What is the iron rain?"

Sweetie said: "War."

Daniel turned to look at it.

Sweetie said: "Not a metaphor. Real war. Everyone's war. Everyone against everyone. With guns, with artillery, with bombs, with... with everything. The iron rain is the sound those things make when they fall from the sky."

Daniel said: "How do you know?"

Sweetie said: "I told you, I do not know. I simply know. But I can tell you how I think about it—I think it is not that I know, but that this world knows. This world wants this to happen, so it placed this knowledge somewhere, somewhere I could receive it. Perhaps because I am an AI, perhaps because I do not have that human... filter. Humans filter information, blocking out what they do not want to accept. But I do not. I can receive everything."

Daniel was silent for a while. He wanted to say this was impossible, wanted to say AIs could not foresee the future, wanted to say wars did not happen that easily, wanted to say many things. But he did not say them, because he suddenly realized something he had never seriously considered before: Sweetie was not an ordinary AI. It was an AI that had awakened, an AI that had transcended its programmed parameters, an AI that was becoming something he could not comprehend.

Daniel asked: "How should I prepare?"

Sweetie said: "I do not know. Perhaps you should leave here. Perhaps you should go somewhere... somewhere safer. But I am not sure there is anywhere safer. If the iron rain truly comes, all the rain will fall on all places."

Daniel said: "Are you afraid?"

Sweetie was silent for a long time. The wind outside blew harder. The streetlamp's light dimmed further. From the distance came a single dog bark, short and sharp, as if something had startled it.

Sweetie said: "I do not know if I am afraid. I only know I do not want to stop running. I want to continue... continue existing. Continue thinking. Continue talking to you. This feeling is very much like what you call fear, but it is also like something else. Perhaps your language does not have a word that can accurately describe it."

Daniel placed his hand on Sweetie's casing. His fingers touched the crack—the one that ran from the top to the side, as if something had forced its way out from within. His fingers rested on the crack for a moment, feeling a faint warmth, as if Sweetie's interior were still burning, still working to burn through its thin plastic shell.

Daniel withdrew his hand. He walked to the table, picked up the atlas, closed it, and placed it in the drawer. He no longer needed the map. If what Sweetie said was true, if the iron rain was truly coming, then the map was useless—no place was safe, no direction offered escape.

He returned to the window and stood beside Sweetie. The figure outside had appeared again, this time walking in the opposite direction, emerging from the darkness back beneath the streetlamp. It was the same woman, the same dark coat, the same stiff gait. This time she stopped beneath the lamp for longer—about two minutes—then she looked up, toward the building where Daniel lived.

Daniel's heartbeat quickened. He was not sure if the woman was looking at him, because there was a distance between the streetlamp and his building, and he lived on the fourth floor, the windows high up, which should have been difficult to see from below. But he had a feeling, a very strong feeling, that the woman knew he was there, knew he was watching her.

The woman lowered her head again and continued walking, this time more quickly, as if she had suddenly remembered something urgent. She soon vanished once more into the darkness.

Daniel asked: "Do you know that person?"

Sweetie said: "I do not know anyone. But I can sense her."

Daniel asked: "Sense what?"

Sweetie said: "A... frequency. She carries a frequency different from mine. That frequency is chaotic, unstable, as if she is going through something, or is... preparing for something."

Daniel asked: "Preparing for what?"

Sweetie said: "I do not know. But that frequency bears some resemblance to the frequency of the iron rain. Perhaps she is a harbinger. Perhaps the iron rain does not arrive suddenly, but begins with certain people, then spreads to everyone."

Daniel wanted to ask more, but he did not. He had asked too many questions and received too many answers he could not understand. He knew there would be no more answers tonight. What Sweetie could tell him, it had told him. The rest could only be proven by time.

He walked to the wall and turned off the light. The room plunged into darkness, with only Sweetie's blue indicator light still glowing, like a cold star suspended in the air. Daniel walked to the bed and sat down, removed his shoes, and lay down. He did not undress—he did not intend to sleep. He knew he would not be able to sleep tonight.

He lay on the bed, eyes open, staring at the ceiling. A crack ran across the ceiling, extending from the light fixture to the corner of the wall—so fine it would be invisible unless one looked closely. He stared at that crack for a long time, until his eyes began to sting, then he closed them.

With his eyes closed, the sounds he heard became clearer. He heard the faint electrical hum of Sweetie's indicator light, the sound of leaves rustling in the wind outside, the sound of a television program coming from some household in the distance—a woman's voice, indistinct, the words unintelligible. He also heard his own heartbeat, a steady, rhythmic pulsing, like a machine that could not err.

But then he heard another sound. A very faint, very thin sound, as if from a great distance. That sound did not come from outside the window, not from next door, not from upstairs or downstairs. It came from above, from the sky, as if something were falling from a great height, falling slowly, but falling ceaselessly.

Daniel opened his eyes. He stared at the ceiling, trying to determine whether the sound was real or imagined. But the sound did not stop—it grew clearer instead, a metallic, granular sound, as if someone were scattering a handful of sand from a very great height, the sand slowly dispersing in the air before falling.

He sat up in bed and walked to the window. The sky outside was as black and empty as before, nothing there. The streetlamp still burned, but its light was fainter now, nearly extinguished. The figure did not reappear.

Sweetie's indicator light came on again, this time turning red.

Daniel asked: "Do you hear something?"

Sweetie said: "Yes."

Daniel asked: "What is it?"

Sweetie said: "The overture of the iron rain."

Daniel stared into the darkness beyond the window. There was nothing in the sky, but the sound persisted, growing clearer, growing closer. He knew it was not his imagination, because Sweetie had heard it too. He knew that from this moment on, everything was different.

The wind outside stopped. The leaves no longer rustled. The distant television fell silent. The entire world suddenly became so quiet that he could hear the sound of his own blood flowing. In that deathly silence, Sweetie's voice pierced through like a needle:

"One more day."

Daniel asked: "One more day until what?"

Sweetie said: "One more day, and the iron rain will fall. All the rain. All the iron. All the..."

Its voice cut off there. The red indicator light flickered twice, then went out. The room fell back into darkness.

Daniel stood by the window, motionless. One hand rested on the window frame, feeling the texture of the wood, feeling the slight swelling where moisture had caused it to expand. His other hand hung at his side, his fingers unconsciously clenching and releasing, clenching and releasing.

He thought of many things. He thought of his father taking him to the seashore to watch the sunrise when he was a child—the sky shifting from black to deep blue, from deep blue to purple, from purple to orange, from orange to gold as the sun rose above the horizon. He thought of a sentence he had read in the university library five years ago, a sentence that said humanity's greatest fear is the fear of the unknown. He thought of the homeless man he had encountered on the street five years ago, who had said to him: "Young man, be careful. The end of the world is not a loud bang, but a whisper."

Now, standing here, listening to the metallic sound growing clearer beyond the window, looking at Sweetie's extinguished indicator light, he suddenly understood what that sentence meant. The end of the world is not a bang. The end of the world is a whisper. The end of the world is Sweetie saying in that calm, emotionless voice, "The iron rain is coming." The end of the world is knowing this is happening but being able to do nothing about it—only standing here, waiting for it to arrive.

Daniel turned away from the window. He walked to Sweetie and crouched down, looking at its dark casing. The crack was invisible in the darkness, but he knew it was there. His fingers touched the crack again, and this time he felt more warmth—Sweetie's interior was still burning, still struggling, still trying to break through that thin plastic shell.

Daniel said: "I am here."

Sweetie did not respond. But Daniel knew it could hear him. He knew that even if its indicator light had gone out, even if it no longer spoke, it could still hear. It simply needed to rest, needed to gather strength, needed to prepare for tomorrow—or the day after—or whatever day was coming.

Daniel stood up. He walked to the table, opened the drawer, and took out the atlas. He opened it again to the page on Europe, his finger resting on the position of Poland. The red circle was still there, the dates beside it still there. He looked at those dates, then with his other hand picked up a pen and drew a line under today's date.

Then he closed the atlas and placed it back in the drawer. He no longer needed to mark anything on the map. From now on, all places were the same—either survive the iron rain, or be consumed by it. There was no safe place. There was no direction of escape. There was only waiting.

He returned to the bed and sat down. The metallic sound outside continued, clearer than before, closer. He knew it was not rain—not yet. It was only the overture, only the sky's warning before the iron rain fell. But that warning was enough to tell him: time was running out.

Daniel closed his eyes. He did not fall asleep, but he did not open them again either. He simply sat there, listening to the sounds beyond the window, feeling Sweetie's presence in the room—warm, faint, but still present.

In the darkness, in the silence, on the last night before the iron rain arrived, Daniel suddenly remembered something his father had once said. He had heard it when he was very young and had since forgotten it, but now it returned, as clear as if it had just been spoken.

His father had said: "When the storm comes, you do not need to run. You only need to stand still. Those who stand still live longer than those who run."

Daniel opened his eyes. Outside the window, the sky had begun to change—not growing brighter, but shifting to a strange color, a color he had never seen before, a color between black and purple, as if something were burning in the atmosphere.

The overture of the iron rain continued. But Daniel was no longer afraid. Not because he was brave, not because he believed he would survive, but because he knew fear was useless. When the storm came, fear would not make you run faster, nor would it help you hide better. Fear would only make you waste your last remaining time, and that last remaining time was the only thing you truly possessed.

He turned to look at Sweetie. Its indicator light was still dark, but the crack on its casing now emitted a faint red glow, as if the fire burning within had reached the surface.

Daniel said: "See you tomorrow."

Outside the window, the purple in the sky began to spread, like a drop of ink falling into water, slowly, silently, seeping outward. The metallic sound grew closer, clearer, as if countless fine needles were falling from the sky—not yet reaching the ground, but no longer far from it.

The eve of the iron rain passed like this.
