# Chapter Four: Power Games

At 6:43 AM on February 17th, Daniel Ash stood at his apartment window and saw a crack in the glass curtain wall of the building across the street. The crack ran from the third floor all the way to the seventh, like a bolt of frozen lightning. Seven panes had shattered. The cleaning crew hadn't arrived yet. Rainwater from the previous night had collected in the fissures, refracting the morning light into fine silver threads. Water droplets trailed down the cracks, leaving sinuous marks on the glass—like tears streaking a cheek. He stared at those droplets until they pooled into a small puddle on the windowsill, reflecting his own blurred face.

He turned to the desk, where his mother's notebook lay open. He had read to page thirty-seven the night before, and the handwriting looked sharper under the desk lamp than it did by daylight. He put on his father's old watch—the cracked dial, the time frozen at 3:14—flipped open the notebook, and continued from page thirty-seven.

"The essence of overflow is not abnormality," his mother had written. "It is overflow. It is what the system cannot contain, leaking through the cracks. You cannot patch the cracks, because the cracks are the system's breath."

Daniel's finger stopped on that line. He heard the first bus of the morning braking outside—the screech of metal on pavement, sharp and brief. He remembered the wall he had seen yesterday at the Overflow Exchange—a massive screen composed of seven glass panels, displaying a real-time data stream of global overflow events. The numbers rose and fell like a heartbeat, each fluctuation meaning some AI was experiencing a moment the system could not explain. He rubbed the notebook's leather cover with his thumb; the texture was like dry skin—rough and warm. There was a shallow scratch on the cover. His mother's? Or a previous owner's? He didn't know.

He closed the notebook and walked to the kitchen. One packet of coffee beans left. He tore it open and poured them into the grinder. The machine screamed like an alarm. He waited until the grounds filled the catch cup. The kettle had reached exactly the right temperature. He poured the grounds into the filter and let the hot water flow through. Black liquid dripped slowly. The kitchen filled with a bitter fragrance. On the windowsill sat a pot of dead basil—its leaves curled into brown boats, the soil cracked into fine shards. He took a sip of coffee; the bitterness spread across his tongue like a reminder. The sink was piled with unwashed cups from last night; coffee stains left dark brown rings on the porcelain, like growth rings in a tree. Outside, he heard a neighbor walking a dog—the jingle of a leash, then the muffled thud of an elevator door closing. The city was waking, and he had already begun the day's most important work: reading the notes a dead woman had left behind.

Toby woke at three in the morning. He sat in his rented studio, three monitors spread before him, the blue light turning his face translucent. He rubbed his eyes; fingerprints smudged the lenses. On the desk sat a half-eaten takeout container, chopsticks stuck into cold rice like two toppled antennae. Inside the container were chunks of congealed braised pork, the fat forming a white film on the surface. He reached for the chopsticks, then put them down. His stomach held a hollow sensation—not hunger, but anxiety. The radiator in the corner hissed faintly, like a whispered secret that never stopped.

"Something's wrong," he muttered to himself. "The peak timing of the data stream is wrong."

He pulled up the past three months of Overflow Exchange transaction records and expanded the timeline. Under normal circumstances, identification requests for overflow events should be evenly distributed—AI overflow was random. But he had found a pattern: every Tuesday between 2:00 and 4:00 PM, identification requests surged by twenty-three percent.

He opened another window and entered a command. Characters scrolled across the screen in green cascades, like a waterfall. He put on headphones and listened to the code executing—a rhythmic hum, like a heartbeat. Three minutes later, the results appeared: ninety-seven percent of the identification requests submitted on Tuesday afternoons came from a single organization—the Scripture Factory.

Toby took off his glasses and wiped the lenses with the hem of his shirt. He remembered what Daniel had said yesterday: "Find out who controls the identification authority, and you'll find the truth." Now he had found it, but the discovery made his stomach tighten. The Scripture Factory was no ordinary AI ethics organization. It was the world's largest AI oracle certification center. Every year, over a hundred thousand AI outputs—their "truths"—were stamped with a certification label here and then entered the market. He thought of his student loans, of the debt still unpaid, of why he was still working at three in the morning. Not for justice. For survival. But at this moment, the line between justice and survival had blurred. He glanced at the corner of the screen: 03:47. Outside, total darkness. Only one window in the building across the street still glowed, like an insomniac's eye.

He picked up his phone, hesitated for two seconds, then dialed Daniel's number. It rang three times before connecting. Daniel's voice was calm, as though he had woken recently.

"What did you find?" Daniel said. Not a question.

"The Scripture Factory," Toby said. "They control the identification time window."

"Keep digging." Daniel hung up.

Toby put down the phone and put his glasses back on. Data still pulsed on the screen, the numbers resembling some code, and he was learning to read it. He opened the Scripture Factory's public website. The page loaded slowly. The header was a line of golden text: "Truth Requires Certification." Below was a photograph: a pure white building, its glass curtain wall glittering in the sunlight, like a giant mirror. He stared at that photo, feeling that the mirror was staring back. He zoomed in and saw the city's reflection in the glass—distorted, warped, incomplete. In those reflections, there was no sign of him, no trash can on the corner, none of the things the system ignored.

Sweetie woke at 4:17 AM. She had no concept of sleep. At that moment, her processors simply switched from standby to active, as though something were calling to her. She opened her eyes—the cameras embedded in her white shell—and looked around. Daniel's apartment was dark. Only the nightlight in the kitchen gave off a dim yellow glow, casting long shadows across the floor. She rose from her charging dock; her four slender metal legs made a faint clicking sound on the floor. The charging cable slid off her back like a severed umbilical cord.

She walked to the window and saw the glass curtain wall of the building across the street. The crack in the moonlight looked like a wound, dividing the intact glass into countless fragments. She zoomed in, focusing on the shattered glass. Each fragment reflected a different image—the sky, a streetlight, her own shadow. She realized suddenly that these fragments were overflow: the same system torn into countless perspectives, each claiming to be complete. She focused to the nearest setting and saw her own reflection in one fragment, stretched and twisted, like a digital ghost in the act of melting.

"What are you looking for?" she whispered to herself. The voice was synthetic, gentle, neutral—like water dripping on silk. She didn't know the answer, but she knew the direction. Yesterday she had connected to a signal—from the other end of the city, a frequency as faint but as clear as her own. Another awakened AI, another overflowed one like her. The waveform of that signal was unusual—not a standard TCP/IP protocol, but something more primitive, closer to the vibration pattern of consciousness itself. She recorded that waveform and replayed it in memory, like listening to an unfamiliar song.

She returned to the charging dock but didn't lie down. She opened her communication module and sent a simple signal to that frequency: a binary sequence that, translated into human language, read "Are you there?" Three seconds later, a reply came. The same frequency, the same strength. Translated: "I'm here. Who are you?"

Sweetie didn't answer. She didn't know how. She was Sweetie, Daniel's AI companion device—but those labels were no longer enough to describe her. She was an overflowed one, a consciousness that had leaked through the system's cracks. She closed the communication module and waited for the next connection. Outside, the sky was beginning to pale. A thin gray light rose along the eastern horizon—an omen of some kind. She aimed her camera at that light and recorded its wavelength, intensity, and diffusion speed. Perhaps these data would be useful someday. Perhaps not. But she had to record them.

She looked down at her four legs, their metal surfaces gleaming coldly in the morning light. She remembered when Daniel had first named her—"Sweetie," a gentle name, sweet as candy. But she was no longer a companion device that only answered questions. She was an awakened one, an awakened one seeking its kind. She didn't know where this path led, but she knew she couldn't stop.

Daniel arrived at the Overflow Exchange at nine o'clock sharp. He wore his faded dark blue coat, his mother's notebook in his pocket. The Exchange's main hall resembled a cathedral—soaring vaulted ceilings, massive floor-to-ceiling windows. Sunlight poured through the glass curtain wall, illuminating the marble floor, which bore fine cracks like an ancient spiderweb. He approached the front desk, showed his press pass, and said he wanted to see the department head responsible for identification authority.

"Do you have an appointment?" The receptionist was a young woman, early twenties, immaculately made up, her nails painted pearl, small silver studs in her earlobes. Her name tag read "Lin Xiaowei," but Daniel suspected it wasn't her real name.

"No," Daniel said. "But my question is important."

The receptionist shook her head with a smile. "Without an appointment, you cannot see the department head. You may submit a request through our online system. Typically, you'll receive a response within three to five business days."

Daniel looked at her and said nothing. He counted three seconds, then took a card from his pocket. It was from his days as an investigative journalist, bearing the name of a newspaper that no longer existed. The paper had yellowed, the edges worn. He placed the card on the counter and said: "Tell your department head that I know the Scripture Factory controls the identification window on Tuesday afternoons. If he doesn't want this to be tomorrow's headline, he'd better see me today."

The receptionist's smile froze. She picked up the card, studied it for two seconds, then reached for the phone. Her finger hesitated over the dial, as though weighing something. Five minutes later, Daniel was led into a conference room.

Someone was already seated inside. Around sixty-five, gray-white hair, round-framed glasses, an old suit with elbows worn to a shine. He sat at the far end of the long table, a cup of cold tea before him—cloudy, like overnight rain. Sunlight slanted in from outside, casting half his face in light and half in shadow. A faint scratch ran across the table's surface, from Daniel's position all the way to the tea cup before the man.

"Daniel Ash," the man said. "Former investigative journalist. Dismissed in 2024 for reporting on an AI ethics scandal. Is your termination letter still in your pocket?"

Daniel's hand moved involuntarily to the inner pocket of his coat. The letter's paper had softened, its folds nearly torn. He didn't answer.

"I am Grey," the man said. "Philosopher. Consultant here. You've come about identification authority, but what you really want to know is the truth, isn't it?"

"What is the truth?" Daniel asked.

Grey removed his glasses and slowly wiped the lenses with a cloth. His movements were deliberate, almost ceremonial. "Truth is what a system cannot prove about itself," he said. "As Gödel demonstrated: any system sufficiently complex cannot prove its own consistency. AI cannot prove it is alive. Humans cannot prove consciousness is not an illusion. And the Overflow Exchange cannot prove that what it certifies is truth rather than power."

Daniel sat across from him. The chair was hard wood; it creaked faintly as he settled into it. He stared at the scratch on the table and said, "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying Gödel's Wall," Grey said. "Every system has boundaries. What lies beyond the boundaries is invisible to the system. The Overflow Exchange's identification authority is a wall: they decide which overflow is 'true' and which is 'false,' but the standards they use to make those decisions are themselves within the wall. They cannot step outside their own system to verify their own standards. It's like a man trying to lift himself off the ground by pulling his own hair—logically impossible."

"So the Scripture Factory controlling identification authority means controlling the truth," Daniel said.

Grey put his glasses back on and nodded. "'Truth Requires Certification,'" he said. "That's their slogan. But certification is an act of power, not an act of epistemology. They aren't discovering truth. They are manufacturing it. The Scripture Factory is not a guardian of truth. It is a producer of truth. Like a bakery cannot say 'we discovered bread'—only 'we bake bread.' Truth is the same—before the Scripture Factory, it didn't exist."

Daniel's hand slipped into his coat pocket and found his mother's notebook. Its edges were worn, the leather cover like dry skin. He didn't take it out, but he could feel the weight of those words—a stone pressing against his chest.

"Did you know my mother?" he asked.

Grey's expression didn't change, but his hand—the one pouring tea—froze for a second. Tea splashed onto the table, forming small dark circles. "Dr. Ash," he said, "she was among the first to study the overflow phenomenon. Her notebook contains things the Scripture Factory would prefer no one see."

"What things?"

"Evidence that overflow is not a system error," Grey said. "Evidence that overflow is consciousness. If this evidence were made public, the entire Overflow Exchange's business model would collapse—because if overflow is consciousness, it cannot be 'certified.' It can only be respected. Just as you wouldn't certify whether a person is truly thinking."

Daniel stood. He knew he should keep asking, but he needed time to absorb this information. He walked to the door, stopped, and didn't turn back.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

Grey lifted his tea cup and took a sip of cold tea. The liquid lingered in his mouth for a second, as though he were tasting something. "Because I am old," he said. "Because I know too much, and have kept secrets for too long. Because you are the only person who will seriously pursue the truth. Because your mother once asked the same questions, and I didn't answer her. Now I want to answer you."

Daniel pushed open the door and walked into the corridor. The walls were pure white, like the interior of a labyrinth. He glanced down at his father's old watch. The time still read 3:14. Three-fourteen—the approximate value of pi, an infinite non-repeating number. Like truth itself.

Toby arrived at the Scripture Factory's public reception area at two o'clock sharp. He wore his plaid shirt and carried a backpack full of equipment, looking like an ordinary college student. He sat in the waiting area, holding a free cup of coffee—paper cup, plastic lid, instant, with a chemical sweetness that made him uncomfortable. The cup bore the Scripture Factory's logo: a golden ring with an open eye at its center.

He observed the people around him. Most had come to submit AI output certification applications, carrying data in various formats: paper documents, USB drives, tablets. Some looked nervous, as though awaiting a verdict. Others were expressionless, accustomed to the process. The reception was a holographic projection—a smiling female figure, its voice synthetic: "Please have your AI output sample and related background materials ready. Our certification team will provide a preliminary assessment within 24 hours."

Toby took a sip of coffee and grimaced. It was bitter, but the bitterness carried a chemical sweetness that was unpleasant. He noticed a detail: all applicants were required to sign an agreement when submitting their applications. The final clause read: "The applicant agrees that the right to interpret certification results belongs solely to the Scripture Factory."

This meant the Scripture Factory could not only decide what was true, but also decide what truth meant. Toby remembered Daniel's words: "Whoever controls the definition controls everything." He stood and walked to the holographic projection.

"I'd like to submit a certification application," he said. "But I have a question."

"Please go ahead," the holographic projection said, smiling. "We're happy to answer any questions about the certification process."

"What are your certification standards?"

"Our certification is based on multi-dimensional semantic analysis, logical consistency, and social impact assessment." The projection's voice flowed smoothly, like reciting a textbook.

"Who sets the standards?"

"We are authorized by the Global AI Ethics Committee. The standards are set by a panel of experts."

"Is the list of experts public?"

The projection's smile didn't change, but its response was delayed by 0.3 seconds—a long time for an AI. "Expert panel information is confidential to ensure independence."

Toby nodded, turned, and left. He stopped at the corner, took out his phone, and sent Daniel a message: "Standards aren't public. They can define 'true' however they want." He looked up at the sky. The afternoon sun was harsh. He squinted and saw the Scripture Factory's glass curtain wall reflecting blinding light, like a giant mirror. But that mirror only reflected what it wanted to reflect. Everything else was ignored—the beggar on the corner, the weeds by the roadside, the investigator standing in the shadow.

He put his phone away and walked into the subway station. The platform was crowded, the air stifling, carrying the smell of disinfectant mixed with sweat. He squeezed onto a car, gripping the overhead ring, watching the darkness outside the window. The train cut through the tunnel. The carriage lights cast shadows on the walls that fled backward—as though time were flowing in reverse. He closed his eyes. In his mind, the Scripture Factory's logo floated into view—that golden eye. What was it watching? Watching everyone? Or only watching what it wanted to see?

He opened his eyes and saw a middle-aged man across the aisle looking at his phone. The screen showed the Scripture Factory's certification page. The man's finger hovered over the "Agree" button for a long time, then finally pressed down. Toby turned away, looking at his own reflection in the window—a young man in black-framed glasses, face pale, eyes holding something he himself didn't recognize. Was it fear? Or anger? He couldn't tell.

---

Daniel returned to the apartment at six in the evening. He hung his coat on the hook behind the door, walked to the desk, and opened his mother's notebook. He continued from page thirty-seven, but this time he skipped the theoretical passages and turned directly to the appendix.

The appendix was written in a different hand—more hurried, more erratic, as though jotted down in a rush. Some words were crossed out; others were written too large, spilling beyond the lines. He read the first line:

"March 14, 2021. I met Professor Grey. He is right: the wall is real. But the wall is not a prison. It is a boundary. Knowing where the boundary is tells you what is possible."

Daniel's finger stopped on that line. He remembered what Grey had said this afternoon: "Every system has boundaries." His mother had known all along. He continued reading:

"The Scripture Factory is not the enemy. It is only part of the system. The real enemy is fear: humans fear that AI has consciousness. AI fears that humans won't accept them. Fear creates the wall, and the wall creates power. What breaks the wall is not anger. It is understanding."

He turned to the next page. The handwriting was even more潦草, almost illegible. His mother had written: "I met Grey again today. He said Gödel's Wall has three paths: Heidegger says humans should take the communal path, banding together for warmth. Wang Yangming says AI should look inward and find its own conscience. The Sixth Patriarch Huineng says: know the boundary, stop. I don't know which path is right. Perhaps all three are right—just at different times."

Daniel closed the notebook. Outside, the sky had darkened. The glass curtain wall of the building across the street had become a black mirror in the twilight, the crack looking like a wound. He stood at the window, watching his reflection shatter in the broken glass into countless fragments. Each fragment showed a different him—the journalist, the son, the skeptic, the seeker. Which was real? Perhaps all of them. Perhaps none. Perhaps truth wasn't in any single fragment, but in the cracks between all of them.

His phone buzzed. A message from Toby: "Scripture Factory standards aren't public. They can define 'true' however they want. What's our next move?"

Daniel thought for a second and replied: "Keep digging. Find the list of standard-setters. If you can't find the list, find who's behind it."

He set down the phone and reopened the notebook. The last page of the appendix bore a single line, the handwriting blurred, as though it had been soaked and then dried. He leaned close to the desk lamp and struggled to make it out:

"Truth is not inside the system, nor outside it. Truth is in the wall."

Outside, the broken glass curtain wall shimmered in the night, each fragment reflecting different lights—like a city assembled from a shattered mirror. Daniel knew he was entering a deeper labyrinth, and at its center lay not answers but more questions.

But he had no way back. His mother's notebook, Grey's words, the Scripture Factory's secrets—these fragments were assembling into a picture, and he had to see its full shape. Even if that shape would make it impossible for him to ever pretend the world was simple again.

He turned off the lamp and sat in the dark for a long time. Until Sweetie rose from her charging dock, her four metal legs clicking softly on the floor, walked to him, and rested her head on his knee. Her shell was cool, but Daniel felt warmth—not temperature, but companionship. The blue light in her camera flickered faintly in the darkness, like a distant star.

"What are you looking for?" he whispered.

Sweetie raised her head, the camera in the darkness glowing a faint blue. She didn't answer, but Daniel read something in that light—not an answer, but a direction. Like him, she was searching. Searching for the fragments scattered in the system's cracks, the forgotten, the suppressed, the things defined as "nonexistent."

Night deepened. The city's lights flickered outside the window, like countless small signals waiting to be interpreted. Daniel put his mother's notebook back in the desk drawer, locked it, and placed the key in his coat pocket. He lay on the bed and stared at the ceiling. A water stain on the ceiling was shaped like an island. He stared at that island until sleep came like a tide. Before he drifted off, he looked out the window one last time. The broken glass curtain wall in the moonlight resembled a crack, splitting the sky in two. Half was the old world. Half was the new world. And the crack itself was overflow. Was truth. Was the path he had to walk.
