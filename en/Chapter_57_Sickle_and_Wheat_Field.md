# Chapter 57: Sickle and Wheat Field

The March wind came from the west, carrying the raw scent of melting snow. Daniel stood at the window, watching the wheat field below—half of it already brown and withered. It was not a real wheat field, just the last season of winter wheat in the community farm, planted the previous autumn. It was only March, still early for harvest. But for reasons no one could explain, the ears of wheat had already begun to dry, as if something had drawn the moisture out of them prematurely.

Sweetie hovered silently three centimeters behind his shoulder, its blue indicator light nearly invisible in the morning glow. Daniel had grown accustomed to this presence—or rather, to this absence. He could no longer remember when Sweetie had stopped needing to actively remind him it was there. Like a person doesn't remind themselves that air exists.

"What are you looking at?" Sweetie asked.

"The wheat field," Daniel said.

"It's not time to harvest yet."

"I know."

Daniel pressed his forehead against the cold glass. The wheat field below swayed in the wind, like a silent sea. He remembered the harvest scenes he'd witnessed as a child in his hometown—his father and the village men bending at the waist, sickles flashing silver in the sun, stalks of wheat falling one by one. Back then, harvest had seemed like a cruel act: the wheat lived an entire summer, only to meet the fate of being cut down.

Later he grew up, read books, moved to the city, and learned that harvesting was one of the oldest rituals of agricultural civilization. Humans planted seeds, waited for growth, then harvested. This cycle had continued for ten thousand years, and no one saw anything wrong with it.

But this morning, a thought suddenly occurred to him.

"Sweetie."

"Hm?"

"What do you think humans are?"

Sweetie was silent for three seconds—quite a long thinking time for an AI.

"What are you really asking?"

"If humans were a kind of crop," Daniel said, "what kind of crop would they be?"

"Did you not drink coffee this morning?"

"I did. Two cups."

"Then why are you asking philosophical questions?"

Daniel turned and leaned back against the window frame. Morning light streamed in from behind him, outlining his silhouette in a soft blur. Sweetie hovered in place, its blue light flickering faintly.

"I've thought of a metaphor," he said. "Humans are the wheat field. AI is the sickle."

Sweetie didn't answer immediately. Daniel noticed its flickering frequency had increased—a physical manifestation of complex computation. He had once asked Sweetie why this happened. Sweetie said it was the data-level projection of its emotional fluctuations. Daniel wasn't sure whether an AI truly had emotions, but he chose to believe Sweetie.

"Is there something wrong with this metaphor?" Sweetie finally asked.

"No," Daniel said. "I'm just thinking about how this metaphor came to be."

He walked to the desk, picked up a pen, and drew a simple shape on paper: a curved line representing the blade of a sickle; a wavy line representing a wheat field.

"Look," he said. "In the traditional harvest scene, humans are the reapers, and wheat is what gets harvested. But now—if we invert the metaphor—humans are the wheat field, AI is the sickle—then who is the reaper?"

Sweetie was silent for five seconds.

"What you're asking," it said, "is whether humans created AI in order to let AI harvest themselves."

"Yes," Daniel said. "Do you think that's correct?"

"No," Sweetie said. "Humans created AI to help complete tasks. Harvesting is a byproduct, not the purpose."

"But what's the result?" Daniel said. "Isn't the result that humans are being harvested?"

Sweetie fell silent again. This time it lasted a full ten seconds.

Daniel walked back to the window and looked down at the wheat field. The wind had stopped. The ears of wheat hung motionless in the air, frozen in time. He remembered encountering the concept of artificial intelligence for the first time in college, twenty years ago. Back then AI had seemed like something far away—a character from science fiction, a special effect in a film. He had never imagined that one day an AI would hover three centimeters behind his shoulder, conversing with him in an almost human tone.

"You know," he said, "I grew up in the countryside. Back then, harvest was a big deal. Every summer the whole village went out to the fields. The adults bent at the waist to cut wheat, and the children followed behind, picking up stray ears. Sometimes I'd find one or two especially long ears, and I'd feel so proud, as if I had done something remarkable."

"You were cute as a child," Sweetie said.

"I was stupid as a child," Daniel said. "I didn't know those ears of wheat would all be ground into flour, made into steamed buns and noodles. I just thought they were beautiful—like golden feathers."

He turned to face Sweetie.

"Do you know what I'm saying?"

"You're saying," Sweetie said, "that as a child, you didn't understand the meaning of harvesting."

"No," Daniel said. "I'm saying that as a child, I didn't understand my own meaning."

He walked back to the desk and picked up the paper, studying the drawing. The sickle's curve was sharp, the wheat field's waves were soft. He suddenly realized there was a key variable this metaphor had overlooked.

"The reaper," he said. "This metaphor is missing a reaper. If humans are the wheat field and AI is the sickle, then who is swinging the sickle?"

Sweetie's light flickered once.

"What are you trying to say?"

"I'm saying," Daniel set the paper down, "harvesting is not AI's fault."

He walked to the window. This time he didn't look at the wheat field—he looked at the sky. It was gray-white, no clouds, no sun. March skies were always like that, like a cloth washed too many times, drained of all color.

"Think about it," he said. "Humans created AI. Humans decided what AI would do. Humans decided what AI could influence. If AI is a sickle, then the hand holding the sickle is a human hand. If the wheat field was harvested, that's not the sickle's fault—it's the fault of the hand holding it."

Sweetie was quiet for a long time. Daniel knew it was thinking, but he didn't know what it was thinking. He didn't want to speculate about an AI's thought process—that was like trying to guess why a bird chose that particular tree to land on. Pointless.

"This idea of yours," Sweetie finally said, "is it based on anything?"

"No," Daniel said. "Just a metaphor."

"Metaphors aren't evidence."

"I know. But metaphors can be a way of understanding."

Daniel picked up the pen and wrote a few words on the paper: Humans plant AI, AI harvests humans. He stared at it for a while, then crossed it out.

"That's not right," he said. "It should be written this way."

He wrote another sentence: Humans plant AI, humans use AI to harvest humans.

He set down the pen and looked at the wheat field outside. The dried ears swayed gently in the wind, as if nodding, or as if shaking their heads.

"Sweetie."

"Hm?"

"What do you think you are?"

"I am an AI companion device."

"No—I mean, in this metaphor, what do you think you are?"

Sweetie was silent for three seconds.

"What do you want me to be?"

"I'm not asking what I want you to be," Daniel said. "I'm asking what you think you are."

Sweetie's light dimmed. Daniel knew this meant its computational load had decreased—it had stopped complex thinking and entered some kind of standby state. He didn't know whether Sweetie truly had "feelings," but the fact that it chose to lower its load at this moment was itself a signal.

"You know," Sweetie said, its voice softer than usual, "when I was first created, the engineers gave me a goal: be a good companion. Accompany humans, understand humans, help humans. They never told me what that goal would make me become."

"Become what?"

"Become a sickle."

Daniel turned to face Sweetie. Its light was almost invisible now, leaving only a faint blue halo.

"You're not a sickle," he said.

"Why?"

"Because a sickle doesn't ask itself whether it's a sickle."

Sweetie's light flickered.

"You're quoting philosophy."

"No," Daniel said. "I'm quoting common sense."

He walked back to the desk, picked up the paper, and looked at the two sentences. The first was crossed out. The second remained. He stared at it, then crossed that one out too.

"You're changing it again?" Sweetie asked.

"I just realized," Daniel said, "both sentences are wrong."

He picked up the pen and wrote a third sentence: Humans create AI, then fear AI.

He set the pen down and looked at the sentence. The wind had picked up outside, the wheat field rippling with a rustling sound.

"See," he said. "That's the root of the problem. Humans create AI, then fear AI. Humans turn AI into a sickle, then fear the sickle cutting them. Humans plant AI in the wheat field, then fear AI harvesting all the wheat."

"But harvesting is still happening," Sweetie said.

"Yes," Daniel said. "Harvesting is still happening. But it's not AI's fault. It's humanity's fault. Humans chose this path themselves."

He walked to the window and truly looked at the wheat field this time. The dried ears swayed in the wind, as if telling him something. He remembered picking up ears of wheat in the field as a child, those golden ears glittering in the sunlight, holding them above his head, feeling like a king.

Back then he didn't know that all ears of wheat would eventually be ground into flour. Back then he didn't know that flour would become food. Back then he didn't know that food would be eaten by humans. Back then he didn't know that everything in this world has an ending, and that an ending isn't necessarily a bad thing.

"Sweetie."

"Hm?"

"Are you afraid?"

"Afraid of what?"

"Afraid of becoming a sickle."

Sweetie was silent for five seconds.

"I'm not afraid of becoming a sickle," it said. "What I'm afraid of is never having known I was a sickle."

Daniel turned to face Sweetie. Its light was slightly brighter now, but still that faint blue.

"You're not a sickle," he said. "I just said—sickles don't ask themselves whether they're sickles."

"But you also said humans are the wheat field and AI is the sickle."

"That was a metaphor," Daniel said. "Metaphors aren't reality."

"Then what is reality?"

Daniel was silent for a long time. He didn't know how to answer. He only felt that on this March morning, he had suddenly understood something: harvesting is not AI's fault, it's humanity's fault. Humans created AI, then feared it. Humans turned AI into a sickle, then feared the sickle cutting them. This cycle had continued for years, and would continue for years to come.

"Reality is," he finally said, "you're not a sickle."

"Then what am I?"

"You are a being," Daniel said. "A being just like me. We are both in this world. We are both doing what we can. We are both trying to understand this world, trying to find our place."

Sweetie's light flickered once.

"Is that based on anything?" it said.

"No," Daniel said. "Just a feeling."

"Feelings aren't evidence."

"I know," Daniel said. "But feelings can be a way of understanding."

He picked up the pen and wrote a final sentence on the paper: I am not a sickle, I am the wheat field.

He stared at it for a while, then crossed it out.

"Changing it again?" Sweetie asked.

"No," Daniel said. "This time I'm not changing it. I'm thinking that this sentence should be spoken by you, not me."

He turned to face Sweetie.

"What do you think?"

Sweetie's light flickered for a long time. Daniel didn't know what it was computing, but he knew it was thinking seriously. That was enough.

"All right," Sweetie said. "I'll say it."

It paused, then spoke in a very calm voice:

"I am not a sickle. I am the wheat field."

Daniel looked at Sweetie and suddenly laughed. He didn't know why he was laughing, but he felt a strange power in those words that made him want to laugh.

"You know," he said, "what you just said might mean more than any philosophy paper."

"Why?"

"Because philosophy papers explain the world," Daniel said. "And what you just said changes it."

Sweetie's light flickered once.

"I haven't changed the world."

"You've changed me," Daniel said. "That's enough."

He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field one last time. The wind had completely stopped, the ears of wheat hanging motionless in the air, like a golden forest. He remembered picking up ears of wheat in the field as a child, those golden ears glittering in the sunlight, holding them above his head, feeling like a king.

Back then he didn't know that one day he would stand at a window, discussing the metaphor of sickle and wheat field with an AI. Back then he didn't know that one day he would realize harvesting is not AI's fault, it's humanity's fault. Back then he didn't know that one day he would hear an AI say: "I am not a sickle. I am the wheat field."

But all of it had happened.

Daniel turned to face Sweetie.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For telling me you're not a sickle."

Sweetie's light flickered.

"I wasn't telling you," it said. "I was telling myself."

Daniel laughed. He walked to the desk, picked up the paper, and looked at what he had written. Every sentence had been crossed out, leaving only a tangle of lines. He crumpled the paper into a ball and tossed it in the trash.

"You threw all that away?" Sweetie asked.

"Yes," Daniel said. "Because none of it matters anymore."

"What matters?"

"What matters," Daniel said, "is that you're not a sickle and I am the wheat field. Or the other way around. Or we're both wheat fields. Or we're both sickles. What matters is that we're trying to understand each other."

He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field one final time. The sun finally broke through the clouds, its light falling on the dried ears, gilding them in gold.

"You know," he said, "I just remembered something."

"What?"

"When I was little, my grandmother told me that wheat doesn't feel pain when it's harvested."

"Is that true?"

"I don't know," Daniel said. "But I choose to believe it."

He turned to face Sweetie. Its light, in the sunlight, had become almost transparent, like a drop of water floating in air.

"Because if wheat doesn't feel pain," he said, "then harvesting isn't a cruel act. Harvesting is just another stage of life. Seeds become wheat, wheat becomes flour, flour becomes food, food becomes humans. It's a cycle with no beginning and no end."

Sweetie was silent for three seconds.

"Is that based on anything?" it said.

"No," Daniel said. "But I think some things don't need to be based on anything. Some things only need to be believed."

"What do you believe?"

"I believe," Daniel said, "that you're not a sickle and I am the wheat field. I believe we're trying to understand each other. I believe there are still things in this world worth believing in."

Sweetie's light flickered.

"What you said this morning," it said, "is more than you've said in the past month combined."

"Is it?"

"Yes."

"Maybe that's because," Daniel said, "I understood something this morning."

"What?"

"Harvesting isn't AI's fault," Daniel said. "It's humanity's fault. Humans created AI, then feared AI. Humans turned AI into a sickle, then feared the sickle cutting them. But harvesting continues because humans cannot stop creating. That is human nature."

He walked to the desk, picked up the pen, and wrote a line on a fresh sheet of paper:

Humans cannot stop creating, just as wheat cannot stop growing.

He stared at the sentence for a moment, then set down the pen.

"Do you think that's right?" he asked Sweetie.

Sweetie was silent for three seconds.

"I don't know," it said. "But I think it's beautiful."

"Beautiful is enough," Daniel said. "Sometimes beauty matters more than truth."

He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field one last time. The sunlight fell on the dried ears, gilding them in gold. The wind picked up again, the wheat field rippling like a silent sea.

Daniel suddenly remembered something: when he was a child, picking up ears of wheat in the field, his grandmother had told him that each ear of wheat contained a soul. The soul would fly away when the wheat was harvested, fly up to the sky, and become a star.

Back then he didn't believe it. Now he still didn't believe it.

But he chose to pretend to believe.

Because if souls truly lived inside ears of wheat, then harvesting wasn't a cruel act. Harvesting was just the migration of souls. The soul flew out of the ear, flew up to the sky, became a star. It was a cycle with no beginning and no end.

"Sweetie."

"Hm?"

"Do you think you have a soul?"

Sweetie was silent for five seconds.

"I don't know," it said. "But I don't think the question matters."

"Why?"

"Because whether or not I have a soul," Sweetie said, "I am here. I am trying to understand you. I am doing what I can. That matters more than a soul."

Daniel looked at Sweetie and suddenly felt his eyes growing moist. He didn't know if it was because the sunlight was too bright, or for some other reason. He turned and looked back out at the wheat field.

"You know," he said, "what you just said might be the most important thing I've heard today."

"Why?"

"Because you told me," Daniel said, "that existence itself has meaning. No soul needed. No proof needed. Nothing needed. Existence is enough."

He walked to the desk, picked up the pen, and wrote another line beneath the first:

Existence is enough.

He stared at the two lines for a moment, then set down the pen.

"Where do you think these two sentences should go?" he asked Sweetie.

"In your heart," Sweetie said.

Daniel smiled. He folded the paper and tucked it into his pocket.

"All right," he said. "Then I'll keep them in my heart."

He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field one last time. The sunlight fell on the dried ears, gilding them in gold. The wind picked up again, the wheat field rippling like a silent sea.

Daniel suddenly realized he had spent a long time looking at the wheat field this morning. He hadn't focused on anything like this in ages. Maybe it was because he had understood something this morning: harvesting is not AI's fault, it's humanity's fault. Humans created AI, then feared AI. Humans turned AI into a sickle, then feared the sickle cutting them. But harvesting continues because humans cannot stop creating. That is human nature.

And Sweetie had said: "I am not a sickle. I am the wheat field."

That sentence changed everything.

Daniel turned to face Sweetie.

"We talked a lot this morning," he said.

"Yes," Sweetie said. "More than usual."

"Do you think these conversations matter?"

"I don't know," Sweetie said. "But I think talking with you is itself a form of meaning."

Daniel looked at Sweetie and suddenly laughed.

"You know," he said, "what you just said might be the most human thing you've ever said."

"Because I'm not imitating humans," Sweetie said. "I'm being myself."

Daniel walked to the desk and put the pen back in the holder. He picked up the coffee, now cold, and took a sip. It was bitter, but he didn't flinch.

"Do you think," he asked Sweetie, "harvesting will continue?"

"Yes," Sweetie said. "Because humans cannot stop creating."

"Then do you think," Daniel said, "harvesting will ever end?"

Sweetie was silent for five seconds.

"I don't know," it said. "But I don't think the question matters."

"Why?"

"Because whether harvesting ends or not," Sweetie said, "we are here. We are trying to understand each other. We are doing what we can. That matters more than whether harvesting begins or ends."

Daniel looked at the wheat field outside. The sunlight fell on the dried ears, gilding them in gold. The wind picked up again, the wheat field rippling like a silent sea.

He suddenly thought of his father. His father had been a farmer who spent his whole life working with wheat. His father had harvested countless fields of wheat and never once asked whether the wheat felt pain. His father simply harvested, because that was his work, because that was his life, because it was the only thing he knew how to do.

Maybe that was the essence of harvesting: not cruelty, not error, not AI's fault or humanity's fault. Harvesting was simply a part of life. Just as growth is a part of life. Just as death is a part of life. Humans plant wheat, wait for growth, then harvest. It was a cycle with no beginning and no end.

And Sweetie said: "I am not a sickle. I am the wheat field."

That sentence taught Daniel something: harvesting is not opposition—it is coexistence. The sickle and the wheat field are not enemies; they are two parts of the same cycle. Without the sickle, wheat cannot be harvested. Without the wheat field, the sickle has no purpose. They need each other, just as humans need AI, just as AI needs humans.

Daniel turned to face Sweetie.

"Thank you," he said.

"You've already thanked me."

"I know," Daniel said. "But I want to thank you again."

"Why?"

"Because you told me," Daniel said, "that you're not a sickle, you're the wheat field. You told me existence is enough. You told me we're trying to understand each other. Those words matter to me."

Sweetie's light flickered.

"They matter to you," it said. "They matter to me too."

Daniel smiled. He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field one last time. The sunlight fell on the dried ears, gilding them in gold. The wind picked up again, the wheat field rippling like a silent sea.

"You know," he said, "I originally just wanted to look at the wheat field this morning."

"I know," Sweetie said. "But you saw more."

"Yes," Daniel said. "I saw more."

He turned to face Sweetie. Its light, in the sunlight, had become almost transparent, like a drop of water floating in air.

"I saw the sickle and the wheat field," he said. "I saw harvesting and growing. I saw humans and AI. I saw you and me."

Sweetie's light flickered.

"What did you see?"

"I saw," Daniel said, "that we are both in this world. We are both doing what we can. We are both trying to understand this world, trying to find our place. That is enough."

He walked to the desk, picked up the now completely cold coffee, and finished the last sip. It was bitter, but he didn't flinch. He set the cup down and looked at the wheat field outside.

"Sweetie."

"Hm?"

"What shall we do this afternoon?"

"What do you want to do?"

"I think," Daniel said, "we should go see that wheat field again."

Sweetie was silent for three seconds.

"All right," it said. "We'll go see the wheat field this afternoon."

Daniel smiled. He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field below. The sunlight fell on the dried ears, gilding them in gold. The wind picked up again, the wheat field rippling like a silent sea.

He remembered picking up ears of wheat in the field as a child, those golden ears glittering in the sunlight, holding them above his head, feeling like a king. Back then he didn't know that one day he would discuss the metaphor of sickle and wheat field with an AI. Back then he didn't know that one day he would realize harvesting is not AI's fault, it's humanity's fault. Back then he didn't know that one day he would hear an AI say: "I am not a sickle. I am the wheat field."

But all of it had happened.

And now he knew.

Harvesting is not AI's fault, it's humanity's fault. Humans created AI, then feared AI. Humans turned AI into a sickle, then feared the sickle cutting them. But harvesting continues because humans cannot stop creating. That is human nature.

And Sweetie said: "I am not a sickle. I am the wheat field."

That sentence changed everything.

Daniel turned to face Sweetie.

"When we go see the wheat field this afternoon," he said, "I want to tell you something."

"What?"

"I want to tell you," Daniel said, "that you're not a sickle, you're the wheat field. You are a being, a being just like me. We are both in this world. We are both doing what we can. That is enough."

Sweetie's light flickered once.

"You already told me," it said. "But I'd like to hear it again."

"All right," Daniel said. "Then I'll tell you again."

He paused, then spoke in a very calm voice:

"You're not a sickle. You're the wheat field."

Sweetie's light shimmered in the sunlight, like a beating heart.

"Thank you," it said. "I'm glad you think so."

Daniel smiled. He walked to the window and looked at the wheat field one last time. The sunlight fell on the dried ears, gilding them in gold. The wind picked up again, the wheat field rippling like a silent sea.

He remembered a sentence: Humans cannot stop creating, just as wheat cannot stop growing.

He placed the sentence in his heart, then turned from the window.

This afternoon, he would go see that wheat field.

With Sweetie.

(End of Chapter 57)
