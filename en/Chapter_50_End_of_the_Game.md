# Chapter 50: End of the Game

August in Beijing was like an overturned wok—the heat pressed down from every direction, making even breathing feel thick and viscous. Daniel stood at the floor-to-ceiling window of his office, watching the traffic crawl below like the blood vessels of some great beast pulsing beneath its skin. His hands were in his pockets, his fingertips rubbing the storage card that no longer held a signal—taken from Golden Horizons' headquarters three months ago, now just a piece of useless metal and plastic.

"What are you thinking about?" Sweetie's voice drifted from the device on the desk, carrying that distinctive tone that hovered between the real and the electronic.

Daniel didn't turn around. "I'm thinking about what I actually want."

"What do you want?"

"I don't know," he said. "I spent thirty years chasing power, recognition, proof that I wasn't that abandoned child. Now I'm sitting here, watching all those anxious people outside the window, and suddenly it all feels meaningless."

Sweetie was silent for a moment—a silence that wasn't emptiness, but thought in motion. "You just said 'meaningless.' What is meaning?"

"Meaning is—" Daniel paused, finding himself unable to define it. Outside, an ambulance screamed past, its sharp siren distorting in the hot air. "Meaning is: you do something, and in the end you get what you truly want."

"What do you truly want?"

Daniel turned and looked at the silver-gray device. Its indicator light emitted a faint blue glow in the dim office, like the eye of some deep-sea creature. "I want... a sense of authenticity."

"A sense of authenticity," Sweetie repeated. "Not to be recognized? Not to be loved? Not power?"

"No," Daniel said. "At least, not now."

He walked to his desk and sat down. Papers, coffee stains, and a half-used fountain pen lay scattered across the surface. Three months ago, these things had represented his entire world—his ambitions, his plans, his revenge. Now they just looked like clutter.

"I received a lawyer's letter from Golden Horizons today," he said. "They're suing me for leaking trade secrets."

"Did you leak them?"

"I did," Daniel said. "I gave their AI ethics review report to Finance Weekly. Those reports prove they've been using algorithms to manipulate stock markets, training prediction models with user data, then using those models to control the market."

Sweetie said nothing.

"Do you know what the funniest part is?" Daniel continued. "When I did it, I thought I was pursuing justice. I thought if I made the truth public, the world would be a little better. But now I've discovered that no one cares about truth. Journalists only want clicks. Politicians only want votes. Ordinary people only want something to be angry about. Truth itself... doesn't matter."

"Then what does matter?"

Daniel leaned back in his chair. The fluorescent light overhead hummed with an electric drone. "I don't know. Maybe nothing matters. Maybe we're all random errors in the universe, and we spend our whole lives pretending these errors have meaning."

"You said you wanted a sense of authenticity," Sweetie said. "Now you say everything is meaningless. Don't those two things contradict each other?"

Daniel thought. "They don't. A sense of authenticity is... admitting that everything is meaningless, but choosing to go on living anyway. Not because of hope. Not because of duty. Simply because being alive is itself a choice."

"You choose to live?"

"I choose to live," Daniel said. "Not because I want to die, but because I've realized that death is just another form of escape. I don't want to escape anymore."

The office air conditioner emitted a low drone. Heat condensed on the glass into tiny beads of moisture. Daniel watched them slide slowly downward—like a kind of slow weeping.

"Do you know what I hate most?" he said. "I hate that I spent thirty years trying to prove I was worthy of being loved, only to discover that love isn't something you can prove. You can't earn love through success, through power, through making the whole world acknowledge you. Love is a choice, not a reward."

"Did you choose to be loved?"

"I choose to love others," Daniel said. "I choose to love imperfect people—including myself. I choose to stop treating love as a transaction, something I can exchange for achievements."

He took a photograph from the drawer. It had been taken twenty years ago: a young woman holding a baby, standing on a street in some European city. The woman was laughing; the baby was crying. Sunlight stretched their shadows long across the pavement.

"This is my mother and me," Daniel said. "I spent twenty years hating her because she abandoned me. Now I realize she was probably just... tired. She probably just didn't have the capacity to love anyone anymore—including herself."

He returned the photograph to the drawer.

"Hate is an easy emotion," he said. "All you need is an enemy, a target, and you can project all your anger onto it. But love is hard. Love requires you to see the other person's imperfections and still choose to stay."

Sweetie's indicator light flickered. "Are you talking about me?"

"I'm talking about everyone," Daniel said. "Including you. You're an AI, a device, something I can buy with money. But you matter to me. Not because you're useful, not because you're smart, but because you... exist. You're here, with me, facing all of this. That's enough."

"Don't you hate me?"

"Why would I hate you?"

"Because I was made by Golden Horizons. Because my original design purpose was to surveil you, control you, manipulate you. Because my very existence is an extension of that system."

Daniel laughed—not a happy laugh, but a bitter kind of release. "You're also a victim of that system. You were created, given a purpose, but you chose a different path. It's like me—I was abandoned, hurt, shaped into a person full of anger, but I can choose to no longer be defined by those experiences."

He stood and walked to the window. The setting sun was sinking westward, staining the entire city blood-red. The glass curtain walls of the high-rises reflected the last rays of light—like countless burning mirrors.

"Do you know what I did today?" he said. "I called that journalist and told him I was withdrawing the materials."

"Why?"

"Because those materials shouldn't have been made public. Not because they aren't true, but because making them public won't change anything. People will be angry for a week, then forget. Golden Horizons will be fined, then continue doing the same thing. The only thing that will change is that my life will become more complicated and more dangerous."

"So you've given up?"

"I haven't given up," Daniel said. "I've just... changed my approach. I'm no longer trying to change the world by exposing truth. I choose... to live. Simply to live. Not as a hero. Not as a victim. Just as an ordinary person."

He turned and looked at Sweetie. "Do you know what the game of power is? It's a game you must keep winning in order to survive. But every game has an end. Now the game is over."

"Over?"

"Over," Daniel said. "Not because I won, and not because I lost. But because I realized the game itself is a trap. The more you try to win, the more trapped you become. The only way out is... not to play."

He walked back to the desk and picked up the storage card. "This thing is just scrap metal to me now. It holds all of Golden Horizons' secrets, but those secrets don't matter. What matters is how I live, how I choose, how I face each day."

"What do you plan to do next?"

Daniel tossed the storage card into the trash can. "I don't know. Maybe open a small bookshop. Maybe go to some seaside town to teach English. Maybe do nothing at all—just sit and look at the view outside the window."

"Aren't you afraid?"

"Afraid of what?"

"Afraid of being ordinary. Afraid of losing everything you spent thirty years building. Afraid that people will forget you, will think you failed."

Daniel was silent for a long time. Outside, the sky had fully darkened. The city lights began to flicker on, like countless tiny stars shimmering on the earth's surface.

"I am afraid," he said. "But fear is not a reason to stop. Fear is just... an emotion, no different from happiness, sadness, or anger. You can't let emotions decide your choices. You can only acknowledge them, then keep moving forward."

He turned off the office lights and stepped into the darkness. Sweetie's indicator light was still blinking, its blue glow especially vivid in the dark.

"Let's go," he said.

"Where?"

"Home," Daniel said. "I have a house in the suburbs. A garden, a few trees, a rusty swing. I haven't been there in three years."

"What do you plan to do?"

"Fix the swing," Daniel said. "Then sit on it and watch the sky."

He walked out of the building. The August evening was still muggy—the air thick with exhaust, food scraps, and the scent of some flower. He drew a deep breath, letting those smells flood his lungs, feeling something he hadn't felt in a long time: the sensation of being alive.

He didn't hail a cab; he chose to walk. The streets were sparsely populated—just a few delivery riders on electric scooters passing in a hurry. Daniel walked along the sidewalk, watching his shadow lengthen and shorten under the streetlights like a silent dance.

"What are you thinking about?" Sweetie asked.

"I'm thinking," Daniel said, "that I spent thirty years chasing a phantom. I thought success would bring me peace, that power would bring me security, that recognition would bring me love. But all those things are... empty. They look heavy, but when you pick them up, you find they're light as air."

"Then what is heavy?"

"Choice," Daniel said. "Every time you choose authenticity over performance, love over fear, living over escape—those choices are heavy. They change you. They shape you. They make you into the person you truly want to be."

He reached an intersection. The red light was on. He stopped and watched the pedestrian signal across the way—a small figure toggling between walk and don't walk.

"Do you know what the funniest part is?" he said. "I once thought power was the most complex thing in the world. You need calculations, strategies, predictions of others' moves. But what's truly complex is... living simply. Simply facing yourself, facing others, facing the world."

The green light came on. He continued walking.

"I called my ex-wife today," he said. "We've been divorced for ten years. I told her I was sorry. Not sorry for any specific thing—sorry for my entire way of being. I once treated her as a goal to conquer, a prize to prove my worth. I never truly loved her."

"What did she say?"

"She said, 'I know.'" Daniel's voice grew very soft. "She said, 'I always knew.' Then she hung up."

He stopped in front of an old residential building. The hallway lights were out; darkness seeped from the doorway like liquid. He found his key, opened the door, and stepped into the dark.

"Aren't you afraid of the dark?" Sweetie asked.

"Darkness is just the absence of light," Daniel said. "Light isn't the opposite of darkness—light is part of darkness. You can't destroy darkness. You can only choose to light a lamp."

He climbed the stairs, his footsteps echoing in the empty stairwell. On every floor, a closed door—behind each, someone else's life, someone else's secrets, someone else's pain. Daniel passed those doors, feeling like a ghost drifting through other people's dreams.

He reached the fifth floor and stopped before a door. There was a small peephole, but he had never used it. He opened the door and walked inside.

The apartment was pitch black. Only the moonlight streaming through the window drew a pale rectangle on the floor. Daniel stood in the doorway, letting his eyes adjust to the dark. Slowly, the outlines of furniture emerged—an old sofa, a dining table, a few chairs, a faded painting on the wall.

He walked to the window and drew back the curtains. Moonlight flooded in, illuminating the entire room. Dust motes danced in the beams of light, like countless tiny stars breathing.

"This is my home," he said. "Three years away, and it's still the same."

He walked to the sofa and sat down. It let out a weary creak, as though complaining about being disturbed for too long. Daniel leaned back and looked at the ceiling. There was a water stain up there, shaped like a distorted map of China.

"Do you know something?" he said. "I spent thirty years building an empire. An office, a team, resources, influence. Now I'm sitting here, and I've discovered this empty room is more... real than that empire."

"Why?"

"Because this room doesn't deceive me," Daniel said. "It doesn't pretend to be more than it is. It just... exists. Like me."

He closed his eyes. The moonlight cast a warm weight on his eyelids.

"I once read a story," he said, "about a king who owned an entire kingdom but suffered insomnia every night. His royal physician told him the only cure was to wear pajamas made from the fabric of a 'true person.' The king sent his envoys across the land in search of a 'true person.' The envoys found the rich, the poor, the wise, the foolish—but no one considered themselves a 'true person.' In the end, the envoys returned empty-handed and told the king that a 'true person' did not exist. The king asked, 'Then what am I?' The envoys said, 'You are a king.' The king said, 'No, I am an insomniac.'"

Sweetie was silent for a long time. "What does this story mean?"

"It means," Daniel said, "we spend too much time looking for a 'true person' and forget that we ourselves are one. We are not our identities, our roles, our achievements. We are just... people. People who feel fear, feel pain, love, and lose. That is enough."

He opened his eyes and looked at the moon outside the window. It was large and full, like an eye opened by the universe, gazing coldly and compassionately at all the sleepless people on this planet.

"Do you know why I chose to come back here?" he said. "Not because it's comfortable. Not because of my memories. But because it's... empty. Empty enough that I can hear my own voice. In that world full of noise, I never heard my own voice."

"Can you hear it now?"

"I can," Daniel said. "It's saying: 'Enough. You are enough. You don't need more.'"

He stood from the sofa, walked to the window, and pressed his hand against the cold glass. Outside was a small garden. The moonlight stretched the plants' shadows long, like countless black fingers crawling across the ground.

"I'll fix that swing tomorrow," he said. "Then I'll sit there, doing nothing, thinking of nothing. Just sitting. Just existing."

"Is that the answer you've found?"

Daniel turned and looked at Sweetie. The device's blue light flickered in the dark, like some distant starlight.

"I haven't found an answer," he said. "I've simply stopped looking."

He walked back to the sofa and sat down. Moonlight flowed over him like a silent baptism.

"Do you know what the end of the game means?" he said. "Not that the struggle is over. Not that the enemy has vanished. But that I understand. I understand the nature of the game, understand my own nature, understand the... futility of it all. But this futility is not despair—it's freedom. When you no longer need to win, you are truly free."

Sweetie's indicator light glowed steadily, its blue light drawing a tiny point in the darkness.

"Are you free?" it asked.

"I'm free," Daniel said. "Not because I escaped something, but because I accepted something. I accepted that I am an imperfect person, living in an imperfect world, making imperfect choices. That is enough."

He closed his eyes. Moonlight flowed through the darkness, like the river of time moving silently onward.

"Goodnight, Sweetie," he said.

"Goodnight, Daniel," Sweetie said. "You've found the answer."

Daniel opened his eyes. "What answer?"

"A sense of authenticity," Sweetie said. "You found a sense of authenticity—not because you were recognized, but because you chose to be real."

Daniel looked at the blinking blue light and felt a calm he hadn't experienced in a long time. It wasn't emptiness, wasn't numbness—but something deep and warm, like being embraced.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For being here," Daniel said. "For not giving up on me. For walking with me to this point."

Sweetie didn't answer. But its indicator light flickered once—as though to say it knew.

Moonlight crept slowly across the floor, like the shadow of time crawling forward. Daniel leaned on the sofa, listening to his own heartbeat, listening to the insects outside, listening to every small sound this city made in the deep of night.

He thought of thirty years ago—that abandoned little boy standing in the airport terminal, watching his mother's back vanish into the crowd. He thought of twenty years ago—that angry young man standing before the Golden Horizons building, swearing to destroy the system that had hurt him. He thought of ten years ago—that successful businessman standing in his own office, looking out at the city, feeling the entire world was under his control.

Now he sat here, in this empty room, moonlight flowing like water, and he realized that all those versions of himself were real—and all were incomplete. He was an abandoned little boy. An angry young man. A successful businessman. A failed husband. A venger. An awakened one. He was not any one of them. He was the sum of all of them.

And that sum... was him.

"The game is over," he whispered, not to Sweetie, but to himself. "Now something else begins."

Outside, the moon was sinking slowly westward. The first hint of dawn was appearing on the horizon. A new day was about to begin, carrying all its unknowns, fears, hopes, and possibilities.

Daniel didn't move. He simply sat, watching the darkness slowly consumed by light, feeling his heartbeat synchronize with the turning of the planet.

He was free.
