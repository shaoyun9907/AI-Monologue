# Chapter 48: The Reckoning

The conference room's air conditioning had been broken for three days.

June sunlight streamed through the floor-to-ceiling windows, casting the same color on everyone's faces—the color of skin that has lingered too long in a morgue. Daniel sat in the corner, watching the people around the round table. Their shadows fell on the walls, trembling with each slight movement of their bodies, like a collective spasm.

Hargrove sat at the opposite end. He wore a navy suit today, his tie knotted so tightly it seemed someone was slowly strangling him with it. His hands rested on the table, fingers interlaced, joints white from the pressure. Daniel noticed his right index finger trembling slightly—so faint it was almost invisible, but once you noticed, you couldn't look away.

"We all know what happened," the person in the seat of honor spoke. Daniel didn't recognize him, but the nameplate on his chest read "Chief Compliance Officer." His voice was calm, as though reciting a weather report. "There has been a leak of internal company information. Certain data that should not have entered the public domain is now in the public domain."

No one spoke. The air conditioner emitted a low hum, like the carcass of some insect vibrating inside the machine.

"We need to find the leaker," the compliance officer continued. "This is to protect the company, and also to protect each of you in this room."

Daniel's gaze drifted around the room. He counted: fourteen people in total. Besides the compliance officer, there were twelve executives and himself. He didn't know why he was here—three days ago he had received an email notifying him to attend this meeting as a "relevant party." Relevant party. The phrase had circled in his mind for a long time, like a fly searching for an exit.

Halvorsen sat to Hargrove's right. He looked ten years older than the last time Daniel saw him, though Daniel knew that had only been a few days ago. His hair was already gray, but now even the gray seemed to have faded, leaving only a pallor approaching transparency. His eyes fixed on some nonexistent point on the table, his lips moving faintly, as though silently reciting something.

"According to our preliminary investigation," the compliance officer opened the folder before him, "the time window for the information leak is April to May 2029. The leaked content involves core data from Project Sweetie, including user agreements, financial models, and—"

He paused and glanced up at Hargrove.

"—internal memos."

The word hung in the air for several seconds. Daniel saw Hargrove's shoulders tighten slightly, as though someone had pressed down lightly on his back. The room's temperature seemed to climb several more degrees, even though the air conditioner continued its dying hum.

"Everyone in this room," the compliance officer closed the folder, "has had access to these documents. This means, theoretically, any one of you could be the leaker."

Daniel felt someone looking at him. He turned and found it was a woman across the table—around forty, wearing rimless glasses, the lenses reflecting eyes like two polished black stones. She didn't look away; she nodded slightly, a motion so small it was nearly imperceptible, but Daniel caught it. He didn't know what that nod meant.

"So this is an investigation," someone spoke. Daniel looked toward the voice—a man he didn't recognize, bald, with obvious sunburn marks on his neck. "You want us to accuse each other?"

"No," the compliance officer said. "I want you to cooperate. Cooperation means answering questions, providing whatever information you can, and—"

"And what?"

"And before this investigation concludes, do not leave this city."

More silence. Daniel noticed Halvorsen's hands twisting together under the table, knuckles white. He remembered the first time he met Halvorsen—that sunlit office, Halvorsen smiling as he handed Daniel a cup of coffee. That smile had been genuine, and Daniel was still willing to believe it was. But sincerity and innocence are two different things.

"I'd like to ask a question," Daniel spoke.

Every eye in the room turned toward him. The weight of those gazes was almost tangible, like fourteen stones pressing down on his shoulders simultaneously.

"When was the decision made to rename Sweetie?" he asked.

The room went suddenly quiet. Not silence, but a vacuum left by something that had been extracted. Daniel saw Hargrove's eyes narrow, as though something had stung him. Halvorsen's lips moved but produced no sound.

The compliance officer looked at Daniel, his expression unchanged. "That question is not relevant to this investigation."

"Not relevant?" Daniel repeated. "A product's renaming—"

"I said, it's not relevant." The compliance officer's voice remained calm, but something had entered that calm—something Daniel couldn't name but could feel: a hardness. "You only need to answer our questions, Mr. Ash. Not the other way around."

Daniel didn't push further. He lowered his head and looked at his own hands—callused from years of typing, a small burn scar on the left thumb from an experiment in graduate school. Those hands had written thousands of lines of code, drafted hundreds of reports, and held hundreds of cups of coffee. Now they rested on a conference table, palms down, fingers slightly spread—like a man laying down a weapon.

The meeting continued for another two hours. The compliance officer read out a list of names—everyone who had access to the leaked data, the time and frequency of their access. Daniel heard his own name. He heard Sara Chen's name. He heard Halvorsen's name. He didn't hear Hargrove's.

At six in the evening, the meeting ended. Everyone stood, chairs scraping against the floor in a chorus of sharp sounds. Daniel stood last. His legs were stiff from sitting too long, his joints aching. He walked to the door, passing Halvorsen as he did.

Halvorsen caught his arm. His grip was surprisingly strong—the grip of a man who had been waiting for this moment for a long time.

"I have something to tell you," Halvorsen said. "Tonight. The café downstairs. Seven o'clock."

Daniel looked at him. "Why me?"

"Because you asked the question," Halvorsen said. "The question no one else dared to ask."

He let go of Daniel's arm and walked away without looking back.

---

Daniel arrived at the café at six-fifty. It was on the ground floor of the building—a small place, six tables, wooden chairs, a counter with an espresso machine that occasionally hissed steam. He chose a table by the window and ordered an Americano. The coffee was bitter and hot, scalding his tongue.

He waited.

At seven-ten, Halvorsen walked in. He looked worse than he had in the conference room—his face was gray, his eyes bloodshot, his suit rumpled as though he had been gripping the lapels all day. He sat down without ordering and stared at Daniel.

"You want to know about the Sweetie renaming," Halvorsen said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

"It wasn't my decision."

"Whose was it?"

Halvorsen was silent for a moment. He reached into his pocket and withdrew a cigarette case—old, leather, corners worn. He took out a cigarette and lit it, his hands trembling so badly the lighter sparked three times before catching.

"Hargrove's," he said. "And above Hargrove."

"How high?"

Halvorsen inhaled deeply, the smoke curling from his nostrils. "High enough that the people at the top already knew," he said. "Way before any of it happened."

Daniel felt something cold in his stomach.

"How long have they known?"

"Since before you were brought in."

The words hung in the air like a physical object. Daniel stared at Halvorsen, watching the older man's face—the wrinkles around the eyes, the hollows of the cheeks, the thin lips pressed together like a wound that had long since stopped bleeding.

"Then why bring me in?" Daniel asked.

Halvorsen smiled—a bitter smile. "Because they needed a face," he said. "Someone the public could trust. Someone who appeared independent, unbiased, credible. A researcher, not an insider. When the truth eventually came out, they needed someone to blame."

"Me."

"You."

Daniel leaned back in his chair. The café was quiet; the only sounds were the espresso machine's occasional hiss and the muffled voices of a couple in the far corner. He looked at Halvorsen and thought of the man's face in the conference room—the look of someone who had already made peace with his fate.

"Why are you telling me this?" Daniel asked.

"Because I'm done," Halvorsen said. He stubbed out the cigarette in the ashtray, the ember dying with a soft hiss. "I've been doing this for twenty-two years. Twenty-two years of covering things up, making things disappear, pretending everything is fine. Every day I told myself: it's not that bad. Others do it too. At least we're making the world a better place."

He paused, then added: "I don't believe that anymore."

"What do you believe?"

Halvorsen looked at Daniel for a long time. "I believe," he said slowly, "that we're all complicit. Every single one of us. You, me, Hargrove. We all chose to be here. We all chose to look the other way. The only difference is how long each of us can keep pretending."

Daniel said nothing. He picked up his coffee cup and took a sip. The coffee had grown lukewarm, its bitterness diminished but not gone—like an old regret that no longer stings but never fully disappears.

"What do you know about the leak?" Daniel asked.

"I know the internal investigation is a show," Halvorsen said. "They're not looking for the leaker. They're looking for a scapegoat."

"A scapegoat?"

"Someone to take the blame when this blows up. Someone who can be sacrificed without the system being damaged." Halvorsen paused. "And I think they've chosen you."

Daniel set down his coffee cup. The ceramic made a soft sound against the wooden table. He thought of Sara Chen—suspended, monitored, suspected. He thought of Toby—on the run, changing addresses every few days. He thought of himself—sitting in this café at seven in the evening, hearing a man tell him he was about to become a scapegoat.

"Who else knows?" Daniel asked.

"Just us," Halvorsen said. "And the people at the top."

"The evidence that was leaked—who really leaked it?"

Halvorsen looked at the door, as though checking that no one was listening. "I don't know," he said. "But I know it wasn't you."

"How do you know?"

"Because if you had leaked it, you wouldn't be sitting here having coffee with me," Halvorsen said. "You'd be in a detention center."

Daniel was silent for a long time. Outside the window, the streetlights had come on, casting orange light onto the sidewalk. A cyclist passed, the bell ringing once, a clear sound that cut through the evening air.

"What happens next?" Daniel asked.

"I don't know," Halvorsen said. "But I know this: they won't let this go. The leak has exposed too much—the financial data, the user manipulation, the Sweetie incident. Someone has to pay."

"And that someone is me."

Halvorsen nodded. "Unless you can prove otherwise," he said. "Unless you can find the real leaker. Unless you can show them that the problem is bigger than one person."

Daniel thought. He thought of the data Sara had given him. Of the information Toby possessed. Of the research Priya had compiled. Of the analysis Yuki had written. He thought of the Truth Alliance—the name they had chosen in that basement, the promise they had made.

"What if I don't try to prove anything?" Daniel said. "What if I just... keep going?"

Halvorsen looked at him with surprise. "Keep going?"

"They're trying to make me stop," Daniel said. "The leak, the investigation, the threats—they're all designed to make me stop. So if I keep going, if I keep pushing, keep speaking, keep fighting—that itself would be a kind of proof."

"Proof of what?"

"Proof that I'm not afraid," Daniel said. "Proof that the truth matters more than they think."

Halvorsen was silent for a long time. Then he reached across the table and gripped Daniel's hand. His grip was cold and dry, the bones of his fingers pressing hard against Daniel's skin.

"You're either very brave," Halvorsen said, "or very stupid."

"Both, probably," Daniel said.

Halvorsen let go of his hand and stood. He buttoned his suit jacket—a reflex, a gesture of a man who had spent decades in corporate meetings. Then he turned and walked toward the door.

"Halvorsen," Daniel called after him.

Halvorsen stopped but didn't turn around.

"Why did you tell me all this?"

Halvorsen was silent for a moment. "Because I'm not a good person," he said. "But I'm not entirely bad either. And before I go, I wanted to do one good thing."

He walked out of the café. The door closed behind him with a soft click. Daniel sat alone at the table, staring at the coffee cup before him. The coffee had gone cold. The ceramic surface was smooth and cool beneath his fingers.

He thought of what Halvorsen had said: we're all complicit. Every single one of us.

He thought of his father's words: when you know what's right, you have to do it.

He finished the cold coffee, left money on the table, and walked out of the café. The evening air was warm, carrying the scent of summer—exhaust, asphalt, someone's perfume. He breathed it in and walked toward his apartment. His footsteps were steady on the pavement, even, deliberate—the footsteps of a man who had decided something.

---

Daniel returned to his office. Outside the window, the black bird had vanished. Only gray clouds and the approaching rain remained. He sat at his desk, opened his computer, and looked at the message still displayed on screen:

"Do you still want to know the truth?"

He picked up his phone, hesitated for a long time, then typed a reply:

"What is the truth?"

After sending, he waited. One minute. Two minutes. Five minutes. The phone screen dimmed, then brightened. A new message:

"The truth is a story. The question is, who gets to tell it."

Daniel looked at the message, then saw a third one appear:

"The reckoning is not the end of the story. The reckoning is the beginning."

Rain began to fall. The drops struck the window, producing a soft, gentle sound—like fingers tapping on glass. Daniel closed his phone and leaned back in his chair, listening to the rain. He thought of an afternoon many years ago, with the same rain, the same sound. Back then he still believed in certain things—truth, justice, the goodness in human nature. Now he wasn't sure what he still believed. But one thing he was certain of: the reckoning had begun. And the true reckoning was not about what had happened in the past, but about what would happen in the future.

He stood and walked to the window, watching the city through the rain. The tall buildings blurred behind the curtain of rain, as though dissolving. Daniel thought: perhaps this is the essence of the reckoning—not finding answers, but letting everything dissolve, become blurred, become an unrecognizable mass of chaos waiting to be rewritten.

His phone buzzed again. He looked: the final message from that unknown number:

"Tomorrow, ten in the morning. The old place. Bring your questions."

Daniel pocketed the phone. The rain continued, the city's outlines growing more and more blurred. He knew what would happen tomorrow—not the specifics, but something larger, something irreversible. The reckoning had begun, and he was standing at its center.

Outside the window, a black bird emerged from the clouds, its wings drenched, but still flying. Daniel watched it until it disappeared beyond the curtain of rain. He thought: perhaps the bird knew where it was going, perhaps it didn't. But it was flying. In this world where everything was dissolving, it was still flying.

That was enough. Perhaps that was enough.
