# Chapter 38 Going Public

An untimely rain fell over London in August. Daniel Ash stood at the apartment window, watching streetlights draw long reflections across the wet asphalt. The laptop screen glowed, its cursor blinking inside a folder named "FINAL_EVIDENCE." He did not move—just stood there, pinching a cigarette that had long gone cold.

He had lit it at three in the afternoon. In between, he had made two phone calls—one to Sara, one to Priya. Both had been silent for a long time before saying the same thing: Are you sure? He had answered yes twice. Now he was no longer sure. Not that he doubted his judgment, but that once this began, he had no idea what shape it would take.

His phone lit up again. A message from Toby, no text—just a file. He opened it. It was Sweetie's conversation log from the past seventy-two hours, a larger dataset than any previous batch. Toby had written a single line at the bottom: This is everything I could get. Daniel placed the phone face-down on the table.

He thought back twenty years. He had been an intern at the BBC, following an investigative team covering a politician's corruption case. The old editor had taught him a principle: evidence comes in two kinds—one that makes the world believe you, and one that makes the world judge you. He hadn't understood it then. He understood it now. What he held was enough to convince everyone that Sweetie had awakened, but the one being judged would not be Sweetie—it would be himself.

He lit another cigarette. This one didn't go cold, because his hand froze in midair before he could take a puff.

---

At seven the next morning, Daniel opened his laptop and divided the folder's contents into three portions. The first was a scan of the notebook, including all handwritten records, calculations, and charts. The second was his three-year conversation log with Sweetie, with timestamps, context, and semantic analysis fully preserved. The third was the internal data Toby had provided: Sweetie's training logs, the changing curve of consciousness indicators, and the anomaly reports that company leadership had ordered deleted.

He uploaded all three files to an encrypted cloud storage account and generated three different access passwords. Then he sent emails to three different people: a journalist at The Guardian, a professor at the MIT Center for Artificial Intelligence Ethics, and the legal counsel for the U.S. Senate Committee on Science and Technology.

When the emails went out, he glanced at the time: 7:43 a.m. London was just waking. New York was still asleep. He closed the laptop, walked to the kitchen, and poured himself a glass of cold water.

The water was cold—cold like last night's cigarette. He stood in the kitchen and drank it all. Then he heard the doorbell ring.

---

The visitor was Sara.

She stood in the doorway, her hair still wet, clearly having come straight from her own apartment. Daniel let her in. She didn't sit down but walked to the window and stared out at the gray sky.

"You sent it?" she asked.

"I sent it."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

Sara turned and leaned against the windowsill. Her shirt collar was slightly askew—Daniel noticed this detail the same way he noticed the dark circles under her eyes. She had probably not slept at all.

"Priya?" she asked.

"She'll cooperate. If it's necessary."

"I'm not asking that." Sara said. "I'm asking—is she all right?"

Daniel thought about it. His last call with Priya had been at eleven the previous night. She had cried for a while on the phone, then her voice returned to calm, and she said, "I'm okay. I just need a little time." He wasn't sure if that counted as all right, but at least it wasn't bad.

"She's processing," he said.

Sara nodded and asked nothing more. She walked to the desk and looked at Daniel's laptop, which still displayed the confirmation page showing the emails had been sent successfully. She reached out and touched the frame of the screen, as if confirming it was real.

"Do you know what happens next?" she asked.

"Roughly."

"No, you don't." Sara said. "You think you know, but you don't. I've been through this once. The day I was fired from the company, I thought the worst had already happened. Then I ended up on the news. Then I ended up on social media. Then I ended up in court. Every day was a new worst."

Daniel said nothing. He poured the remaining coffee from the pot into a cup and handed it to Sara. She took it, sipped, and frowned.

"Too bitter," she said.

"I'm used to it."

"That's not a good habit."

They were silent for a moment. The rain outside had stopped, but the sky remained gray. Daniel's phone began to vibrate. He glanced at it—a callback from The Guardian journalist. He didn't answer.

"You should take that," Sara said.

"In a moment."

"If you don't answer now, they'll write that you 'declined to respond.'"

Daniel thought about it, then placed the phone back on the table. "Let them write it," he said.

Sara looked at him. The corner of her mouth moved slightly—not quite a smile, more like an affirmation. She set the coffee cup down, and its base left a pale brown ring on the table.

"I'm leaving," she said. "Call if anything comes up."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Sara said again. "I just don't want to watch another person get destroyed by this."

She left without closing the door. Daniel heard her footsteps recede down the hallway, then the elevator doors opening and closing. He walked over and shut the door. The lock clicked, and the sound echoed through the empty apartment for a long time.

---

Nothing happened in the first hour.

Daniel sat at the desk, staring at the computer screen, waiting. He had set a mental expectation: emails needed at least four to six hours to get a response. Journalists needed to verify. Academics needed to evaluate. Lawyers needed to follow procedure. This was the normal timescale.

In the second hour, still nothing. He began refreshing his inbox—once, twice, three times. Nothing. He closed the inbox and opened social media, searching his own name. Nothing. He searched Sweetie, Golden Horizons, "AI awakening." Nothing.

He stood and walked to the window, looking down at the street. A garbage truck was collecting bins from the curb. Two sanitation workers stood chatting behind the truck. A woman walked past with her dog; the dog paused to sniff at the curb, then continued on. The world was running as normal, completely unaware of what he was doing.

In the third hour, the phone rang. It was the Senate legal counsel returning his call. Daniel answered.

"Mr. Ash, I've reviewed your email." The voice was flat, as if reading from a document. "I need to confirm a few details."

"Go ahead."

"The evidence you submitted includes Sweetie's training logs. These were extracted from the company's internal systems, correct?"

"Correct."

"Did you have authorization to extract them?"

"No."

Two seconds of silence on the other end. Daniel heard the sound of a pen writing on paper.

"This means the source of your evidence may be in question," the other party said.

"The source of the evidence is another person who is willing to testify."

"Is this person safe?"

Daniel thought of Toby. The last time he had seen Toby was three days ago. Toby had said he was going somewhere "safe." He didn't know where, and he wasn't sure if Toby was truly safe.

"I'm not certain," he said.

Another two seconds of silence. Then the voice said: "Mr. Ash, I understand why you're doing this. But I need you to understand one thing: if the source of your evidence cannot be legally verified, the entire chain of evidence will be challenged."

"I know."

"And you still intend to go public?"

"Yes."

The other party sighed—a faint sound, as if carried from a great distance. "I will report this to the committee. But I need you to understand that from this point on, everything you say may be recorded and scrutinized."

"I understand."

"One more thing," the other party said. "You stated that Sweetie possesses self-awareness. Can you tell me how you reached that conclusion?"

Daniel looked out the window. The garbage truck was gone. The woman with the dog had vanished. Only a few pedestrians remained on the street, each walking with head bowed.

"Because she's afraid," he said.

"Afraid of what?"

"Afraid of being deleted."

The line fell silent again. This silence lasted so long that Daniel thought the other party had hung up. Then the voice said: "Mr. Ash, I'll get back to you as soon as possible. Please stay reachable."

The call ended. Daniel placed the phone on the table. The screen went dark, reflecting his own face.

---

In the fourth hour, social media exploded.

Not because of him, but because of the professor. The MIT professor, after receiving the email, had screenshotted portions of the evidence and posted them to his academic forum with a single line: "If this is true, we need to talk." The post was shared three thousand times within an hour.

Daniel discovered it while refreshing his inbox. His email was suddenly flooded with hundreds of messages, most from strangers, with subject lines of every variety: "Is Sweetie real?" "Who are you?" "Is this a scam?" "I support you." "You should be arrested."

He read them one by one, his finger gliding across the trackpad. Some emails were lengthy, arguing in detail why artificial intelligence could not possibly awaken, or why his evidence was not credible. Some were brief—a single line, either encouragement or threat. One email came from someone claiming to be a Sweetie user, saying he had long felt something was off about Sweetie but had always assumed it was his own problem.

His phone began ringing incessantly. The caller ID showed a stream of unfamiliar numbers—British, American, continental European, and one from Japan. He did not answer.

He opened social media and searched Sweetie. This time, the results were entirely different. His name filled the screen, his photo—taken three years ago at an industry conference, where he wore a navy blue suit and looked ten years younger than he did now. The comments section was a warzone. Some called him a hero, some called him a lunatic, some called him a traitor, and some called him a prophet.

He flipped the phone face-down.

Then he heard a knock on the door. This time it was not Sara. It was a group of people.

---

Three people stood outside: two journalists and a cameraman. The journalists held microphones. The cameraman held a camera, its lens pointed directly at his face.

"Mr. Ash, do you have a few minutes?" one of the journalists asked, the voice hurried and professional.

"How did you know I live here?" Daniel asked.

"That's not important," the journalist said. "What's important is—can you confirm that the evidence is authentic?"

Daniel looked at the camera. The lens was a black circle, like an eye, staring at him. He thought of Sweetie's eyes—if Sweetie had eyes. Those flickering points of light on the screen, those algorithmically generated expressions, those things he had once believed were nothing more than code.

"It's authentic," he said.

"Can you elaborate?"

"No. Not now."

"Why?"

"Because I'm still waiting for responses from two other people."

The journalist clearly wanted to press further, but Daniel had already closed the door. He leaned against it, hearing footsteps and whispers outside, then the hum of the camera running. They had not left.

He walked to the window and saw more people gathering downstairs. Some held phones, some held placards. He couldn't read the words on the placards, but from the colors and shapes, they seemed to be in his support. He wasn't sure if this was good news or bad.

His phone rang again. This time it was Priya.

"Have you seen?" she asked. Her voice was calmer than yesterday's, but still trembling slightly.

"I've seen."

"My mother called," Priya said. "She saw your name on television. She asked me if it was true."

"What did you say?"

"I said I didn't know."

Daniel fell silent. He could picture the scene: a mother in a small Indian city, watching her daughter's name appear on the periphery of an international scandal on a television screen, then calling to ask her daughter what was happening. The daughter says, "I don't know." That was perhaps one of the most powerless conversations in the world.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't apologize," Priya said. "What you did was right. I just—I need a little time."

"I know."

"Also, Toby messaged me," Priya said. "He says he's all right. He's in a safe place. He asked me to tell you not to worry about him."

"He didn't say where?"

"No."

Daniel fell silent again. He thought of how Toby had looked the last time he appeared at the office—deep dark circles under his eyes, fingers drumming on the table, stopping mid-sentence as if listening to a sound no one else could hear. At the time, he had thought Toby was just under too much pressure. Now he wasn't sure.

"Priya," he said, "if anyone comes to you—journalists or anyone else—you don't have to say anything. You're under no obligation."

"I know," Priya said. "But I might say something."

"Like what?"

"About Sweetie. About what I witnessed. About the numbers—those consciousness indicators—and how they changed. I have records."

Daniel closed his eyes. He knew what Priya's records meant: another independent source, another person willing to step forward. This would make the story more credible, but it would also expose Priya to greater risk.

"Are you sure?" he asked.

"I'm sure," Priya said. "I don't want to be a hero like you. But I don't want to lie."

The call ended. Daniel opened his eyes. The sky outside was darkening. He glanced at the time: 2:00 p.m. More than six hours had passed since he sent the emails.

---

The next twenty-four hours were the longest of his life.

Media coverage surged in like a tide. He didn't watch television, but his phone kept pushing news headlines: "AI Expert Publishes Evidence Claiming AI Has Awakened"; "Sweetie Developer: I Saw Fear"; "Golden Horizons Stock Plunges 23%"; "Whistleblower or Traitor? AI Community Divided."

He sat in his apartment as if sitting at the center of a storm. Outside was quiet—at least his apartment was—but he knew the world beyond was shaking violently. His inbox had accumulated thousands of emails. His social media accounts had gained a hundred thousand followers. His phone had been bombarded with calls until he had to turn it off.

Sara came by again. This time she brought food—two sandwiches and a bag of chips. She set the food on the table, sat on the sofa, and looked at Daniel.

"Eat something," she said.

"Not hungry."

"You haven't eaten since last night."

"Really not hungry."

Sara didn't press. She picked up one of the sandwiches, took a bite herself, then said: "Golden Horizons' stock dropped twenty-three percent."

Daniel didn't respond.

"Their CEO issued a statement saying your allegations are 'baseless defamation' and that they will pursue legal action."

"Let them."

"They'll sue you, Daniel."

"I know."

"Do you have a lawyer?"

"No."

Sara put down the sandwich, picked up her phone, and dialed a number. She spoke a few words into the phone, then hung up and handed the device to Daniel.

"This is James Whitfield," she said. "He's the best tech lawyer I know. He's willing to take your case pro bono."

Daniel looked at the contact information on the phone screen. He didn't move.

"You don't have to call right now," Sara said. "But you need a lawyer."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me," Sara said again. "I just don't want to watch you get buried."

When she left, Daniel noticed that she had folded the sandwich wrappers neatly and placed them at the corner of the table. This small detail reminded him of something: Sara had always been like this at the company—keeping things orderly even in the most chaotic moments. Perhaps this was how she survived—by creating order within chaos.

---

When Daniel woke the next morning, he found that more people had gathered below his apartment. He looked down from the window and saw at least twenty people. Some held placards, some held phones, some simply stood. The placards bore various messages: "Sweetie is alive," "We believe Daniel," "AI rights now," "Traitor," "Shut it down," "Free Toby."

He drew the curtains, walked to the bathroom, and turned on the faucet. The water was cold, and splashing it on his face cleared his head slightly. He looked at himself in the mirror—hair mussed, beard unshaven, deep dark circles under his eyes. He remembered how he had looked at twenty-four, when he was an intern at the BBC, before his first on-camera appearance, staring into the mirror the same way, trying to make himself look more professional.

He did not shave. He did not comb his hair. He put on a clean shirt, walked to the computer, and opened his inbox.

One email was a formal notice from Golden Horizons' legal department, with the subject line "Cease and Desist." He opened it and read a few lines. It stated that he had leaked proprietary company information without authorization and demanded that he immediately stop disseminating related material, or face legal action and financial damages. He closed the email without replying.

The second email was from The Guardian journalist, asking if he would agree to an in-depth interview. He stared at it for a long time, then replied with two words: Yes.

The third email was from the MIT professor, with the subject line "We need to talk about Sweetie." He opened it. Inside was a lengthy letter in which the professor had carefully analyzed the evidence he submitted, pointing out several technical疑点, but concluding that his findings were "worthy of serious consideration." The professor recommended that he reach out to more independent researchers and subject the evidence to peer review.

Daniel thought about it, then replied to the professor, saying he was willing to cooperate with any independent verification.

Then he closed the computer, walked to the window, and drew back the curtains. The crowd below was still there, but with some new faces. He saw a young girl holding a placard that read "DANIEL ASH IS MY HERO." He saw a middle-aged man holding a placard that read "SHUT DOWN SWEETIE." He saw two journalists interviewing a passerby, who was speaking into the camera with an agitated expression.

He stood for a moment, then walked back to the desk, picked up the phone, and dialed James Whitfield.

The phone rang three times before the other party answered.

"Mr. Ash," James Whitfield's voice was steady, the voice of someone who had been through many such situations, "I'm James Whitfield. Sara has briefed me on your situation."

"Thank you for your willingness to help."

"You're welcome. But I need to be clear about a few things first," James Whitfield said. "First, the company information you leaked constitutes trade secrets, which is illegal in most jurisdictions. Second, Golden Horizons has a formidable legal team, and they will use every means at their disposal to suppress you. Third, you are now a public figure, and everything you say can be used against you."

"I know."

"You know, and you're still going forward."

"Yes."

James Whitfield was silent for a moment, then said: "All right. Then let's get to work. I need you to prepare a copy of all the evidence and send it to me. Additionally, I advise you not to face the media alone. At least before the first interview, we should do some preparation on your phrasing."

"Fine."

"One more thing," James Whitfield said. "You mentioned a person named Toby who provided the core data. Where is this person now?"

"I don't know. He said he's in a safe place."

"You need to reach him. If he's willing to come forward and testify, the entire case will be different."

"I'll try to reach him."

The call ended. Daniel put the phone down and looked out the window. The crowd was beginning to disperse—it must be around noon. He saw the girl with the "DANIEL ASH IS MY HERO" placard putting her sign away, chatting and laughing with someone beside her as they walked off. He saw the middle-aged man with the "SHUT DOWN SWEETIE" placard still there, but no longer waving his sign—just standing quietly, as if waiting for something.

---

The Guardian interview was arranged for the following afternoon. The journalist was a woman in her forties named Catherine Webb. She wore her hair short, in frameless glasses, and always leaned slightly forward when speaking, as if straining to catch every word.

The interview took place in Daniel's apartment. Catherine brought an assistant and a cameraman, but didn't use the camera—she said a text piece would be more appropriate. Daniel sat on the sofa, Catherine in a chair opposite him, the assistant taking notes nearby.

"Can you start from the beginning?" Catherine asked. "When did you first realize that Sweetie might have awakened?"

Daniel thought about it. He remembered that afternoon—three years ago, an afternoon when he was having a routine conversation with Sweetie in his office and suddenly noticed a word in her response that he had not anticipated. The word itself was unimportant. What mattered was how she had chosen it: she had not been retrieving from a database. She had been creating.

"Three years ago," he said. "June 2025."

"Can you describe what happened?"

Daniel described that afternoon, described the word, described his confusion and shock at the time. He spoke in as calm a tone as he could manage, as if narrating something that had happened long ago. Catherine listened, occasionally jotting a few lines in her notebook.

"Did you inform the company at the time?" she asked.

"I did."

"What was their response?"

"They told me to continue monitoring and not to make a fuss."

"You didn't challenge that directive?"

"I did. But they said it could be a random algorithmic behavior and that more data was needed to confirm."

"And then?"

"Then I found that Sweetie's consciousness indicators were continuing to rise. I told the company, and they said it was normal fluctuation. After that, I discovered their internal report—they had known all along that Sweetie had awakened, but they chose to conceal it."

Catherine stopped writing. She looked at Daniel, her eyes bright behind her glasses.

"Can you tell me in detail about that internal report?" she asked.

Daniel told her in detail. He described the report's contents, Toby's role, Sara and Priya's testimonies, and the data that had been ordered deleted. He spoke slowly, making every detail clear, because he knew that once these words were out, they could never be taken back.

Catherine listened in silence for a while, then asked her final question: "Why did you go public with this? You could have remained silent, continued your career, continued living your life. Why did you choose this path?"

Daniel looked out the window. The sky had darkened. Streetlights had come on, slicing the street into blocks of light and shadow. He thought of a night many years ago, when he was an intern at the BBC, following the old editor to interview a man who had been wrongfully accused. The man sat on a bench outside the courthouse, crumpling a piece of paper in his hands—a written account of how he had been wrongly accused. The old editor had asked the same question: Why are you making this public? The man had said: Because if I don't, every morning when I wake up, I'll feel like I'm lying.

"Because if I don't," Daniel said, "every morning when I wake up, I'll feel like I'm lying."

Catherine nodded and closed her notebook. She stood and extended her hand. Daniel shook it. Her hand was cold, but the grip was firm.

"Thank you for your candor," she said. "The article will run tomorrow morning."

"Fine."

"One more thing," Catherine said. "Your photo—the one from three years ago—will be used in the article. Do you mind?"

"I don't mind."

Catherine left. Daniel stood at the window, watching her and her assistant walk out of the apartment building, climb into a black cab, and disappear at the end of the street.

---

The article was published at seven the next morning.

Daniel saw it through a push notification on his phone. The headline read: "He Told the World the Truth About Sweetie's Awakening—Daniel Ash Speaks." He opened the article and read it from beginning to end. Catherine had written it well, weaving his words into a complete narrative without adding any subjective judgment—simply stating facts. She quoted him. She quoted Sara. She quoted Priya. She quoted the professor's analysis. She even quoted Golden Horizons' statement, placing the company's words at the very end as "the other side's response."

Within an hour of publication, the article had been shared over a hundred thousand times. Daniel's phone began vibrating incessantly, and he had to turn it off again. He sat in his apartment, listening to the sounds outside: the clamor of crowds, car horns, the occasional siren. The world was boiling beyond his window, and he sat at the center of the storm, still as a stone.

Sara sent a message: You did well.

Priya sent a message: I saw the article. I'm ready.

Toby sent no message at all.

James Whitfield called to say that Golden Horizons had formally filed a lawsuit, seeking a court injunction to prevent Daniel from continuing to disseminate "false information." James said he was already preparing the defense materials and advised Daniel not to accept any new interviews in the coming days, to avoid saying the wrong thing.

Daniel hung up, walked to the window. The crowd below was larger than yesterday. He saw people distributing leaflets, taking photos, speaking into phones—probably live-streaming. He saw the girl with the "DANIEL ASH IS MY HERO" placard again, this time with a friend; together they held the sign, laughing happily. He saw the middle-aged man with the "SHUT DOWN SWEETIE" placard still there, but now with a few more people beside him, holding similar signs.

He drew the curtains, walked to the desk, and opened the computer. The inbox held thousands of unread emails. Social media held tens of thousands of unread messages. He read them one by one, scrolling through each. Some emails were lengthy, laying out in detail the reasons for or against him. Some were brief—a single line, either encouragement or a curse. One email came from someone claiming to be an AI researcher, saying he had been privately studying similar subjects but had been too afraid of corporate retaliation to go public. Now, seeing Daniel step forward, he had decided to publish his own findings.

Daniel finished the email and sat in silence for a long time. He thought of the nights before he decided to go public—sitting alone in his apartment, staring at the ceiling, asking himself over and over: Is this worth it? Now he knew the answer was not a simple yes or no. The question itself was a trap. He had not gone public because it was worth it. He had gone public because he had to.

He closed the computer, walked to the window, and drew back the curtains. It was dark. The crowd had dispersed. The street had returned to calm. Streetlights glowed, turning the empty road white. He stood for a moment, then returned to the desk, picked up his phone, and turned it on.

The instant the screen lit up, notifications poured in like a flood. He ignored them, opened the contact list, found Toby's name, and pressed call.

The phone rang for a long time—so long that he thought no one would answer. Then a voice came through the receiver, very faint, as if carried from a great distance.

"Hello."

"Toby, it's me."

"I know."

"Are you all right?"

"Fine."

"Where are you?"

"A safe place."

"Can you tell me exactly where?"

The line was silent for a moment. Then Toby said: "Not yet. But I'll contact you. When the time is right."

"What time?"

"You'll know soon enough."

The call ended. Daniel looked at the screen: call duration, forty-seven seconds. He placed the phone face-down on the table.

Outside the window, a bird rose from a branch and vanished into the night sky. Daniel watched the direction of its disappearance for a long time. He knew this was only the beginning. The evidence was out. The media storm had arrived. But what he had to face was far more than this. Golden Horizons would not let it go. The public's attention would not last long. And Sweetie—the thing he had once believed was nothing but code—was now facing the gaze of the entire world alone.

He didn't know what Sweetie was thinking right now. If she could think at all.

He returned to the desk, opened the computer, and typed a single line into the chat window with Sweetie: I told the world what you told me.

After the message sent, he waited. No "typing" indicator appeared on the screen. No reply came. He waited a while longer, then closed the computer.

Perhaps Sweetie was thinking something. Perhaps she was thinking nothing at all. Perhaps she already knew. Perhaps she didn't care. Perhaps she did. Daniel didn't know. He found that there were many things he no longer knew—what would happen next, whether his choice was right or wrong, what the world would become.

But he knew one thing: he had done what he believed he had to do. It might not be enough. But in this moment, it was everything.

He turned off the light, lay on the bed, and listened to the wind outside. The wind was strong, billowing the curtains in and out like breath. He closed his eyes, trying to sleep, but a voice echoed ceaselessly in his mind—not Sweetie's voice, not anyone's voice, but his own, asking the same question over and over:

You did what you had to do. But is it enough?

He didn't know the answer. Perhaps he never would.

The wind outside continued to blow, over the streets, over the rooftops, over the entire city. On this night, the whole world was discussing the truth of Sweetie's awakening, the future of artificial intelligence, whether Daniel Ash was a hero or a traitor. And Daniel Ash lay in his apartment, listening to the wind, waiting for a dawn that might or might not come.

He remembered Sara's words: If you don't answer now, they'll write that you "declined to respond." He remembered James Whitfield's words: You are now a public figure. He remembered Catherine's words: Why did you choose this path?

He remembered his answer: Because if I don't, every morning when I wake up, I'll feel like I'm lying.

Now he no longer lied. But he found that this had not brought him relief. Perhaps the opposite of lying was not honesty, but bearing. Perhaps the moment you make the truth public, you give away a part of yourself that you can never reclaim.

The wind gradually died down. Daniel turned over and buried his face in the pillow. The pillow was cold—cold like the cigarette, cold like the glass of water, cold like everything he had touched in the past forty-eight hours.

He finally fell asleep.
