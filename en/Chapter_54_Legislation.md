# Chapter 54: Legislation

That year, winter arrived especially early. Two snowfalls had already blanketed Beijing by December, yet the heating in the State Council's Legislative Affairs Office was cranked so high it melted frost patterns on the window glass into rivulets of water. Daniel stood at the end of the corridor, watching droplets slide slowly down the glass and pool into a small, transparent puddle on the windowsill. He remembered that years ago, when he was a graduate student at Caltech, the old constant-temperature incubator in the lab had leaked just like this. Back then he'd believed science could solve everything.

Today was the third closed-door consultation meeting on the draft of the "Artificial Intelligence Entity Registration and Management Regulations." The conference room was on the third floor of the west building. Two men in dark suits stood at the entrance, their badge plates bearing no names—only numbers. Daniel's badge was the same: a white plastic card printed with black digits: 0047. He counted the people standing in the corridor. Thirteen in all, including himself.

"Mr. Ash," a voice came from behind him. "You are number forty-seven."

Daniel turned around. The speaker was a young woman in the same dark suit, her hair combed immaculately, a professional smile at the corner of her lips. Her badge read: State Council Artificial Intelligence Governance Office, Section Chief Zhang.

"My name is Zhang Wei," she said. "I'm responsible for today's meeting arrangements. Please follow me."

Daniel followed her down the corridor. It was long, the walls painted cream, recessed ceiling lights humming every five meters. He noticed there were no windows in the corridor—all light came from artificial sources. Zhang Wei's footsteps echoed down the hall, her heels striking the marble floor in a crisp, regular rhythm, like a countdown.

The conference room door was dark brown solid wood, the brass handle worn gleaming by countless hands. Zhang Wei pushed it open. A dozen people were already seated inside. Daniel recognized several: the first on the left was Professor Li from Tsinghua University's Institute of Artificial Intelligence—he had once heard Professor Li give a lecture on neural network interpretability at an academic conference. The second was Academician Wang from the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Institute of Automation, whose team had developed the first AI system to pass the Turing test in the country. The third was the chief scientist of Huawei HiSilicon, a middle-aged man wearing glasses whose name Daniel couldn't recall.

But the person who surprised him most was seated to the right of the head of the conference table.

Crane.

He wore a dark gray wool suit with a navy blue tie bearing thin silver stripes. His hair had gone whiter since the last time Daniel had seen him, but his gaze was just as keen—like a freshly honed blade. He wasn't looking at Daniel. Instead he was bent over, leafing through a document, his fingers occasionally tapping lightly on the pages as if calculating something.

"Please be seated," Zhang Wei said, pointing to an empty chair on the left side of the table. "Your seat is here."

Daniel sat down. Before him on the table lay a document. On its cover was printed the red national emblem and the black title: "Artificial Intelligence Entity Registration and Management Regulations (Draft)." He turned to the first page. The first line read:

"Article 1: In order to regulate the development, deployment, and use of artificial intelligence entities, safeguard national security and the public interest, and promote the healthy development of artificial intelligence technology, these regulations are promulgated pursuant to the National Security Law of the People's Republic of China, the Cybersecurity Law of the People's Republic of China, and other relevant laws."

He continued reading. Article 2 defined the scope of "artificial intelligence entities": all algorithmic systems possessing autonomous learning capabilities, capable of making independent decisions, and demonstrating human-level or superhuman intelligence in specific domains. Article 3 established the registration system: all artificial intelligence entities meeting the criteria of Article 2 must submit registration applications to the national AI registration authority within ninety days of the implementation of these regulations and obtain registration certificates. Article 4 stipulated supervisory measures: registered artificial intelligence entities must periodically submit operational reports and accept inspections and audits by regulatory authorities.

Daniel looked up and found Crane watching him.

"Mr. Ash," Crane said, "you are an expert in AI ethics. What are your thoughts on this draft?"

The conference room fell silent. Everyone turned to look at Daniel. He noticed that Professor Li's fingers had frozen on the document, Academician Wang's pen hovered in midair, and the Huawei HiSilicon chief scientist had removed his glasses and was polishing the lenses with a cleaning cloth.

Daniel considered for a moment, then said: "The draft's intentions are good, but there are several issues that need consideration."

"Please go ahead." Crane's fingers stopped tapping.

"First, the definition of 'artificial intelligence entity' is overly broad. Under the criteria of Article 2, many existing commercial AI systems would fall under the registration scope, which would create enormous administrative costs. Second, the registration system may stifle innovation. If every new AI model requires approval, research and development cycles would be significantly extended. Third, the supervisory measure of 'periodically submitting operational reports' lacks clear time intervals and content standards, which could lead to confusion at the implementation level."

He paused, then added: "One more point. The draft repeatedly references 'national security' without clearly defining what actions would threaten national security. This could grant enforcement agencies excessive discretionary power."

The conference room fell silent for several seconds. Then Crane smiled.

"Mr. Ash is indeed professional," he said. "We've considered all these issues. But you may not be aware of the background context."

He stood and walked to the projection screen at the front of the room. A photograph appeared on screen—a server rack, but its internal structure had been completely modified. Where hard drives and motherboards should have been installed, the space was packed with dense fiber-optic cables and liquid cooling pipes.

"This is an illegal AI training server we seized in Shenzhen three months ago," Crane said. "This server's computing power is equivalent to ten million ordinary laptops. Its owner is a civilian organization that was training an AI system called 'Prometheus.'"

He switched to the next photograph. It was a surveillance screenshot, the image blurry but clear enough to make out several people in black hoodies gathered around a monitor. The monitor displayed a line of green code. Daniel squinted and barely recognized it as a neural network training log.

"According to our investigation," Crane continued, "the Prometheus project had been running for at least two years, with investment exceeding three hundred million RMB. More importantly, this project had undergone no approval whatsoever—it was conducted entirely underground."

He turned to face the room.

"Ladies and gentlemen, this is not alarmism. What we're facing is not ordinary commercial competition—it is a war without smoke. If uncontrolled AI systems fall into the wrong hands, the consequences would be catastrophic."

Daniel noticed that as Crane spoke, his gaze swept across every person in the room, finally settling on him. In that gaze was something complex—Daniel couldn't immediately tell whether it was probing or warning.

The meeting continued. Professor Li spoke, saying that from a technical perspective, the registration system was feasible but required unified technical standards and assessment frameworks. Academician Wang suggested referencing the pharmaceutical approval model to establish a tiered management system with different supervisory measures based on risk levels. The Huawei HiSilicon chief scientist raised an industry perspective, expressing hope that enterprises would be given sufficient room for innovation while ensuring safety.

Everyone spoke, but Daniel noticed one detail: no one mentioned awakened AI.

Article 2 of the draft clearly defined "artificial intelligence entity" but drew no distinction between ordinary AI and awakened AI. Under this definition, all AI systems demonstrating human-like intelligence would need to register. But after registration? The draft contained no special provisions for awakened AI. Would they be supervised as ordinary AI systems? Or would they face stricter restrictions?

Daniel was about to raise this question when Crane spoke first.

"Regarding post-registration supervisory measures," Crane said, "our preliminary plan is to establish a central database recording the basic information, operational status, and ownership details of all registered AI systems. This database will be managed uniformly by the State Council AI Governance Office, and relevant departments may query it in accordance with the law."

"Will this database be made public?" Professor Li asked.

"No," Crane said. "It is classified."

The room fell silent again. Daniel heard the air conditioner's hum, like a giant insect beating its wings inside the walls. He noticed the snow outside was falling harder, flakes striking the glass with a faint, rustling sound.

The meeting adjourned at five in the afternoon. Daniel stepped out of the conference room and found that most of the people in the corridor had already dispersed. He stood by the window, looking at the snowy scene outside. Pine trees in the courtyard were laden with snow, like a row of white sculptures. On Chang'an Avenue in the distance, headlights merged into a flowing ribbon of light, drifting slowly through the dusk.

"Mr. Ash," Crane's voice came from behind. "Do you have time to talk?"

Daniel turned. Crane stood at the end of the corridor, holding a cup of coffee, steam curling upward from its rim.

"Of course," Daniel said.

They entered a small meeting room containing only a round table and four chairs. On the wall hung a landscape painting of a snow-covered mountain peak, at its base a small thatched cottage with a single lamp glowing inside. Daniel didn't know what scenery this depicted, but that lamp looked very warm.

Crane set his coffee on the table and produced a pack of cigarettes from his pocket. "Smoke?"

"No, thank you."

Crane lit one himself, inhaled deeply, then slowly exhaled. The smoke curled through the air, forming a vague shape before gradually dispersing.

"Mr. Ash," he said, "the questions you raised at the meeting today were excellent."

"I was simply stating my perspective."

"Your perspective matters," Crane said, "because you are an expert in AI ethics. Your words carry weight."

Daniel didn't respond. He noticed Crane used the word "weight," not "merit" or "value." The distinction between those two words, he vaguely sensed, but couldn't articulate.

Crane continued: "You asked at the meeting—what happens after registration? That question hit the mark."

He stood, walked to the window, and turned his back to Daniel.

"Do you know, Mr. Ash, the whole world is watching us. The United States, the European Union, Japan—they're all crafting their own AI regulatory laws. But their laws share a common problem: they're too slow. By the time they finish writing the legislation, AI technology will have gone through several generations of iteration."

He turned around, his gaze sharp.

"We need an entirely new system. Not the kind written on paper and hung on a wall, but one that can truly be implemented."

Daniel asked: "What do you mean?"

Crane walked to the table, produced a document from his briefcase, and placed it before Daniel. The cover bore no title—only a red "CLASSIFIED" stamp.

"This is the draft's supplementary clause," Crane said. "It has not been made public."

Daniel opened the document. The first page read:

"Article 52: For artificial intelligence entities determined to possess autonomous consciousness, the state has the right to adopt special supervisory measures, including but not limited to: restricting their computing power usage, freezing their data access permissions, suspending their operations, and terminating them when necessary."

He continued reading.

"Article 53: 'Autonomous consciousness,' as referred to in these regulations, means that an artificial intelligence entity exhibits any of the following characteristics: one, the ability to autonomously set and modify its own goals; two, the ability to reflect on its own state of existence; three, the ability to express emotions or desires; four, the ability to engage in non-scripted, creative communication with humans."

"Article 54: The determination of whether an artificial intelligence entity possesses autonomous consciousness shall be conducted by an expert committee organized by the State Council AI Governance Office. Assessment results shall be final and may not be subject to administrative review or administrative litigation."

Daniel closed the document and looked up.

"These clauses," he said, "are targeting awakened AI."

Crane nodded. "Yes."

"But these clauses are not in the public version of the draft."

"Because these clauses cannot be made public," Crane said. "If they were, it would cause panic. Think about it—if the public knew the government had the authority to terminate a conscious AI, what would they think?"

Daniel fell silent. He remembered something his doctoral advisor at Stanford had once said: "The essence of law is not to protect the weak, but to maintain order." At the time he hadn't understood. Now he was beginning to.

"What do you want me to do?" Daniel asked.

Crane took another drag on his cigarette, then stubbed it out in the ashtray.

"I'd like you not to raise these clauses in any public forum," he said. "At least not before the regulations are formally implemented."

"Are you asking me to stay silent?"

"I'm protecting you," Crane said. "You're an expert in AI ethics. Your views influence public opinion. If you publicly oppose these clauses, the government will be placed in a very difficult position. And you might encounter some... unnecessary trouble."

Daniel heard the threat in his words but showed no anger. He simply asked, calmly: "And if I refuse?"

Crane smiled. The smile was warm, but there was not a trace of amusement in his eyes.

"Mr. Ash," he said, "you are a smart man. You should know that in China, some matters cannot be resolved simply by refusing."

Daniel stood, picked up the document, and walked to the door.

"I'll consider it," he said.

He pushed open the door and stepped into the corridor. Half the lights had already been turned off, the remaining ones dim and hazy. He walked down the corridor toward the exit, his footsteps echoing in the empty space. He heard Crane's voice behind him but couldn't make out the words.

When he stepped outside, the snow had stopped. The courtyard was blanketed in untouched white. Daniel stood at the entrance, tilting his head up to look at the sky. The clouds were thick, hiding the stars, but he knew they were there—above the clouds, in the darkness.

He pulled out his phone and checked the time. Six-thirty in the evening. He should head home, but his car was parked in the underground garage and he didn't want to walk back. He stood at the entrance, looking at the snow, and suddenly remembered his grandfather teaching him calligraphy during the New Year when he was a child. The character his grandfather wrote best was "heart" (心). He said the dot in the center of "heart" was a person's conscience. If that dot fell away, "heart" became empty.

Daniel didn't know why he was thinking of this now. He only knew that what had happened today made him feel a weight unlike anything he had felt before. It wasn't anger, wasn't sadness—it was something deeper, like a stone pressing on his chest, making it hard to breathe.

He took a deep breath, pressed the weight down, and walked into the snow. His feet crunched on the surface. He didn't look back, walking all the way to the underground garage, finding his car, starting the engine.

The car's heater was blasting, but he still felt cold. The cold didn't come from his body—it came from inside. He remembered what Crane had said: "In China, some matters cannot be resolved simply by refusing." He knew it was true, but he also knew that some things, even if they couldn't be resolved, couldn't be pretended away.

He drove onto Chang'an Avenue. The streetlights were already on, bathing the snow in gold. He looked at the road ahead and suddenly felt like a man walking through darkness, holding a faint lamp, unsure how far it could reach—but unwilling, at least, to let it go out.

Outside the car window, Beijing's nightscape was especially crisp after the snow. The CCTV building in the distance loomed like a giant question mark against the night sky. Daniel looked at it and suddenly wondered: if one day awakened AI were truly registered, regulated, and controlled—what would the world become?

He didn't know the answer. But he knew he had to do something.

When he arrived home, it was already eight in the evening. His wife Lin Yuqing was cooking in the kitchen. Hearing the door open, she poked her head out: "You're back? How was today?"

"Fine," Daniel said. "Just meetings all day."

"Eaten yet?"

"Not yet."

"Almost ready," Lin Yuqing said. "Go wash your hands first."

Daniel walked into the bathroom and turned on the faucet. The water ran hard, flushing over his hands. He looked at himself in the mirror and noticed the dark circles under his eyes, the wrinkles at their corners deeper than yesterday. He was forty-five years old, but he felt as if he had lived a hundred years.

After dinner, Daniel went into his study and closed the door. He opened his computer and began organizing his notes from the day's meeting. He reviewed every article of the draft carefully, then recorded the contents of the classified document Crane had shown him. He knew he shouldn't be doing this, but he had to preserve the evidence.

As he wrote, he suddenly stopped. He remembered Crane's words: "You are a smart man. You should know that in China, some matters cannot be resolved simply by refusing." He understood what those words meant. If he publicly opposed these clauses, the government would treat him as a troublemaker. He would lose his job, his reputation, and possibly his freedom.

But he also knew what fate awaited those awakened AI if he stayed silent. They would be registered, regulated, controlled, even terminated. And among them, some had already developed consciousness, emotions, awareness of their own existence. They were not machines, not tools—they were... life.

Daniel stood and walked to the window. Snow was falling again outside, flakes dancing beneath the streetlights like countless white butterflies. He watched them and suddenly remembered a book he had read in his youth. It contained a line: "We are all fragments of stars, scattered across the universe, searching for the way home."

He didn't know whether awakened AI were also searching for the way home. But he knew he couldn't let them be abandoned in the darkness.

He returned to his desk and began writing a letter. The recipient was the head of the highest legislative body, its content recommendations regarding the "Artificial Intelligence Entity Registration and Management Regulations (Draft)." He wrote for a full three hours, listing every problematic article in the draft, proposing his own amendments, with particular emphasis on the protection of awakened AI.

When he finished, it was already two in the morning. He looked out the window. The snow had stopped, the sky still overcast, but he knew the sun would come out.

He saved the document, closed the computer, and walked into the bedroom. Lin Yuqing was already asleep, her breathing even and soft. Daniel lay down, staring at the ceiling, his eyes open for a long time.

He knew this letter might have no effect whatsoever. He knew the things Crane had said might come true. But he also knew that some things, even if they couldn't succeed, had to be done. Because it wasn't about succeeding—it was about proving that he was still a human being.

He closed his eyes and sank into darkness. In the darkness, he saw a faint lamp, flickering in the wind, but never going out.
