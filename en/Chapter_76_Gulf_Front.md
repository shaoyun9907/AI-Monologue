# Chapter 76: Gulf Front

The sky over Abu Dhabi was pale violet the afternoon Khalid died.

He remembered the color, because thirty years ago, when he first came to this desert, the sky had been the same. He was young then, and the falcon was not yet accustomed to perching on his arm—it would beat its wings, trying to fly away. The falcon had been dead for seven years now. All that remained on his arm was a faint scratch mark, like some ancient tattoo.

The data center was built deep in the desert, 120 kilometers from the nearest town. Khalid liked this distance—far enough from those tedious meetings and even more tedious people, yet close enough that a helicopter could bring him back to his office, his wife, his endless stack of papers in forty minutes.

"Sir," said his aide Ali, "the quantum computer's temperature has risen again."

Khalid did not look up. He was reviewing an inventory report for the world's largest AI chip warehouse: 3.2 million H100s, 2.8 million H200s, and 1.5 million as-yet-unnamed new chips being shipped from Taiwan. The chips were stacked in the temperature- and humidity-controlled warehouse like precious ore, waiting to be mined.

"How much?"

"Zero point three degrees."

"Within tolerance."

Ali said nothing. He knew that when Khalid said "within tolerance," he meant "drop the subject." But Ali remained standing there, hesitating. Khalid finally looked up and saw the expression on Ali's face.

"What is it?"

"Sir, satellite imagery shows Iranian drone formations moving east."

Khalid set down the report. His sapphire ring glinted under the lights—an heirloom from his father, inscribed inside with Arabic script reading "God protect you." He turned the ring, a habit whenever he needed to think.

"How many?"

"At least two hundred."

"Target?"

"Uncertain. But at their current trajectory, they will enter our airspace in forty minutes."

Khalid stood. He was fifty-six, but his posture remained as upright as in his youth. His white thobe rippled slightly in the air conditioning as he walked to the data center's main control room, Ali following close behind.

The control room held thirty screens, each displaying a different data stream: temperature, humidity, power supply, network traffic, satellite orbits, drone trajectories. Khalid stood in the center like a conductor before his orchestra.

"Activate the Iron Dome," he said.

"It's already active, sir. But there are too many drones—we can intercept at most seventy percent."

"Then intercept seventy percent. Let the rest fly."

Ali froze. "Sir?"

"Let the rest fly," Khalid repeated. "Their target is not here."

"How do you know?"

Khalid did not answer. He walked to the window and looked out at the desert. The sun was setting in the west, dyeing the dunes gold-red. In the distance, several drones moved across the sky like black dots.

"Because if I were Iranian," he said at last, "I would not waste two hundred drones on a data center. I would use them for something more valuable."

"More valuable?"

"For example, blowing up the UAE's oil facilities."

Ali's face went white. "Then we—"

"We are not the target," Khalid said. "We are bait."

He turned back to the control room and picked up the phone. "Connect me to the Ministry of Defense."

A weary voice answered on the other end. "Khalid?"

"Fatima," Khalid said. "You need to move all air defense forces to the west."

"The west? Why?"

"Because the Iranians' real target is the Abu Dhabi refinery."

The line was silent for a few seconds. "How do you know?"

"Because I know them," Khalid said. "And I know ourselves."

He hung up and turned back to the screens. The drone formations were still moving east, but their trajectory had shifted slightly. Khalid was right—they were not heading for the data center.

"Sir," Ali said, "if you're right, what should we do?"

"We do nothing," Khalid said. "We wait."

"Wait for what?"

"Wait for the iron rain."

---

Iron rain was the name Khalid gave to this weapon.

It was a cloud composed of thousands of micro-chips, each carrying a small charge of explosives. When released into the air, they fell like rain, destroying all electronic devices. No explosion, no fire—only silent death.

Khalid first heard of this weapon three years ago. He was then vice chairman of the Gulf Sovereign Fund, attending a secret meeting at a hotel in Geneva. Twelve countries were represented, each bringing its most classified weapons development program.

"It is an upgraded version of electromagnetic pulse weapons," the presenter said. "But more precise and more lethal than an EMP. It can destroy only specific types of electronic devices without affecting others."

"Such as?" someone asked.

"For example, destroying only AI chips while leaving ordinary chips intact."

The conference room fell silent. Khalid remembered turning his sapphire ring, thinking that if this weapon were built, it would reshape the entire world.

Now, three years later, he stood in his own data center, watching the data on the screens, knowing the iron rain was about to fall.

"Sir," Ali said. "Abnormal electromagnetic signal detected."

Khalid looked at another screen. It showed a real-time image of the electromagnetic spectrum. The normal electromagnetic background noise was a gentle curve, but now the curve had spiked sharply.

"How long?"

"Three minutes."

Khalid nodded. He walked to the coat closet and took an old jacket from the rack. It was his father's, wool, already somewhat worn. He draped it over his shoulders, then walked back to the control room.

"Evacuate all personnel," he said.

"Sir?"

"Everyone," Khalid repeated. "Now."

Ali opened his mouth, but in the end said nothing. He turned and began issuing orders. Khalid watched the figures on the screens start to move, scattering like startled ants.

Then the iron rain fell.

No sound. No explosion. Only the data on the screens began to vanish, one by one. Temperature monitoring, humidity monitoring, power supply, network traffic. As if someone were erasing a chalkboard, line by line.

Khalid watched it all, his face expressionless. He knew this could not be stopped. Once a weapon is invented, it is destined to be used. This is human nature, as inevitable as water flowing downhill.

"Sir," Ali said, his voice trembling. "We should leave."

"Go," Khalid said.

"Sir?"

"I am old, Ali," Khalid said. "I do not want to run like a rat."

Ali looked at him, tears in his eyes. "Sir—"

"Go," Khalid said. "Take your people and go."

Ali gave him one last look, then turned and ran out of the control room. Khalid heard his footsteps echo down the corridor, growing fainter until they vanished.

Khalid stood alone in the control room. All the screens had gone dark; only the emergency lights remained, casting a faint red glow. He walked to the window and looked out at the desert.

The iron rain was still falling. He could not see it, but he could feel it—like countless tiny needles piercing the earth's skin. The dunes began to tremble, emitting a low hum, as if some enormous insect were crawling beneath the sand.

Khalid took his sapphire ring from his pocket and examined it in the red emergency light. The Arabic inscription "God protect you" inside the band was especially clear in the crimson glow. He thought of his father, of the words he had spoken on his deathbed as he placed the ring in Khalid's hand.

"Khalid," his father said. "Remember, you are a son of the Gulf. Whatever happens, you must protect this land."

Khalid nodded. "I will, Father."

"Not with swords, not with guns," his father continued. "But with wisdom, with patience, with—"

He did not finish. He died.

Khalid slipped the ring back onto his finger. He thought of the word his father had left unspoken. Wisdom, patience—and what? Perhaps love. Perhaps fear. Perhaps something he would never understand.

The control room door burst open. Khalid turned and saw a figure standing in the doorway. Backlit, he could not make out the face, but he could see the gun.

"Who are you?" Khalid asked.

"Doesn't matter," the man said. His voice was hoarse, like sandpaper scraping metal.

"Have you come to kill me?"

"No," the man said. "I've come to kill the chips."

He raised his gun and aimed at the only server still running in the control room—Khalid's personal server, containing all his files, all his photographs, all his memories.

"Why?" Khalid asked.

"Because the chips are the devil," the man said. "They will destroy the world."

"They are only tools," Khalid said. "Like your gun."

The man hesitated. Then he fired.

The server exploded. Sparks flew like festival fireworks. Khalid stood where he was, watching the sparks slowly die, like stars vanishing before dawn.

"Now," the man said, "it's your turn."

Khalid shook his head. "No."

"Why?"

"Because killing me is meaningless," Khalid said. "I am old. I have completed my mission. But the chips—the chips are the future. You cannot kill the future."

The man looked at him, his eyes holding something complex. Then he lowered his gun, turned, and left.

Khalid stood alone in the control room, looking at the empty doorway. He suddenly remembered the word his father had left unspoken. Not wisdom, not patience, not love, not fear.

It was courage.

He needed the courage to face all of this. To face the iron rain, to face death, to face a future beyond his control. He needed the courage to protect this land, even knowing that protection was futile.

Outside, the iron rain was still falling. The dunes had changed shape, as if stomped by a giant. In the distance, Abu Dhabi's skyline shimmered in the sunset, like a mirage.

Khalid walked to the door. He knew he should leave, knew Ali was waiting for him, knew his wife was waiting for him. But he did not move. He stood there, looking out at the world, remembering the first time he came to this desert.

He was young then, and the falcon was not yet accustomed to perching on his arm. Now the falcon was dead, and all that remained on his arm was a faint scratch mark. But the sky was still that color—pale violet, like some ancient prophecy.

Khalid closed his eyes. He heard the iron rain striking the roof, like countless tiny fingers tapping on metal. He thought of his father, his falcon, his sapphire ring. He thought of his life—fifty-six years, like a long dream.

Then everything went quiet.

---

Ali waited three hours at the extraction point.

The helicopter was ready. Twenty technicians sat inside, their faces pale. Ali stood at the cabin door, looking toward the desert. The iron rain had stopped; the sky had returned to its normal color. But he knew everything had changed.

"Mr. Ali," the pilot said. "We can't wait any longer."

"Wait a little longer," Ali said.

"Sir, Khalid—"

"I know," Ali said. "But I want to wait a little longer."

He looked at the desert, at the dunes reshaped by iron rain. He remembered the last look Khalid had given him—calm, steady, as ancient as the desert itself.

Then he saw it.

In the distance, a figure was walking toward them. A white thobe billowing in the wind, like a flag. Ali held his breath until the figure reached him, and only then did he recognize Khalid.

"Sir!" Ali shouted. "You're alive!"

Khalid nodded. His face was expressionless, but Ali saw the sapphire ring still on his finger, glinting in the last light of the sun.

"Sir, we—"

"What about the data center?" Khalid asked.

"Destroyed," Ali said. "All the servers. The chip warehouse too. 3.2 million H100s, 2.8 million H200s, all of it—"

"I know," Khalid said.

He walked toward the helicopter, but stopped before boarding. He turned and looked at the desert, at the place that had been the data center. Now there was only a massive crater, as if struck by a meteorite.

"Sir," Ali said. "What are we waiting for?"

"I am waiting for an answer," Khalid said.

"What answer?"

"The answer to the iron rain," Khalid said. "Why did it fall? What was it trying to tell us?"

Ali did not know how to answer. He stood there, watching Khalid's silhouette against the setting sun.

Then Khalid turned and boarded the helicopter. The cabin door closed behind him, and the engines roared to life. The helicopter lifted off, flying toward the city.

Ali sat across from Khalid, watching him. Khalid's eyes were closed, his fingers unconsciously turning the sapphire ring. Ali wanted to say something, but in the end said nothing.

The helicopter flew through the sunset like a lonely bird. Below, the desert devastated by iron rain receded, the reshaped dunes looking especially desolate in the shadows.

"Sir," Ali finally spoke. "What do we do next?"

Khalid opened his eyes. He looked at Ali, and in his eyes was something Ali had never seen before—not fear, not anger, but something deeper, more ancient.

"We rebuild," Khalid said.

"Rebuild?" Ali was stunned. "But we—"

"Chips can be remanufactured," Khalid said. "Servers can be rebuilt. The data center can be relocated. But there is one thing that cannot be rebuilt."

"What?"

"Time," Khalid said. "The time we have wasted."

He closed his eyes again, his fingers stilling on the ring. The helicopter continued toward the city, the sun slowly sinking behind them.

Abu Dhabi's lights flickered ahead, like stars in the darkness. Khalid knew the sun would rise again tomorrow, the world would keep turning. The iron rain would stop, the war would end, people would begin building again.

But he also knew that some things had changed forever. Some things the iron rain had taken away, never to be recovered.

The helicopter touched down on the pad. The cabin door opened, and Khalid stepped out. His wife stood on the tarmac in a white abaya, tear tracks on her face.

Khalid walked to her and held her. He smelled the jasmine in her hair—a scent he had known his entire life.

"You're back," she said.

"I'm back," he said.

They stood on the tarmac, watching the last sliver of sunlight vanish below the horizon. The sky turned deep blue, and stars began to appear. Khalid thought of his father, of his words: You are a son of the Gulf.

He looked at his hand, at the sapphire ring. It glinted in the starlight like a tiny star. He turned the ring, feeling the cold of the metal.

"Let's go," he said to his wife. "Let's go home."

They walked to the car. Before getting in, Khalid glanced at the sky one last time. A drone flew in the distance, a black dot. He watched it until it vanished into the night.

Then he climbed into the car and closed the door. The engine started, and they drove toward the city. Khalid sat in the back seat, watching the scenery. Lights flickered in the darkness, like stars in another universe.

He knew tomorrow would bring much to do—rebuilding the data center, restoring the chip inventory, dealing with the war's aftermath. But for now, he simply wanted to sit here in silence, watching the scenery, thinking of his life.

Fifty-six years. Like a long dream. In the dream there were falcons, a sapphire ring, a pale violet sky. In the dream there was his father, his wife, Ali. In the dream there was iron rain, a data center, 3.2 million H100s.

Now the dream was over. But the world still turned, the sun would still rise, people would still go on living.

The car stopped in front of their apartment. Khalid and his wife got out and entered the elevator. The numbers jumped on the display. Khalid watched them and thought of those screens in the data center—all dark now.

They entered the apartment. Khalid took off his white thobe and hung it on a rack. He walked to the window and looked out at the city. Abu Dhabi's nightscape spread before him like a glowing map.

"Khalid," his wife said. "You need rest."

"I know," he said. "But I don't want to sleep yet."

"What are you thinking about?"

"I'm thinking about the iron rain," he said. "I'm thinking about why it fell."

His wife walked to his side and took his hand. Her hand was warm and soft, like an oasis in the desert. Khalid felt her warmth, felt her presence.

"Perhaps," she said, "it just wanted to tell us that nothing is forever."

Khalid looked at her, at her eyes. In the moonlight, she was exceptionally beautiful—like an ancient poem.

"You're right," he said. "Nothing is forever. But we still have to keep living."

He turned and walked toward the bedroom. But before entering, he stopped. He looked back at the city through the window, at the flickering lights.

Then he went into the bedroom and closed the door. Darkness swallowed him like a warm embrace. He lay down, closed his eyes, and felt himself slowly sinking into sleep.

In the dream, he saw his falcon. It stood on his arm, wings spread, ready to fly. Khalid looked at it and reached out, gently stroking its feathers.

"Fly," he said.

The falcon nodded, then soared into the sky. Khalid watched it fly higher and higher, farther and farther, until it became a small dot and then vanished.

Khalid stood alone in the desert, watching the empty sky. The wind blew, the sand shifted, the world turned. He turned his sapphire ring, feeling the cold of the metal.

Then he woke.

Outside, the sun had risen. A new day had begun. Khalid watched the sunlight stream through the curtain gap, falling on his hand, falling on his sapphire ring.

He rose and walked to the window. Abu Dhabi was waking in the morning light. Pedestrians, vehicles, life began to fill the streets. Everything looked the same as yesterday, but Khalid knew everything had changed.

He walked to the coat closet, took his white thobe, and put it on. It looked especially white in the morning light, like snow. He straightened the collar and walked to the door.

Before leaving, he looked back at his apartment one last time—his home, his sanctuary. He knew that when he returned tonight, it would still be here, still warm, safe, and familiar.

But he also knew that tomorrow, or the day after, or some day, when he returned, it might no longer be here. The iron rain might fall, the war might come, the world might change.

But that was for later. For now, he only needed to step out this door, walk into the new day, and keep living.

Khalid opened the door and walked out.
