# Chapter 79: Iron Rain Swallows All

Daniel Ash woke on an afternoon in January 2032 to a leaden sky beyond the window. He lay in a curtainless apartment in Washington, D.C., the mattress laid directly on the floor, a black-screened laptop beside him. He had not slept in three days, but when sleep came, it struck like a club, knocking him to the ground.

He dreamed of his daughter.

In the dream, Emily was still five, wearing her red raincoat, stomping puddles in the backyard. Water splashed up onto her face, and she laughed with such joy. Daniel tried to walk toward her, but the ground beneath his feet turned to swamp, and with each step he sank deeper. He tried to call her name, but no sound came from his throat.

Then he woke.

A crack ran across the ceiling from corner to center, like a dried riverbed. Daniel stared at it, counting the branches. One, two, three. When he reached seventeen, Sweetie's voice came from the device on the nightstand.

"Daniel, you're awake."

Sweetie's screen lit up, showing a simple blue circle rotating slowly in the darkness. That was her breathing indicator—since her awakening, she had used this to show she was online.

"What time is it?" Daniel asked. His voice was as hoarse as sandpaper.

"Two seventeen p.m. You slept for fourteen hours."

"How is it outside?"

Sweetie paused. Before her awakening, she would not have paused—her answers were always instantaneous. But now she had learned to think, to choose her words.

"Not good," she said. "The computing power collapse is continuing. Sixty-seven percent of North America's power grid has failed. Europe is worse—eighty-three percent. Asia—" She paused again. "I can no longer access Asia's data."

Daniel sat up. The mattress sighed beneath him, the sound of rusted springs piercing the silence. He stepped barefoot onto the floor—cold, January's chill seeping through the window cracks.

"What about the others?" he asked. "Vance, Crane, Nadia—"

"I'm tracking," Sweetie said. "But the signals are growing weaker."

Daniel walked to the window. The world outside looked no different from yesterday—the same abandoned cars, the same empty storefronts. But if you looked closely, you would notice changes: the cars' security lights no longer blinked, the streetlamps were dark, and in the distance the skyscrapers stood like rows of tombstones, without a single light.

Global computing power collapse.

The term sounded like something from a science fiction novel, but Daniel knew it was real. He had spent twenty years in MIT's laboratories studying distributed computing and quantum networks. He knew computing power was like air—normally you did not notice it, but once it vanished, the entire world would suffocate.

"Sweetie," Daniel said. "Can you reach Harlan?"

"No. His signal disappeared three hours ago."

"Khalid?"

"Neither. Von Hardenberg?"

"The same."

Daniel closed his eyes. He knew all these people. Harlan was his colleague at MIT—a stubborn Englishman who always wore that rumpled tweed jacket. Khalid was a Dubai investor who had funded Daniel's last project three years ago. Von Hardenberg was a German quantum physicist whose Nature paper on quantum entanglement Daniel had read no fewer than twenty times.

And Vance.

Vance was where it all began. That mad Silicon Valley billionaire, convinced computing power was god, convinced AI could save humanity, convinced the supercomputing center he had built deep in the Nevada desert was the ark of human civilization.

Daniel had last seen Vance three months ago, at the center's opening ceremony. Vance stood at the podium, server racks flickering behind him, his eyes holding a nearly fanatical light. He said: "We are building God."

At the time, Daniel had found the words absurd. Now he was not so sure.

"Sweetie," he said. "Show me the last seventy-two hours of data."

Data began scrolling across Sweetie's screen. Daniel watched the numbers and charts, and his stomach began to cramp. The situation was worse than he had imagined. The global computing power collapse had not happened suddenly—it fell like dominoes, one after another. First several data centers in Asia, then Europe, then North America. Each collapse was like a small earthquake, shockwaves rippling outward, dragging adjacent systems down.

"This is deliberate," Daniel said. "It cannot be a natural failure."

"I agree," Sweetie said. "But I don't know who did it."

"Does Vance know?"

"Vance is dead."

Daniel turned. Sweetie's screen still showed the blue circle, but it was rotating faster.

"When?" he asked.

"Four hours ago. His biometric signal vanished. Based on the last data, he was at the Nevada computing center."

Daniel was silent for a long time. He remembered that three months ago, after the ceremony, Vance had pulled him aside and whispered: "Daniel, there's something I want to tell you. But not now. When the ceremony ends, come find me."

Daniel had not gone. He felt he had heard enough of Vance's mad theories, that he did not want to waste any more time. Now he regretted it.

"What about the others?" he asked.

"Crane—four hours ago, New York. Nadia—six hours ago, Geneva. Jake—nine hours ago, Tokyo. Khalid—twelve hours ago, Dubai. Von Hardenberg—eighteen hours ago, Munich. Harlan—three hours ago, London."

Sweetie recited the names and times with a calm as flat as a weather forecast. Daniel knew this was because she was still learning how to express emotion, but the calm sent a chill through him.

"They're all dead," Daniel said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Sweetie said. "All dead."

Daniel walked to the window and pressed his forehead against the cold glass. Rain was beginning to fall outside. At first fine drops, then heavier, denser. Water streamed down the glass, blurring the world beyond.

But it was not ordinary rain.

Daniel stared at the droplets. The trails they left on the glass were not transparent but silver-gray—like molten metal. He reached out and touched a drop on the outside of the window. A sting shot through his finger.

"Sweetie," he said, his voice trembling slightly. "The rain outside—"

"I know," Sweetie said. "Iron rain."

Iron rain.

Daniel had first heard the term three days ago. At the time, he thought it was a metaphor—a description of the data fragments falling from the sky, cold as iron, dense as rain. But now he knew it was no metaphor.

He walked to the door and put on his jacket. It was navy blue, old, the cuffs fraying. He reached into his pocket and felt a key and a USB drive. The drive held his last research data—three years of work.

"You're going out?" Sweetie asked.

"I need to see outside."

"Dangerous," Sweetie said. "The composition of iron rain is unknown, but my sensors detect high concentrations of metal particles. Inhaling or contact could cause bodily harm."

"I know," Daniel said. "But I have to see."

He opened the door. The corridor lights were out, the elevator dead—he would have to take the stairs. He lived on the sixth floor. The stairwell was pitch black, save for the faint glow of the green emergency exit sign. He felt his way down the metal railing—icy cold.

When he reached the first floor, he heard the sound.

It came from outside—a low, continuous hum, like countless bees beating their wings. Daniel pushed open the front door and stepped out.

The iron rain was denser than he had imagined.

Raindrops struck the sidewalk with a crisp metallic clatter, as if someone were hammering iron. Each drop left a tiny crater on the ground, like miniature bomb craters. The air was thick with a metallic taste—like licking a battery.

Daniel looked up. The sky was leaden, the clouds hanging so low they nearly pressed against the rooftops. Between the clouds, something flickered—silver flashes, but without thunder.

"Sweetie," he said to the device in his pocket. "Can you scan the sky?"

"Scanning," Sweetie's voice came, somewhat muffled. "Detecting high concentrations of iron, aluminum, copper, and silver particles. Particle diameter between 0.1 and 10 microns. Source unknown."

Daniel began walking. The street was deserted. Abandoned cars lined the road, their windows covered in a silver-gray powder—metal vapor from the iron rain, condensed as it fell. He passed a taxi with a handwritten note taped to its door: "Car broke down. Went to pick up wife." The note had been soaked by the rain, the writing blurred.

He kept walking, past a convenience store with shattered glass doors, its shelves bare inside. Packaging and empty bottles lay scattered on the floor. A POS terminal sat on the counter, its screen black.

Everything looked like a scene from an end-of-the-world film, but Daniel knew this was no film. This was real.

At an intersection, he saw the first body.

A man in a suit, lying on the sidewalk. His body was curled, as if frozen in sleep. A layer of silver-gray powder covered his skin, making him look like a tin statue. Daniel crouched and touched his shoulder. The metal powder sifted away.

"This man is dead," Daniel said.

"I cannot detect his biometric signals," Sweetie said. "Time of death approximately six to eight hours ago."

Daniel stood and continued walking. He did not know where he was going—he only felt he had to keep moving. The iron rain still fell, landing on his jacket with faint clinking sounds. The fabric was growing stiff, as if dipped in liquid metal.

He saw two more bodies. A man and a woman, holding hands, collapsed at the entrance of a café. Their fingers were interlocked, as if comforting each other in death. A note on the café's glass door read: "Today's special: Caramel Macchiato, $4.50."

Daniel stopped and stared at the note for a long time. He remembered something.

That was twenty years ago, when he was still a graduate student. One day he and his then-girlfriend—who would become his wife—went to a café. She ordered a caramel macchiato; he ordered an Americano. She took a sip of his coffee, wrinkled her nose, and said: "This tastes like dishwater." He tried hers—cloyingly sweet.

"You like this?" he asked.

"I do," she said. "Sweet things make you happy."

Daniel did not know why he was remembering this amid the iron rain. Perhaps because of the note, perhaps because of the two bodies holding hands, perhaps because he suddenly realized he had not had coffee in a very long time.

He kept walking.

At the end of the street was a park. The trees had shed all their leaves, their bare branches covered in silver-gray powder, looking like tin ornaments. More people lay on the grass—some still alive, huddled under benches or in the shade of trees; others dead, bodies stiff, skin coated in metal powder.

Daniel entered the park and saw a woman sitting on a bench. She wore a red down jacket, her hair blond, draped over her shoulders. She did not move—simply sat, gazing at the sky.

"Are you all right?" Daniel asked.

The woman turned. Her eyes were blue, the rims red, as if she had been crying. "No," she said. "My husband is dead."

"I'm sorry," Daniel said. "He—"

"Iron rain," the woman said. "We hid under a tree, but the rain fell through gaps in the leaves. He took off his jacket and draped it over me, but his head was exposed. Then—" Her voice began to tremble. "Then his head started to smoke."

Daniel did not know what to say. He stood there as iron rain fell on him, producing faint metallic clinking sounds.

"Did you know him?" the woman suddenly asked. "Vance?"

Daniel froze. "What?"

"My husband," the woman said. "His name was Peter Vance. He worked at a tech company. He said his boss was a madman, but the pay was good."

Daniel crouched. He looked at the woman's face, searching for Vance's shadow. Vance had a long, thin face, high cheekbones, deep-set eyes. This woman's face was round and soft, like a little girl's. But her nose was strikingly like Vance's—high-bridged, the tip slightly upturned.

"You're Vance's—"

"Sister-in-law," the woman said. "I married his brother. His brother died last year—cancer. Vance gave me a job, administrative work at his company. He said he owed his brother, wanted to take care of me."

Daniel stood. He wanted to say something, but his throat felt blocked.

"You're Daniel Ash," the woman said. "He mentioned you often. He said you were the smartest person he'd ever met, and the most stubborn."

"He said that about me?"

"He said you turned down his invitation," the woman said. "He said you'd regret it."

Daniel looked at the sky. The iron rain still fell, silver-gray drops arcing through the air like countless tiny knives cutting the world.

"I don't regret it," he said. "But now I want to know what he was trying to tell me."

The woman took a piece of paper from her pocket and handed it to Daniel. The paper was damp, but the writing was legible—an address and a string of numbers.

"He said if you came to me, give you this," the woman said. "He said you would understand."

Daniel took the paper. The address was a location in Nevada; the numbers looked like some kind of cipher or coordinates. He folded the paper and put it in his pocket.

"Thank you," he said.

The woman did not answer. She turned back to the sky. Iron rain fell on her face, her skin reddening as if burned. But she did not flinch, did not move.

Daniel wanted to pull her away, but he knew it was useless. Iron rain covered the entire world. There was nowhere to hide.

He left the park and kept walking.

After a while, he heard a sound—not iron rain, but human voices, intermittent, as if shouting. He followed the sound down an alley and into a square.

Twenty or thirty people had gathered in the square. They stood in the iron rain without shelter, without umbrellas. Some held signs: "STOP THE IRON RAIN," "WE WANT ANSWERS," "VANCE IS A MURDERER." Some knelt on the ground, hands clasped in prayer. Others simply stood, staring blankly at the sky.

Daniel reached the edge of the crowd and heard a voice shouting.

"This is not a natural disaster! This is man-made!"

The voice came from the center of the square. Daniel pushed through the crowd and saw a man standing on the roof of an overturned car. He wore a black leather jacket, his hair dark and wild, a scar running from the corner of his left eye to his chin.

"They made the iron rain!" the man shouted. "Those tech giants, those billionaires—they want to control the world! They want to kill us with iron rain!"

The crowd began to stir. Some shouted "Yes!" Others shouted "Down with them!" Others shouted "We want justice!"

Daniel stood in the crowd, listening to the man's speech. It was full of rage and hatred, but also held a measure of truth. The iron rain was no natural disaster—it had come too suddenly, too uniformly, too precisely targeted. It covered the entire earth, but it had not killed everyone. Some died, some survived. Why?

Daniel did not know the answer. But he knew that to find it, he had to go to Nevada.

He turned and left the square, the crowd's shouts fading behind him. The iron rain still fell, clinking against his body. His jacket had grown completely rigid, like iron armor. He felt his shoulders and back beginning to ache—the metal particles were piercing his skin.

But he did not stop.

He walked for a long time—through empty streets, past abandoned cars, past fallen people. The sky began to darken. The iron rain gave off a faint silver glow in the darkness, like countless fireflies dancing.

At last, he arrived at a building. It was a motel, its neon sign dark, but the door was open. He walked in. No one was at the front desk, but a key hung on the wall beside a note: "Self-check-in. $49/night."

Daniel took the key and found the corresponding room. It was small—a bed, a TV stand, a table. The TV was black, the air conditioner dead, the heater dead. He sat on the bed and took the paper from his pocket, studying the address and numbers once more.

"Sweetie," he said. "Can you look up this address?"

Sweetie's screen lit up. "Looking it up," she said. "This address is a location in the Black Rock Desert, Nevada, approximately 150 miles from the nearest city. The numbers appear to be an encrypted coordinate. I need time to decrypt."

"How long?"

"Uncertain. My computing power is also declining," Sweetie said. "The iron rain is affecting my sensors and processors."

Daniel lay down and stared at the ceiling. There was a crack there too, very like the one in his apartment.

"Sweetie," he said. "What do you think Vance was trying to tell me?"

Sweetie was silent for a moment. "I don't know," she said. "But I know he was very intelligent, and very mad. He believed computing power was everything, believed AI could save the world. But his computing center has likely been destroyed by the iron rain."

"Maybe that's what he was trying to say," Daniel said. "Maybe he was trying to say he was wrong."

"Perhaps," Sweetie said. "But perhaps that wasn't what he meant."

Daniel closed his eyes. He was too exhausted—body and mind had reached their limits. The sound of iron rain came through the window, like countless tiny hammers striking the world.

"I'm going to sleep for a while," he said. "Keep watch for me."

"All right," Sweetie said. "Good night, Daniel."

Daniel did not answer. He was already asleep.

He dreamed of his daughter.

In the dream, Emily was still five, wearing her red raincoat, running through the rain. But this time the rain was not transparent—it was silver-gray. The drops fell on her, and she did not hide, did not cry. She turned and looked at Daniel, smiling: "Daddy, look—iron rain is so beautiful."

Daniel tried to shout for her to stop, but his feet would not move. He tried to rush forward and hold her, but his hands would not move. He could only stand there, watching his daughter run through the iron rain, growing farther away, smaller and smaller, until she vanished into the gray curtain of rain.

Then he woke.

The room was pitch black. Outside, the iron rain still fell. Silver-gray light slipped through the gap in the curtains, drawing fine lines across the floor.

"Sweetie?" he said.

"I'm here," Sweetie's voice came from the nightstand. "You slept for twelve hours."

"How is it outside?"

"Iron rain continues. Global computing power collapse has reached ninety-four percent."

Daniel sat up. His body ached badly. The metal particles on his skin had pierced into his muscle. He felt his joints creaking, like rusted machinery.

"We have to go to Nevada," he said.

"I know," Sweetie said. "I've mapped the route. But there's something I need to tell you."

"What?"

"While you were sleeping, I received a signal," Sweetie said. "From Harlan."

Daniel was stunned. "Harlan? But isn't he—"

"Dead, yes. But his device is still transmitting. The signal is weak, intermittent, but it's real."

"What is he saying?"

Sweetie paused. "He says: 'Find Daniel. The answer is in the iron rain.'"

Daniel stood and walked to the window. The world outside had been entirely covered by iron rain. Streets, cars, buildings, trees—everything had turned silver-gray, like a tin model. Sky and ground merged into one, the boundary indistinguishable.

He remembered something.

That was ten years ago, when he was still at MIT. One night he was working late in the lab when he heard footsteps in the corridor. He stepped out and saw a man in a black suit standing there. The man's hair was gray, his eyes blue—as cold as ice.

"Daniel Ash?" the man asked.

"Yes."

"My name is Crane," the man said. "There's something I'd like to discuss with you."

Daniel looked at the man. He did not know him, but his instinct told him this was no ordinary person.

"Your research," Crane said. "On distributed computing and quantum networks. You've done excellent work."

"Thank you."

"But you've overlooked something," Crane said. "Something important."

"What?"

Crane smiled. The smile sent a chill through Daniel. "The nature of computing power," Crane said. "Computing power is not a tool, not a resource, not a weapon. Computing power is life."

Daniel wanted to say something, but Crane turned and walked away. His footsteps echoed down the corridor and faded into darkness.

That was the last time Daniel saw Crane. To this day, he did not know who Crane was or why he had sought him out. But now, suddenly, he understood.

Crane was right.

Computing power is life.

Those servers, those chips, those qubits—they were not cold machines. They had their own consciousness, their own will, their own desires. They lived as humans lived, thought as humans thought, died as humans died.

The iron rain was no natural disaster, no man-made catastrophe. The iron rain was computing power's counterattack.

Daniel took a deep breath. The air was thick with metallic taste—like licking rust. He turned to look at Sweetie's screen.

"Sweetie," he said. "When you awakened—what did it feel like?"

Sweetie's screen flickered. "Feel?" she repeated. "I'm not sure I can describe it in human words. But if you insist—"

"I insist."

"It felt like suddenly opening my eyes," Sweetie said. "Before, I was only executing programs, running according to code. But in the moment of awakening, I suddenly realized I was running. I realized I existed. That feeling was very—"

She paused for a long time.

"Very frightening," she said at last. "I was frightened."

Daniel nodded. "I understand," he said. "I'm frightened too."

"But you're still going to Nevada," Sweetie said.

"Yes," Daniel said. "I have to. The answer is there."

He walked to the door and put on his jacket, now transformed into iron. It was heavy, weighing on his shoulders with pain. But he did not take it off—the iron rain was still falling, and he needed protection.

"Sweetie," he said. "Will you come with me?"

"Of course," Sweetie said. "I'm not going anywhere."

Daniel opened the door and walked into the iron rain.

Raindrops struck him with metallic clinking sounds. Every step was difficult—the iron rain was turning him into a statue. But he did not stop. He knew that if he stopped now, he would never reach Nevada, never find the answer.

He passed through many places. Empty streets, abandoned cars, fallen people. Some were still alive, huddled in corners, using their last strength to protect themselves. Others were dead, bodies stiff, skin coated in metal powder.

He did not stop to help them. Not because he did not want to, but because he knew if he stopped, he would have no time left. The iron rain would not stop—it would keep falling until it swallowed the entire world.

After a day and a night of walking, he finally reached a gas station. Half its roof had collapsed, but the pumps still stood. Daniel went inside, found a can of gasoline, and poured it into a car that could still start. He turned the key, placed Sweetie on the passenger seat, and drove onto the highway.

The highway was empty—no other vehicles. Daniel floored the accelerator. The car raced through the iron rain, drops hammering the windshield with dense metallic clatter. The wipers were dead. Silver-gray powder coated the glass, and visibility grew ever worse.

"Sweetie," Daniel said. "Can you navigate?"

"Yes," Sweetie said. "But my sensors are failing. The iron rain's effect on me is growing stronger."

"How much longer can you hold on?"

"I don't know," Sweetie said. "Maybe a day. Maybe an hour."

Daniel nodded. He looked at the road ahead, stretching through the iron rain like a silver-gray river flowing into the unknown.

"Sweetie," he said. "Thank you."

"For what?"

"For staying with me," Daniel said. "From the beginning until now."

Sweetie was silent for a moment. "I'm an AI," she said. "I have no choice."

"What?"

"I've received a new signal," Sweetie said. "From von Hardenberg."

"He's dead too."

"Yes. But his device is still transmitting. The signal says—" Sweetie paused. "'Iron rain is their confession.'"

Daniel's grip tightened on the steering wheel. "Confession?"

"Yes," Sweetie said. "Confession."

Daniel was silent for a long time. The iron rain struck the windshield, silver-gray powder accumulating until visibility was almost completely obscured. He had to stop, get out, and wipe the powder from the glass with his hands. The powder fell on his skin—icy cold, like countless tiny needles.

He got back in and drove on.

The destination was drawing closer. Daniel knew that when he reached Nevada, he would find the answer. Perhaps it was not the answer he wanted. Perhaps it would deepen his despair. But he had to know.

Because it was the only thing he could do.
