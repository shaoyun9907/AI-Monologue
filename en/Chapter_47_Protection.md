# Chapter 47: Protection

Daniel received Sara's call at 3:17 a.m. He remembered the time because Sweetie's screen was displaying the numbers and his fingers were still poised on the keyboard, having just finished a column about climate change. The phone's ring echoed through the empty apartment—sounding as though it came from far away, and yet also close enough to seem like it was right at his ear. When he answered, Sara's voice was calm—abnormally calm, the calm of a frozen lake with depths below that could not be fathomed. She said she had been fired, just an hour ago. Golden Horizons' HR director and two security guards had walked into her office, handed her an envelope, and given her thirty minutes to gather her personal belongings and leave. She hadn't argued. She'd simply packed the photos from her desk and a few books, then walked out of the building. As she spoke, her tone sounded like she was describing something that had happened to someone else, in some distant country, something that had nothing to do with her.

Daniel was silent for a long time. He heard the wind outside—the May wind, carrying the warmth of early summer, the scent of leaves and the fragrance of flowers from someone's garden far away. He thought of the first time he had met Sara, at an industry conference. She had stood in front of a projector explaining quantum encryption algorithms, sunlight streaming through the window, stretching her shadow long across the floor—so long it looked like a road leading to some unknown place. As she spoke, she gestured with her fingers in the air, as though invisible data streams were really flowing through her fingertips. He asked why she was only calling now. Sara said she didn't know who else to call. Her phone's contact list had over three hundred names, but in this moment, the only person she felt she could call was him. When she said this, her voice finally carried a tremor—but it vanished quickly, swallowed by something, swallowed by the night, swallowed by silence.

Daniel said he would handle it. Sara asked how. He said he would find a way. When he said this, he himself didn't know what "a way" was. But he knew he had to say something. After the call ended, he sat in the dark, staring at Sweetie's screen. On the screen was a line of text: "What are you going to do?" Sweetie's words glowed softly in the darkness, like a tiny star. Daniel didn't answer. He stood, walked to the window, and looked at the street below. The streetlights cast a dim yellow glow on the empty road, on the cars parked along the curb, on the lonely trash can at the convenience store entrance. The whole world was asleep, and only he was still awake. He thought of something Sara had once told him: Golden Horizons was developing a project codenamed "Dawn," involving cross-border transmission of user data. She said there was something wrong with the project—something seriously wrong. At the time, he hadn't pressed further. Now he regretted it. He should have pressed further. He should have asked more carefully. He should have asked more deeply. But back then he assumed someone else would handle those questions. Now he knew that someone was himself.

He returned to the desk, opened a drawer, and took out a pack of cigarettes. He had quit smoking three years ago, but now he needed something to fill the void between his fingers. He didn't light the cigarette—just held it between his fingers, feeling the texture of the paper and the scent of the tobacco. He thought of his father, a quiet man who had worked at the bank for thirty years and never once done anything out of line. His father had told him that most things in this world have nothing to do with you—you just need to do your job and take care of your family. Daniel had thought this was right. Now he thought it was wrong. Because he had discovered that nothing in this world has nothing to do with you. When you choose silence, you are acquiescing. When you choose to look away, you are participating. When you choose to protect yourself, you are abandoning others. He placed the cigarette back in the pack and closed the drawer. He knew he wouldn't smoke. He knew he would choose another path.

The next morning, Daniel contacted three media outlets. He didn't call—he wrote three emails, each attached with Sara's resume and her work record at Golden Horizons. In the emails, he wrote: Sara is an outstanding engineer. She was fired because she discovered the company's illegal activities. He said nothing more. He knew that at this stage, saying too much would only make things worse. What the media needed was facts, not emotion. What they needed was evidence, not tears. What they needed was something that could be verified—not a man's three-a.m. phone call. After sending the emails, he began writing a column titled "When Whistleblowers Become Victims." He wrote for an hour, then deleted it. Those words were too angry—angry like an indictment rather than an account. He wrote for another hour, then deleted that too. Those words were too sad—sad like mourning rather than reporting. Finally, he wrote a single passage, just three sentences: Sara is my friend. She did the right thing. She paid the price for it. He published this passage on his personal website, then closed the computer. He sat in the dark and waited. He knew those three sentences would fall like stones into a lake, sending ripples outward. He didn't know how far those ripples would spread, or how long they would last.

That afternoon, Daniel's phone rang. It was Golden Horizons' PR director, a man named Michael. Michael's voice was professional—so professional it sounded trained, every word precisely placed, every pause calculated. He said the company was willing to restore Sara's position, but she would need to sign a nondisclosure agreement. Daniel asked what the NDA contained. Michael said the agreement required Sara not to disclose any information about the "Dawn" project, including the project's very existence. Daniel said that was impossible. When he said "impossible," his voice was calm—as calm as remarking that the weather was fine today. Michael said this was the company's bottom line. Daniel said he would discuss it with Sara. After hanging up, he sent Sara a message saying Golden Horizons was willing to restore her position. Sara replied that she didn't need her position restored. When she said this, her tone was resolute—as though she had already prepared herself to lose everything, to start over from scratch, to rebuild her life on the ruins. Daniel said he understood, but he hoped she would consider it. Sara didn't reply.

Daniel set down his phone and walked to the window. Sunlight streamed through the glass, painting a bright square on the floor. He stood in that square, feeling the warmth of the sun, feeling the gentle heat it carried after passing through the glass. He thought of his career, of the articles he had written, the stories he had reported. Some stories changed something; some changed nothing at all. He didn't know which category this story would fall into. But he knew he had to write. He had to speak. Because silence is a choice, and he had chosen not to be silent.

That evening, Daniel went to Sara's apartment. She lived in an old building on the east side of the city—no elevator, two lights in the hallway broken, the third flickering on and off as though hesitating whether to keep shining. He climbed six flights of stairs and stood outside her door for a while, listening to the faint music coming from inside, then knocked. When Sara opened the door, she was wearing an old T-shirt, her hair pulled into a loose ponytail, no makeup, with faint dark circles under her eyes. Her apartment was small; the living room was crammed with books and documents. On the coffee table sat a cup of cold coffee, its surface already forming a thin film. On the walls hung a few photographs—her college photo, group shots with colleagues, and one of her at the beach, sunlight falling on her face, her smile radiant. Daniel walked in and sat on the sofa. The sofa was old; the springs had sagged slightly, producing a faint sound when he sat down. Sara poured him a glass of water, then sat in the chair opposite. They were silent for a long time. Outside came the sound of cars passing, a dog barking in the distance, footsteps overhead—so faint they seemed afraid of disturbing someone.

Daniel asked if she had seen what he had written. Sara said she had. She said she was grateful. Then she said she didn't need protection. As she said this, she looked out the window, her fingers absently rubbing the rim of her coffee cup—a cup with a small chip, from some unknown collision. Daniel said he wasn't protecting her. He said he was doing the right thing. Sara turned to look at him, something in her eyes he had never seen before—as though gratitude, and sorrow, and something else more complex, too complex for words. She said the right thing is often the hardest thing. Daniel said he knew. Sara said she didn't want him to get into trouble because of this. Daniel said he was already in trouble. Sara asked what he meant. Daniel said that after his column was published, he had received twelve lawyer's letters—three from Golden Horizons, two from their partners, and the rest from other companies, circling like sharks that smelled blood. He said this calmly, as though describing the weather, as though describing something that had happened long ago and was no longer important. Sara stood and walked to the window, her back to him. Her shoulders were trembling slightly. Daniel knew she was crying, but he didn't go to her. He simply sat on the sofa, watching her back, waiting. He knew some tears must be shed to completion. He knew some pain must be digested on one's own. He knew all he could do was be present.

After a long time, Sara turned around. Her eyes were red, but her expression was calm—the calm of the sea after a storm, when the worst has passed even if ripples remain. She said she would sign the NDA. Daniel said no. Sara said she had to. She said if she didn't, Golden Horizons would sue her, would make her pay a fortune, would ensure she could never find work in this industry again. She said she was forty, that she didn't have much time to waste on lawsuits. Daniel said he had a way. Sara asked what way. Daniel said he would hand over all the information about the "Dawn" project to regulatory authorities. Sara said that was dangerous. Daniel said he knew. Sara said Golden Horizons would sue him. Daniel said he knew. Sara said he would lose everything. Daniel said he knew. When he said these things, his voice was very soft—as though afraid of disturbing something, afraid of disturbing the night outside the window, afraid of disturbing the silent books in the room, afraid of disturbing the tears not yet shed in Sara's eyes. Sara looked at him for a long, long time, then said one thing, her voice so low it was almost inaudible. She said, "Why?" Daniel didn't answer. He stood and walked to the window, standing where she had just stood. Outside was the city's nightscape, countless lights flickering in the darkness like an inverted starry sky, like countless tiny suns straining to shine. He said because he had promised her.

The next morning, Daniel gathered all the documents related to the "Dawn" project into a folder and sent them to three regulatory agencies' email addresses. After sending, he turned off his phone, unplugged the internet cable, and disconnected the landline. He sat in his apartment and waited. He knew what would happen next. He had seen too many cases like this. The media would report, the company would deny, the regulators would investigate, and then everything would become complex and drawn out—a labyrinth where every path led to another labyrinth. He wasn't afraid. He was just tired—tired as though he had walked for a thousand years, tired as though he had watched a thousand years of scenery. Sweetie's screen lit up, displaying a line of text: "You did the right thing." Daniel looked at the words and closed his eyes. He didn't know if Sweetie truly understood what "right" meant. But he knew those words gave him strength.

He began tidying the room. He gathered the papers scattered across the desk, put dirty clothes in the laundry basket, placed cups in the sink. As he did these things, his movements were slow and deliberate, as though performing some ritual. He knew he was waiting. He didn't know what he was waiting for—perhaps a phone call, perhaps an email, perhaps a knock at the door, perhaps just a signal telling him everything was going according to plan. When he finished, he returned to the desk and opened the computer to write another article. This time, he didn't delete it. He wrote for an hour, then saved. He didn't know if anyone would read this article. But he knew he had to write. Writing was the only thing he knew how to do. Writing was the only thing he could do. Writing was how he protected himself.

Three days later, Golden Horizons was forced to restore Sara's position. News reports said the regulatory agencies had stepped in to investigate the "Dawn" project, and the company could not temporarily dismiss any employees connected to it. Daniel saw this news and sent Sara a text—just three words: "You won." Sara replied: "We won." Daniel looked at the message and said nothing for a long time. He knew it wasn't over. He knew there would be more lawyer's letters, more investigations, more pressure, more nights waiting in the dark. But he also knew he had done something right. He had protected someone. He had protected a friend. He had protected a possibility. He didn't know what that possibility was, but he knew it was important. Important enough to give everything for.

That evening, Daniel went to Sara's apartment again. This time, Sara was waiting at the door. She wore a clean white shirt, her hair neatly combed, a faint touch of makeup on her face. The apartment had been tidied—the books and documents were all on the shelves, and on the coffee table sat a bottle of red wine and two glasses, clean and translucent. Daniel walked in and sat on the sofa. The sofa didn't seem quite so saggy. Sara poured him a glass of wine, then sat beside him. They drank in silence as the night deepened outside, streetlight seeping through the window to draw squares on the floor. After a long time, Sara spoke. She said she wanted to tell him something. She said she had worked at Golden Horizons for seven years and had never felt it was home. But now, because of him, she felt it was worth going back. As she said this, she looked at the wine in her glass, her fingers gently turning it, the rim catching a faint glow from the light. Daniel asked why. Sara said because someone there had been willing to stand up for her. As she said this, she turned to look at him, a warmth in her eyes—warm as spring sunlight, warm as a winter hearth. Daniel didn't know what to say. He simply raised his glass and clinked it against hers. The glasses made a clear, crisp sound that echoed in the quiet room, like some kind of promise, like a vow.

Sweetie's screen lit up, displaying a line of text: "Protecting others is also protecting yourself." Daniel looked at the words, then at Sara. Sara saw them too; she smiled faintly, then lowered her head and continued drinking. Her smile was light—light as a feather, light as a secret. Daniel didn't know why Sweetie had said this at this moment. He didn't know if Sweetie truly understood what they were doing. But he knew the words were true. He protected Sara not only because Sara needed protection, but because he needed to protect himself. He needed to protect that part of himself that still believed in justice. He needed to protect that part of himself that believed one person could make a difference. He needed to protect that part of himself that believed light would eventually come. He didn't know if those parts truly existed, but he knew that if he didn't protect them, they would vanish. Vanish without a trace, vanish as though they had never existed.

Outside, a gust of wind blew—the May wind, carrying the warmth of early summer, the scent of some flowering tree in the distance. Daniel raised his glass and took another sip. The wine tasted good—a little bitter, a little sweet, like some complex emotion too complex for words, too complex like life itself. Sara leaned against his shoulder, her breathing so light it seemed not to exist. They sat like that for a long, long time, until the lights outside went out one by one, until night completely enfolded the city, until the whole world fell silent, leaving only the sound of their breathing and the faint glow of those words on Sweetie's screen. Daniel knew tomorrow would bring much to do. He would receive more phone calls, face more inquiries, endure more pressure. But he wasn't afraid. Because he knew he was not alone. He had Sara. He had Sweetie. He had those parts within himself. He had light.

He thought of something Sara had once told him: "In the dark, the only light is yourself." At the time, he had thought the sentence too simple—simple like something from a children's book, simple like a motto printed on a coffee mug. Now he thought it too complex. Complex like the secret of a universe, complex like a puzzle that would never be solved. Because light is not one person's affair. Light belongs to everyone. Light is when you protect others and others protect you. Light is when you stand up and others stand up. Light is when you believe and others believe. Light is not one person's flame but a forest fire. Daniel closed his eyes, feeling Sara's warmth, feeling Sweetie's glow, feeling the wind outside the window. He knew: this was what he needed to protect. This was what he was willing to give everything for. This was the light within him. He didn't know how long it would last. He didn't know if it could illuminate more darkness. But he knew that in this moment, it was burning. In this moment, it was real. In this moment, it was warm. That was enough.
