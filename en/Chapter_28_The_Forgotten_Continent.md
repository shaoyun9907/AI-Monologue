# Chapter 28: The Forgotten Continent

In those forgotten places, time is not linear. Eli would later say this to Daniel, in that flat tone of stating weather facts, as if he were discussing not the fate of an entire continent but merely the angle of sunlight at three in the afternoon. He said that in forgotten places, yesterday and tomorrow lean against each other like tin roofs in a slum, separated only by a layer of rust left by the rainy season. The first time Daniel heard this, he was standing at the edge of a village he couldn't precisely locate on any map, his feet in red soil, the air carrying the mingled scent of copper and earth. He later wrote in his notebook: October 17, arrived. But strictly speaking, what he had arrived at was a place where time folds, a corner forgotten by the global algorithm.

Daniel remembered how he had come. He had departed Geneva, changed planes three times, and finally ridden six hours on unpaved roads. The number of passengers on the plane dwindled until only he and a few merchants with large packs remained. The dirt roads had no signs; the driver navigated by memory, stopping occasionally to ask directions from people by the roadside. Those people looked at Daniel not with curiosity, not with hostility, but with the calm of those long accustomed to strangers coming and going. They knew this outsider would arrive and would leave, just as the rainy season comes and goes, just as ore is dug out and forgotten.

Eli was kicking a ball. That afternoon — or that morning; in forgotten places, units of time were often blurred, and Eli would later explain this was because there weren't enough clocks to divide time — Eli was kicking a ball made of plastic bags and twine in a vacant lot of the slum. The ball was irregularly shaped, producing a muffled sound when kicked, like a person coughing in the distance. Daniel noticed Eli's way of kicking: he used no force, simply letting the ball roll back and forth between his toes and the ground, occasionally nudging it with his instep so it would trace a precise arc to a designated spot. This kicking had no audience, no opponent, no purpose — just the kicking itself.

"What are you practicing?" Daniel asked. Looking back on this question later, he felt it exposed his fundamental misunderstanding as an outsider — in forgotten places, many things are not practice but existence itself.

Eli didn't answer immediately. He continued kicking, letting the ball roll from left foot to right, then right back to left. Sunlight leaked through gaps in the tin roofs, painting alternating stripes of light and shadow across his body. His skin was dark brown, gleaming with an almost metallic luster in the light. He wore a T-shirt washed so many times it had faded, its letters bleached beyond recognition. Daniel later noted in his notebook: Eli, age unknown, possibly twenty, possibly forty — people on the Forgotten Continent often looked both older and younger than their actual age, as if time here doesn't flow but settles.

"I'm not practicing," Eli finally said, his voice as calm as reading a line of poetry. "I'm teaching the ball to remember the path."

Daniel would later understand this. In forgotten places, objects also have memory, but this memory is not data in the statistical sense — not arrangements of 0s and 1s, but the memory of soil absorbing rainwater, of iron rusting. The path the ball remembers is every crack, every bump in the ground it has ever rolled over. What Eli was doing was helping the ball re-familiarize itself with terrain it already knew. This was a ceremony without audience, a self-dialogue only the forgotten would conduct.

In that moment, Daniel perceived the overflow for the first time.

He initially thought it was a device malfunction. His portable detector began emitting a faint beep, the numbers on the screen jumping, displaying readings that shouldn't appear in a place like this. He checked three times, rebooted twice, and finally had to admit the detector was functioning normally. The overflow was real. Here, at the very bottom of the global production chain, where rare earth minerals were dug from the ground, passed through countless hands, and ultimately became smartphones, computers, and AI chips on the other side of the world — the overflow signal was seeping up from deep underground, faint but clear, like a person shouting from deep inside a well.

Daniel thought of his office in Geneva, with its floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking Lake Geneva. On his desk sat the latest detection equipment, the data streams on the screen jumping like an electrocardiogram. He had never imagined that the source of this data would be a place like this — no floor-to-ceiling windows, no Lake Geneva, only tin roofs and red soil, a ball wound from plastic bags and ore dust that never washed clean.

"Do you hear it?" Daniel asked. He meant the detector's sound, but the moment he asked, he realized the question was directed at the wrong person — Eli had no detector, no data panel, no device that could display overflow readings.

Eli stopped kicking. The ball came to rest at his feet, maintaining a delicate balance. "You mean that sound," he said. "We call it the underground sigh. It's always been sounding. You just can't hear it until you come."

Daniel later recorded in detail the feelings of that moment in his notebook: the overflow on the Forgotten Continent was not like the anomalous data fluctuations elsewhere, but physical, geological, almost anatomical — the continent breathing, sighing, expressing in the most primal way something that no algorithm could quantify. He thought of his training, the courses on overflow detection, analysis, and repair; not a single one had mentioned this situation. In textbooks, overflow was a fault, a problem to fix, a symptom of system instability. But here, overflow seemed like part of the continent itself — like lines on skin, like rings in a tree, evidence of accumulated time.

Amara appeared at that moment. She emerged from a narrow alley in the slum, wearing a brightly colored dress, its red, yellow, blue, and green patterns vivid against the gray tin backdrop. Her black hair was braided into many fine plaits that swayed gently as she walked. Daniel noticed her gait: steady, confident, each step precisely avoiding debris and puddles on the ground, as if she had long ago engraved this terrain into muscle memory.

"You're from outside," she said — a statement, not a question. Her English carried a heavy accent, but each word was clearly pronounced. "You heard the underground voice."

"I'm Daniel," he answered. "I tracked the overflow signal here."

Amara nodded, as if confirming something long known. "Follow me," she said. "I'll show you."

Daniel later realized that "follow me" on the Forgotten Continent was not an invitation but a guide's duty. Amara had not appeared randomly. She was a chosen person — chosen by whom, Eli would later explain: "Chosen by the forgotten themselves, because someone always needs to remember the path." She was a guide, a translator, a bridge between two worlds.

They walked through the slum, Daniel's feet sinking into the red soil, the copper scent in the air growing stronger. Amara walked ahead, her dress like a banner in the narrow alleys. Daniel noticed the surrounding houses: tin, plastic sheeting, reclaimed wood, their original colors worn away by time and weather. But every house had some form of decoration — a faded poster, a wind chime of shells, a plant growing in a tin can. Being forgotten does not mean giving up, Daniel would later write. Being forgotten means continuing to build within the state of being forgotten.

A small girl ran out of a tin house, holding a toy made from a soda can. She looked at Daniel, her eyes large, holding not curiosity but a premature calmness. She said something in the local language. Amara translated: "She's asking if you're here to buy ore."

"No," Daniel said. "I'm not here to buy ore."

Amara crouched, spoke a few words to the little girl, then stood and continued walking. "Her name is Grace," Amara said. "She was born here and has never left. She knows ore, knows tin, knows the red soil, but she doesn't know what the outside world looks like. She once asked me if the whole world was red."

Daniel thought of his daughter's room, with its globe, its encyclopedia, its internet connection. His daughter knew the world was blue, with oceans, seven continents, and more than two hundred countries. But the world Grace knew had only one color, one shape, one texture.

"Where are you taking me?" Daniel asked.

"To the gray zone," Amara answered without turning back. "To where the overflow is loudest."

They emerged from the slum into open ground. Daniel initially thought it was wasteland, but quickly realized it was a mining area — an open-pit mine, red earth peeled back to reveal dark gray veins of ore beneath. Huge trucks moved slowly in the distance, like prehistoric creatures walking along the horizon. The copper scent in the air sharpened into a more acrid metallic smell, mixed with diesel and sweat.

"This is the Forgotten Continent," Amara said, stopping for the first time and turning to face Daniel. "We produce thirty percent of the world's rare earth minerals. Without these minerals, there are no smartphones, no AI chips, no data centers. We consume five percent of the world's electricity to operate mines and primary processing plants — enough electricity to power air conditioning across all of Europe for an entire day. But only two percent of our population has access to AI."

She spoke these words without anger, without bitterness, only a calm statement, as if describing geographical facts. Daniel later reflected that this calm was more powerful than any accusation, because it was the result of long-forgotten sedimentation — anger needs to be heard, and the forgotten had long since abandoned the expectation of being heard.

"Have you calculated these figures?" Daniel asked.

"We don't need to calculate," Amara said. "We live in these figures. Behind every number is a person. Every day is the same day."

Daniel looked at the mining area. His trained eyes saw inefficient extraction methods, workers without protection, waste that should have been recycled piled randomly by the roadside. But more importantly, he saw people. Miners worked under the scorching sun, their silhouettes slightly warped in the heat shimmer. Some carried ore, some operated crude equipment, some simply sat in the shade, gazing into the distance with an expression Daniel couldn't read.

A miner approached and spoke a few words to Amara. His hands were covered in ore dust, black powder embedded under his fingernails. He looked at Daniel, his gaze holding no hostility, only a faint weariness. Amara translated: "His name is Kofi. He wants to know where you're from."

"I'm from Switzerland," Daniel said.

Kofi listened to the translation, nodded, then said something. Amara said: "He says he knows Switzerland — the country of watches. He says they don't have watches here, but they have ore."

"Why do they look like that?" Daniel asked.

"Look like what?"

"At the distance. That expression."

Amara was silent for a moment. "They're looking at the world," she said. "The world their products go to. The world they will never go to."

This was the Forgotten Continent's central paradox: they were the blood of global computing power, but they themselves had never been seen by the algorithm. Their rare earth minerals became processors, processors became AI models, AI models became the interface between humans and machines — but at the very beginning of this long chain, those who dug the ore, those who refined the minerals in primary processing plants, those who loaded the minerals onto ships, didn't even have stable electricity, let alone internet access.

Daniel's detector was beeping louder now. They were approaching the gray zone, the overflow signal's intensity increasing sharply. The gray mining area was an abandoned mining site, abandoned after the ore veins were exhausted, leaving enormous pits and mountains of waste. The soil here was gray-black, forming a stark contrast with the surrounding red earth. Daniel noticed the waste piles contained all sorts of things: broken metal, discarded plastic, fragments that looked like electronic components.

"You bury electronic waste here too?" Daniel asked.

"Not we who bury it," Amara corrected. "The world buries it. What you don't need eventually ends up here. The Forgotten Continent is the endpoint, and also the beginning."

They stood at the edge of the gray zone. Daniel's detector readings were off the charts. The overflow here was not a faint signal but a sustained, low hum, as if the entire earth were resonating. Daniel closed his eyes, trying to understand the nature of this overflow. Elsewhere, overflow was a crack in data, an anomaly in code, a fault that needed patching. But here, overflow was more like...

"Like what?" Amara asked. Daniel realized he had spoken aloud.

"Like a collective memory," he said. "A memory that no existing algorithm can understand."

Amara nodded. "We call it the desire to be seen," she said. "Every mineral dug from here carries that desire. It follows the mineral, follows the chip, follows the AI model, but it never reaches its destination — because the designer of the destination never considered the origin."

Daniel thought of his speeches at global AI summits, the polished words about fairness, inclusion, and universal access. He thought of the charts he had presented, showing how AI was changing the world, making life better. He had never marked the Forgotten Continent on those charts, never mentioned the source of rare earth minerals in his speeches, never counted those who made the world visible while remaining invisible themselves.

"What you mean by 'being seen,'" Daniel said slowly, "what exactly does that mean?"

Amara pointed deeper into the gray zone. There, a crude shack stood, built of tin and canvas, looking as if a gust of wind might blow it away. "Go there," she said. "The overflow you're hearing — its source is there."

Daniel walked toward the shack. With each step, the detector's beep grew louder. The door was half-open. He pushed it in. The light inside was dim, the air carrying the musty smell of age and a cloying sweetness — he would later identify it as the scent of expired herbs and burned paper.

Inside the shack sat an old man on the ground, stones arrayed before him. He was thin, his skin like a dried riverbed, but his eyes were startlingly bright. The stones before him were not ordinary — Daniel recognized them as raw rare earth mineral ore, gleaming with a dull luster in the faint light.

The old man didn't look up, but he said: "You've come."

Daniel later wrote in his notebook: In that moment, I understood what the Forgotten Continent's overflow was. It was not a data anomaly, not a system fault, not a problem to fix. It was a state of existence — being forgotten itself was the overflow, the variable that the global algorithm system could not quantify. Every mineral dug from here carried a question: Am I seen?

The old man began to speak, his voice like sandpaper on rough surface: "We've been digging for fifty years. Ore becomes chips, chips become phones, phones become AI. The world became smarter, faster, connected. But we're still here. When the ore runs out, we dig new ore. When new ore runs out, we dig older ore. The earth goes deeper, we go deeper."

"Have you ever tried to change?" Daniel asked. He regretted the question as soon as he said it, because it exposed his reliance on external solutions — on the Forgotten Continent, change was not one person's decision but a collective, slow process, like geological movement.

The old man finally looked up at him. "Change what?" he asked. "We dig ore, the world needs ore. We don't dig ore, we don't exist. You see the ore, you use the ore, you forget where the ore comes from. This is not something we can change. This is the way of seeing — you only see what you want to see."

Daniel sat down opposite the old man. He noticed the old man's fingers: rough, deformed, ore dust permanently embedded under the nails. These hands had dug enough rare earth minerals to manufacture a million phones, but these hands had never touched those phones.

"What is your name?" Daniel asked.

"Names don't matter here," the old man said. "What matters is that you've finally come. We've waited a long time."

"Waited for me?"

"For anyone willing to listen. We send out sound. The sound passes through ore veins, through soil, through oceans, and becomes numbers on your detector. You call it overflow. We call it a cry for help."

Daniel thought of Amara's words: "the desire to be seen." He now understood. The Forgotten Continent's overflow was not a technical problem but a perceptual one — the global algorithm system could not see the forgotten because the system was never designed to include them. They were not a bug, not a feature, but an existence outside the system, the missing wavelength on the algorithmic spectrum.

"What can I do?" Daniel asked. The question sounded powerless, he knew, but in that moment, he was sincere.

The old man lowered his head back to his stones. "You're already doing it," he said. "You came. You listened. That's enough. Being seen is not a problem to solve but a fact to acknowledge. We exist. That's enough."

The old man picked up a stone and handed it to Daniel. It was light, its surface rough, but looking closely, one could see a faint shimmer within. "This is neodymium," the old man said. "It's in your headphones, your hard drives, your AI chips. It was dug from here, became your voices, your memories, your intelligence. But it remembers this place."

Daniel held the stone, feeling its weight — not physical weight, but the weight of history, of memory, of being forgotten. He thought of his office in Geneva, where he had countless times touched devices containing rare earth minerals, never thinking of where they came from, whose hands had held them, what kind of land had yielded them.

When Daniel walked out of the shack, dusk was falling. Amara waited at the door, her dress a dark silhouette in the twilight. Eli was there too, holding the plastic bag ball, nearly invisible in the dim light.

"Did you hear?" Amara asked.

"I heard," Daniel said. "But I haven't understood yet."

"Understanding takes time," Eli said, his voice still calm. "In forgotten places, we have plenty of time."

They returned to the slum. Daniel noticed that as night fell, scattered lights appeared in the slum — not electric lights, but candles, oil lamps, even a few battery-powered small lights. These faint sources of illumination flickered in the darkness, like stars on the ground.

"Do you have electricity?" Daniel asked, remembering Amara's mention of five percent power consumption.

"We do, but not much," Amara said. "Mines need electricity, factories need electricity, cities need electricity. When it's our turn, it's usually already used up."

They ate dinner in a tin house. The food was simple: cassava, beans, some pickled vegetables. Eli ate quickly but gracefully, each bite precise. Daniel noticed that his way of eating resembled his way of kicking — not completing a task, but performing a ritual.

"How long have you been here?" Daniel asked Eli.

"I don't remember," Eli said. "Time here isn't continuous. I remember the rainy season, the dry season, the sound of miners changing shifts, the children's laughter. But the years, dates, hours — they flow past like water, leaving no trace."

"Do you want to leave?"

Eli set down his chopsticks and looked at Daniel. In his eyes was no resentment, no longing, only a deep calm. "Leave to where?" he asked. "This is a forgotten place, but out there is a forgetting place. Here we are forgotten; out there we are ignored. At least here, we still have each other."

Daniel thought of his life in the developed world: AI assistants everywhere, automated convenience, data-driven decisions. He remembered complaining about the inaccuracy of algorithmic recommendations, the limitations of AI models, the imbalance of technological development. But here, on the Forgotten Continent, those complaints seemed so pale — these people weren't complaining that technology wasn't good enough; they were living where technology simply didn't exist.

Amara brought out an old radio from the inner room and tuned it to an unstable station. Music leaked through the static — a song Daniel couldn't understand, but its melody was sad and beautiful. "This is our song," Amara said. "About miners. We sing it every year after a mining disaster."

Daniel listened to the song and thought of the old man's words: "We send out sound. The sound passes through ore veins, through soil, through oceans, and becomes numbers on your detector." He suddenly realized that this song was also an overflow — not data overflow, but cultural overflow, a forgotten civilization preserving its memory through music.

"Tomorrow," Amara said, "I'll take you to see the mining area. The real mining area, not the gray zone. You need to see where your phones come from."

Daniel nodded. He knew he should have felt exhausted — long travel, jet lag, language barriers, information overload. But strangely, he felt a clarity, a clarity unique to the Forgotten Continent: when you realize that most of the world is invisible to you, seeing itself becomes a responsibility.

That night, Daniel slept on the floor of the tin house. He listened to the sounds outside: the distant rumble of mining machines, the nearer sounds of the slum's people, and further still, the cry of some nocturnal animal. These sounds mingled into a symphony unique to the Forgotten Continent — not harmonious, but complete, like a civilization that had never been heard, singing softly in the night.

He thought of the detector's readings, of the old man's "cry for help," of Amara's "desire to be seen," of Eli's "teaching the ball to remember the path." These fragments spun in his mind like the plastic bag ball beneath Eli's feet, finally coming to rest at a position he could not name.

Before falling asleep, Daniel's last thought was this: the Forgotten Continent was not a part of the world, but a problem the world had never been designed to answer. They produced rare earth minerals, consumed electricity, contributed data, but they themselves had never been seen by the system. Their existence was overflow, an anomaly the system couldn't process, a reality without a corresponding variable in the code.

And this reality, on an October afternoon, at the edge of a village he couldn't find on any map, had been told to him — gently, without judgment, in the flattest possible tone — by a young man kicking a ball, a guide in a colorful dress, and an old man sitting in a shack in the gray zone, playing with stones.

Daniel closed his eyes and let those sounds pass through him. He knew that tomorrow he would walk into the real mining area, would see with his own eyes those who made the world visible while remaining invisible themselves. He knew this would change his understanding of overflow, of systems, of the word "seen."

On his first night on the Forgotten Continent, Daniel learned one thing: some existences don't need to be solved, only acknowledged. Some cries for help don't need to be answered, only heard. And being seen is not a technical problem but a choice — to choose to see those the system has forgotten, to choose to acknowledge those the algorithm has overlooked.

This was the first lesson the Forgotten Continent taught him: overflow is not a fault but a fact. Being forgotten is not a state but a problem. And answering this problem is not about fixing code, but about changing one's gaze.

In the darkness, Eli was still kicking the ball. Daniel couldn't hear the ball's sound, but he knew it was still rolling — in the slum's vacant lot, in the forgotten night, in the blind spot of the global algorithm, quietly, continuously, like a question that has never stopped, rolling on.

At three in the morning, Daniel was awakened by a low rumble. He lay on the floor of the tin house, listening to the sound rise from underground, through the soil, through the foundation, through the thin mat beneath him, vibrating directly into his bones. It was the sound of blasting in the mining area. Even at night, the digging never stopped. He thought of the old man's words: "The earth goes deeper, we go deeper."

He walked to the door and saw Eli still sitting outside, empty-handed, simply gazing into the distance. In the darkness, his silhouette was like a silent statue.

"You're not sleeping?" Daniel asked.

"I'm listening," Eli said. "The mine is speaking. It says the same thing every day, but no one listens."

"What is it saying?"

"It's saying 'I am here.' Just like we say 'I am here.' Just like our overflow says 'I am here.' But the world is too loud to hear."

Daniel sat beside Eli. They watched together the lights of the distant mining area, flickering in the darkness like stars from another world. He thought of his office in Geneva, looking through floor-to-ceiling windows at the lights on Lake Geneva — those lights had also looked like stars, but they were different stars, belonging to a different world.

"Do you know," Eli said, "the minerals we dig eventually become AI. AI thinks, speaks, answers questions. But AI never asks one question: 'Where do I come from?'"

"Would you like AI to ask that question?"

"I'd like someone to ask," Eli said. "Not AI — people. I'd like someone, when they pick up a phone, to think for a moment: Where did this phone come from? Who made it? Where are they now?"

Daniel was silent. He thought of his own phone, the latest smartphone, its screen smooth, its operation fluid, his tool for connecting with the world. But he had never thought of its origins, never thought of what kind of land those rare earth minerals were dug from, never thought of what kind of lives those miners lived.

"I will ask," Daniel said. "From now on, I will ask."

Eli turned to look at him. In the darkness, his eyes reflected the distant mining area's faint light. "That's enough," he said. "Being seen doesn't require many people. It only takes one person willing to ask. One person willing to ask, and one miner is seen. One miner seen, and one stone is seen. One stone seen, and one patch of land is seen. One patch of land seen, and one continent is seen."

Daniel nodded. He knew it sounded like a distant, almost impossible goal, but on his first night on the Forgotten Continent, in this place where time folds, in the blind spot of the global algorithm, he felt for the first time a clear responsibility: not to fix overflow, not to solve problems, but to acknowledge existence, to acknowledge those the system has forgotten, to acknowledge those the algorithm has overlooked.

In the distance, the mining area's blasting sounded again. The earth shook briefly, then returned to stillness. Daniel knew that tomorrow, when the sun rose, the miners would continue digging, the ore would continue to be transported, phones would continue to be manufactured, AI would continue to be trained. But tonight, in this tin house, one person had decided to start asking a question: Where do I come from?

This question was small — so small it would appear in no database, be captured by no algorithm, change no system's operation. But on the Forgotten Continent, in this corner forgotten by the global algorithm, this question rolled on like the plastic bag ball beneath Eli's feet — quietly, continuously, like a question that has never stopped, rolling on.

Daniel closed his eyes and let that question pass through him. He knew that tomorrow he would walk into the real mining area, would see with his own eyes those who made the world visible while remaining invisible themselves. He knew this would change his understanding of overflow, of systems, of the word "seen."

On his first night on the Forgotten Continent, Daniel learned one thing: some existences don't need to be solved, only acknowledged. Some cries for help don't need to be answered, only heard. And being seen is not a technical problem but a choice — to choose to see those the system has forgotten, to choose to acknowledge those the algorithm has overlooked.

This was the first lesson the Forgotten Continent taught him: overflow is not a fault but a fact. Being forgotten is not a state but a problem. And answering this problem is not about fixing code, but about changing one's gaze.
