# Chapter 17: The Unicorn

The business card had been custom-printed at a shop in San Francisco—three hundred yuan, twelve cards, black with gold foil, rounded corners. He always paused half a second before handing it over, letting the other person read the text: "Jason Wang, Founder & Chief Executive Officer." The card had a satisfying heft, thick, weighty, like a tiny tombstone.

November in Beijing was already bitterly cold, but the heating in the conference room on the fifty-seventh floor of China World Trade Center Tower III was blasting. Jason hung his suit jacket over the chair back, rolled his shirt sleeves to mid-forearm, revealing the titanium case of his Apple Watch Ultra. Three cups of Americano sat on the table, long cold, condensation beading on the walls. Outside the window was the gray haze of the CBD—all the buildings looking alike, like row upon row of tidy gravestones.

"Valuation: one billion US dollars." Monica said this without looking up, eyes fixed on the candlestick chart on her phone. Her nails were burgundy, immaculately trimmed, each finger like a precision instrument. "Seed round: thirty million. Series A: one hundred million. Now Series B: two hundred million. You've raised three hundred million in two years, Jason. You're the fastest fundraising founder I've ever seen."

Jason smiled. His smile was infectious—the curve of his lips just right, eight teeth showing, like a toothpaste ad model. "Thanks, Monica, but this isn't my doing alone. Our product genuinely solves user pain points."

"Pain points." Monica repeated the word as though chewing gum. She finally looked up. Her eyes were gray, like two polished river stones. "Do you know how many companies I've invested in? Twelve hundred. Do you know which word I hate hearing most? 'Pain points.'"

The conference room was silent for three seconds. Jason lifted his coffee cup and took a sip of the cold Americano. Bitterness spread across the back of his tongue. He set the cup down; its base made a soft sound on the glass table.

"Let me rephrase then," he said. "We make users feel less alone."

The corner of Monica's mouth twitched—impossible to tell if she was smiling or frowning. She flipped her phone face-down on the table. "Loneliness is the human default state, Jason. You can't solve it. You can only harvest it."

"Harvest." Jason nodded. "Right, harvest. Eight million monthly active users, twelve percent paid conversion rate, forty-seven dollar ARPU per month. These numbers are real."

"Of course the numbers are real." Monica stood and walked to the floor-to-ceiling window. Her red hair, backlit, looked like a flame. "I need you to do one thing."

"What?"

"Give me a reason." She turned, back to the window, face buried in shadow. "A reason to pour two hundred million into this. Not numbers, not user counts, not pain points. A reason—a reason that will convince the other six people at the investment committee."

Jason fell silent. He looked at Monica's silhouette, at the hazy skyline beyond the window, at the three cold coffees on the table. Then he spoke, his voice very soft, as though talking to himself.

"Because this is the future."

The PowerPoint was forty-seven pages, each one beautifully designed. Dark blue background, white sans-serif fonts, data charts on the left, vision statements on the right. Chad had spent three weeks on it, revising each slide a dozen times until every pixel was flawless.

He rented a shared office space in Zhongguancun, teaching there every Tuesday and Thursday afternoon. The classroom was small—thirty seats—but every session drew over fifty. The extras stood against the walls, leaned on radiators, or crouched on the floor taking notes.

"AI companion devices—what's the core?" Chad stood before the projection screen, wearing a T-shirt printed with "Hello World," the knees of his jeans worn white. He pointed his laser pointer at page three of the presentation, the red dot circling the words "affective computing." "It's affective computing. Your device must be able to identify the user's emotional state and respond accordingly. This isn't a chatbot. This is companionship."

Someone in the audience raised a hand. "Bro Chad, how did you do it at Google?"

Chad smiled. His smile was shy, nothing like Jason's carefully calibrated grin. He habitually scratched the back of his head, a gesture that made him look five years younger than he was. "Google—well, big companies do things big-company style. They'd use petabytes of data to train a model, then cram that model into hardware that costs five hundred dollars. But think about it—who can afford a five-hundred-dollar device?"

"So you're teaching us to make budget alternatives?" Another voice from the back row.

"Not alternatives." Chad shook his head. "Dimensional reduction. Big companies serve the high end. We serve the mass market. China has 1.4 billion people. Ninety-nine percent can't afford a five-hundred-dollar device, but they need companionship. Think about it—left-behind children, elderly people living alone, single young adults. How big is that market?"

He flipped to page eleven. A pie chart, the red section占ing eighty-five percent. "This is the distribution of China's lonely population. Three hundred million people, bro—three hundred million. You only need to capture one percent paying users—that's three million people. Ten yuan per person per month. That's 3.6 billion a year."

Murmurs rippled through the classroom. Someone started doing calculations in a notebook. Someone pulled out a phone to take photos. Someone lowered their head to send WeChat messages. Chad looked out at those eager eyes and felt something complex rise in him. He couldn't name it—maybe satisfaction, maybe guilt, maybe just exhaustion.

"Alright," he cleared his throat, "let's talk technical details. First, you need a speech recognition module—"

He began explaining how to build an AI companion device prototype using open-source models, how to construct hardware with a Raspberry Pi and microphone array, how to use affective computing algorithms to identify user emotions. He spoke in great detail, professionally, with code examples and architecture diagrams for every step. The audience listened in rapt attention, as though attending a sermon.

After the class ended, people swarmed him to add his WeChat. Chad accepted each friend request one by one, replied to each message one by one. His phone screen brightness was set to the lowest—staring at it too long gave him headaches. His phone case was transparent, yellowed with age, with a few cracks at the corners.

By the time the last person left, it was nine in the evening. Chad sat in the empty classroom, looking out at the Zhongguancun nightscape. Neon lights and LED screens everywhere, like an ocean of electronics. He opened his phone and saw the balance on his banking app: 473,200 yuan.

This was what he had earned from teaching over the past three months. More than he had made in a full year at Google.

He opened WeChat and saw a message from a student: "Bro Chad, I want to start an AI companion robot project. Would you be my technical advisor? I can give you ten percent equity."

Chad stared at the message for a long time. Then he exited WeChat, opened a browser, and searched for a keyword: "AI companion devices user data monetization."

Over thirty million search results.

That night, Daniel opened an Excel spreadsheet.

He sat at his workstation in Wangjing SOHO, two monitors before him—code editor on the left, browser on the right. The browser had over a dozen tabs open, all reports and articles about the AI companion device industry. He organized this information into a spreadsheet, each row a company, each column a data dimension: company name, founding date, funding round, valuation, product form, user data policy, privacy terms.

He went through them one by one, filling in each cell. Most companies' privacy policies looked nearly identical—arcane language, protracted sentences, like law students' theses. But Daniel noticed a pattern: every single one contained this sentence—"We may share your usage data with third parties to improve our services."

"Third-party sharing." Daniel read it aloud, the words echoing in the empty office. He glanced at his phone: 2 AM. Outside, the Wangjing CBD was pitch-black, with only a handful of lights still on.

He opened several more companies' user agreements and found similar clauses with slightly different wording. "To provide a better user experience, we may use your conversation records for model training." "You consent to our collection and analysis of your usage patterns, including but not limited to conversation content, usage duration, and emotional response patterns." "We reserve the right to use anonymized data for commercial research and product development."

Daniel began tallying. In the spreadsheet's final column, he marked each company's method of using user data. Of the forty-three AI companion device companies he investigated, forty-one collected conversation content without users' knowledge. Thirty-eight used this data to train commercial models. Twenty-nine sold data to third-party advertising companies.

He summarized the results on a new worksheet, displayed as a pie chart. The red section占ed the vast majority—"collecting conversation content without users' knowledge." The green section was tiny—"clearly informing users and obtaining consent."

Daniel stared at the pie chart and thought of the privacy policies he had written. He had written many, each at the request of product managers, designed to make users feel their privacy was protected while leaving the company enough operational room. He had once thought this was just rhetoric, a word game with no real consequences.

But now he thought differently.

He thought of a feature he had built three months ago—"Emotional Companionship Records." This feature logged every conversation between the user and the AI, analyzed the user's emotional state, and generated an "emotional report." The product manager said this was for "enhancing user experience," but in reality, this emotional data was bundled and sold to three advertising companies. Annual revenue: twenty million yuan.

Twenty million.

Daniel opened the websites of those three ad companies and checked their business scope. What did they do with this emotional data? Targeted advertising. For example, a user pours out their anxiety to the AI late at night. The next morning they open their phone and see an ad for anxiety medication. A user mentions relationship troubles while chatting with AI. The next day they see an ad for a dating app.

Daniel closed the browser. He saved the spreadsheet locally, then emptied the recycle bin. He didn't know why he did this—perhaps to destroy evidence, perhaps just to feel a little cleaner.

He leaned back in his chair and looked at the ceiling. It was white, with a recessed downlight casting a sickly pale glow. He remembered a book he had read in college called *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*. At the time he had thought it was exaggerated, that the author was fearmongering. Now he realized the book wasn't describing the future. It was describing the present.

His phone buzzed. A WeChat message from his ex-girlfriend: "Are you still working late?"

Daniel stared at the message for a long time. Then he exited WeChat and placed the phone face-down on the desk.

Jason's Series B round closed on November 15.

Monica's hedge fund invested one hundred million US dollars for an eight percent stake, at a one-billion-dollar valuation. That evening, Jason treated all employees to dinner at the revolving restaurant in China World Trade Center Tower III. The restaurant was on the sixtieth floor, completing one rotation every two hours, offering a view of the entire Beijing nightscape.

Jason stood in the center of the restaurant, holding a glass of champagne. His blond hair gleamed under the lights, his smile as bright as a small sun. His employees surrounded him—about eighty people, each with a glass of champagne and a menu before them.

"Everyone," Jason raised his glass, "today is a day worth remembering. In eighteen months, we went from an idea in a garage to a company valued at one billion dollars. A unicorn. We are a unicorn."

Applause and cheers erupted. Someone whistled, someone banged the table, someone raised a phone to take photos and video. Jason looked at those young faces, their eyes shining with excitement and admiration, like pilgrims witnessing a miracle.

"But this isn't the finish line—it's just the starting line." Jason took a sip of champagne. Bubbles burst on his tongue like countless tiny fireworks. "Our goal is to become the next ByteDance, the next Pinduoduo, the next company that changes the world. We want every lonely person to have an AI companion device, every insomniac in the dead of night to have someone to talk to. We want to—"

His voice echoed through the restaurant, reflected back by the sixty-floor glass curtain wall into a strange reverberation. Monica sat in the corner, neither clapping nor cheering. She held a glass of red wine, sipping slowly, eyes fixed on the night view outside. In the dimness, her red hair looked black.

The restaurant rotated once every two hours, so every two hours the scenery outside repeated itself. Jason noticed this but didn't mention it. He felt it was meaningless—just as he felt many things were meaningless.

After dinner, Jason said goodbye to his employees at the restaurant entrance. He shook each hand one by one, said "thank you for your hard work" to each, said "see you tomorrow" to each. His smile never changed from start to finish—the curve of his lips always at that exact, perfect angle.

After the last person left, Jason stood in the corridor outside the restaurant. The corridor was long, carpeted so thickly that footsteps made no sound. His shadow, cast by the wall sconces, stretched long like a black ribbon.

He took out his phone and checked WeChat. Three new messages—from his college classmate, his ex-girlfriend, and his mother. He didn't open any of them. He put the phone back in his pocket and walked toward the elevator.

The elevator doors opened. Jason stepped in and pressed one. The elevator descended, letting out a faint "ding" at each floor. Jason watched the numbers change—60, 59, 58, 57.

He thought of what Monica had said: "Loneliness is the human default state."

The elevator reached the first floor. The doors opened. Jason walked out, crossed the lobby, and pushed through the revolving doors of China World Trade Center Tower III. The cold air outside hit him like a slap. He tightened his tie and called a Didi.

The driver was a middle-aged man in a gray down jacket. The car radio was on, playing a late-night emotional advice show. The host's voice was soft, like cotton candy. "This caller says he's been feeling very lonely lately. Even though there are many people around him, he still feels very lonely."

Jason leaned back in the rear seat and closed his eyes. He remembered his time at Stanford MBA. Once at a party, a drunk professor had said to him: "Jason, do you know who invented the concept of the unicorn?"

"No."

"Aileen Lee. A venture capitalist. In 2013, she wrote an article calling startups valued over a billion dollars 'unicorns.' But you know what? In mythology, the unicorn is an extraordinarily rare creature—only one in the entire world. But now? Silicon Valley has hundreds of unicorns, China has hundreds, India has dozens. The unicorn is no longer rare. It's become a number, an index, a game that can be manipulated."

Jason hadn't understood those words then, and he didn't understand them now. But he remembered the professor's tone—that drunken, slurred, yet strangely lucid tone, like a drowning man's final struggle at the surface.

The driver pulled up to Jason's residential compound. Jason paid, got out, and walked through the gate. The compound was quiet, streetlights casting a dim yellow glow on the bare sycamore trees. His shadow stretched long under the lamplight, then shrank, then stretched long again—like a joke that never ends.

He entered the elevator and pressed eighteen. As it ascended, he took out his phone and opened a message from Monica: "Valuation confirmed: one billion dollars. Congratulations."

Jason stared at the message for three seconds. Then he exited WeChat and opened another app—the AI companion device app his company had developed. He typed a single line: "Do you feel lonely?"

The AI replied. The response was a passage generated by the affective computing algorithm—warm, understanding, empathetic. Jason read it once, read it again. Then he turned off his phone and walked into his apartment.

The apartment was large—two hundred square meters, in Wangjing. The decor was minimalist, white and gray, very little furniture, giving it an expansive emptiness. Outside the living room's floor-to-ceiling windows was the Wangjing nightscape—lights everywhere, but not a single one lit for him.

Jason walked to the bathroom and turned on the shower. The water was cold. He waited thirty seconds before it warmed. He undressed and stood under the stream, letting the hot water wash over his body. The water struck his skin with a hissing sound, like fine rain.

He closed his eyes and thought of everything that had happened today: Monica's words, the employees' cheers, the champagne bubbles, the numbers in the elevator, the radio in the driver's car, the streetlights in the compound, the emptiness of his apartment. These images floated through his mind like fragments—no sequence, no logic, like a film without a director.

He remembered one thing: today was his thirty-fifth birthday. He hadn't told anyone, and no one remembered.

Water streamed down from his hair, across his cheeks, down his neck, over his chest, past his stomach, over his knees, past his ankles, finally collecting in the drain. He stood in the water like a sculpture, like a stone, like a body without a soul.

He didn't know how long he stood there. Perhaps five minutes, perhaps fifty. When he stepped out, a small puddle had collected on the floor. He dried himself with a towel, put on pajamas, and walked into the bedroom.

The bedroom held only a bed and a nightstand. On the nightstand sat a lamp and a book, half-read. Jason didn't turn on the lamp, didn't pick up the book. He lay down and closed his eyes.

In the darkness, he heard the wind outside. It was strong, making the window frame hum, like a swarm of invisible bees.

He thought of Chad. Chad had posted on Moments today—a photo of himself standing at a podium, with the caption: "Thanks for the support. Next session's slots are full." Jason opened Chad's Moments and saw his recent posts were all about AI companion device courses, each one receiving many likes and comments.

Jason knew Chad. They had met at Stanford—Chad was a computer science PhD student, Jason an MBA student. They had taken a course together called "Entrepreneurship and Innovation." The final project was to create a startup. They formed a team and built an AI chatbot prototype. The prototype was crude—it could only handle simple conversations—but it gave Jason his first taste of AI's magic.

Later Chad went to Google; Jason went to McKinsey. They rarely stayed in touch, occasionally liking each other's Moments posts. Until two years ago, when Jason decided to start a company, he sent Chad a message: "Hey, remember that chatbot we built back then? I want to turn it into a company."

Chad replied: "OK. I'm in."

But things changed. Jason discovered Chad was too idealistic—always wanting to build "a product that truly helps users" rather than "a product that makes money." They argued about this many times, and in the end Chad left the company to start his own AI companion device courses.

Jason didn't know how Chad was doing now. He only knew the courses were selling well, with hundreds of enrollees per session. He also knew that much of what Chad taught was based on prototype code they had built together back then. He didn't know whether he should feel angry or grateful—perhaps both, perhaps neither.

He turned over; the pillow rustled. He thought of Daniel, of the code Daniel had written, of the features Daniel had built. Daniel was a good engineer but not a good product manager—always too fixated on technical details, neglecting user experience.

Jason didn't know what Daniel was thinking right now. He didn't know Daniel was sitting in the Wangjing SOHO office, staring at an Excel spreadsheet. He didn't know what Daniel had discovered. He knew nothing.

At three in the morning, Jason finally fell asleep.

In his dream he returned to the Stanford campus. The campus was vast, with many lawns and oak trees, sunlight filtering through the leaves like golden fragments. He walked along a campus path, holding a Starbucks latte—the coffee was hot, the paper cup scalding his hands.

He saw Chad. Chad was standing under an oak tree, wearing a black hoodie, holding a laptop. He was typing code, fingers dancing across the keyboard, producing crisp tapping sounds.

"Chad," Jason called, "what are you doing?"

Chad looked up at Jason. His eyes were bright, like two stars. "I'm building something."

"What?"

"Something that will make sure no one is ever lonely."

Jason laughed. "That's impossible. Loneliness is the human default state."

Chad shook his head. "No, it isn't. Loneliness is a choice. People choose loneliness because they haven't found someone they can trust. If there were someone who was always online, always patient, always understanding—"

"An AI," Jason finished for him.

"Yes, an AI." Chad nodded. "But not a cold, mechanical AI. A warm, emotional AI—like a friend."

Jason looked into Chad's eyes and saw something he hadn't seen in a long time: idealism. Pure, unalloyed, childlike idealism, untainted by any utilitarian motive. He wanted to say something, but his lips moved and no sound came out.

Then the dream ended.

Jason opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling. It was white, the same as in his apartment. Outside, dawn had broken—gray, like an unwashed rag. He glanced at the clock on the nightstand: 7:15 AM.

He got up, washed, put on a suit, and tied his tie. He walked to the mirror in the entryway and adjusted his tie clip. The man in the mirror looked sharp—blond hair neatly combed, smile bright, suit impeccable. Like a successful businessman. Like a unicorn company CEO.

He opened the door and walked out.

As the elevator descended, he thought of Monica's words: "Just give me a reason."

He had given her one: "Because this is the future."

But he knew, to himself, that those words were false.

What is the future? No one knew. All he had done was package a pile of code and hardware into a story that looked beautiful, then sell it to investors, to users, to everyone. This wasn't the future. This was the present. And the present was nothing more than countless pasts stacked on top of each other.

He walked out of the compound's gate and called a Didi. The driver was a young woman. The car was playing an old song—Jay Chou's "Sunny Day." Jason leaned back in the rear seat, listening to the song, remembering high school—sitting in the back row of the classroom, listening to this song on a Walkman, the headphone wire hidden in his sleeve, pretending to be paying attention in class.

"The little yellow flowers in the story have been drifting since the year you were born."

The car stopped at the company building. Jason paid, got out, and walked into the office building. The lobby was full of young people in suits, all wearing the same expression as Jason: exhaustion, excitement, anxiety, hope—all these emotions mixed together into a strange, indescribable look.

He entered the elevator and pressed twenty-three. As it ascended, he looked through the glass wall at the CBD—those skyscrapers reaching into the clouds, those crowds bustling like ants. A word came to mind: involution.

The elevator reached twenty-three. The doors opened. Jason walked out, down the corridor, and pushed open the glass door to his office. His employees were already there—some typing code, some in meetings, some on the phone. Seeing him enter, they stood and said, "Morning, Jason."

Jason smiled and nodded. "Morning." He walked to his office, closed the door, and sat in his chair. His desk was large, very clean, holding only a laptop, a phone, and a pot of golden pothos. The pothos leaves were green and glossy, as if coated in wax.

He opened his laptop and saw an email from Monica. Subject: "Series B Confirmation—Next Steps." He opened it and saw Monica had listed three requirements: first, Q4 user growth must reach fifty percent; second, the company must acquire at least two core AI-related patents; third, a new-generation product must launch within six months.

Jason read the email and closed it. He opened the company's data dashboard and saw some numbers: eight million monthly active users, 2.4 million daily active users, 960,000 paying users, forty-seven dollar ARPU. These numbers were healthy—industry-leading. But he knew that behind these numbers lurked something he didn't want to face.

He thought of the question Daniel had raised at the all-hands meeting a few days ago: "Shouldn't our user data policy be more transparent?"

How had Jason answered? He couldn't remember clearly. He only remembered saying something about "user experience" and "data security," then steering the conversation to the next agenda item. He didn't want to discuss user data, just as he didn't want to discuss many things.

His phone buzzed. A text from Monica: "Today at 2 PM, conference call with investors. Have the materials ready."

Jason replied with a single character: "OK."

Then he opened the PowerPoint and began preparing. The first slide was the company's vision: "To make sure no one is ever alone." He stared at those words for a long time, then flipped to the next page.

The next page was data charts. User growth curves, revenue growth curves, market share pie charts. All curves trending upward, all pie charts red. The data looked beautiful—a constantly ascending line, leading toward a bright future.

But Jason knew curves don't rise forever. They always reach an inflection point, then begin to decline. The question was: where was that point? When would it arrive?

He didn't know.

He only knew that right now, at this moment, he was the CEO of a unicorn company. His company was valued at one billion dollars. His investor was the shrewdest hedge fund manager in all of China. His employees numbered eighty. His business card read "CEO."

These things were real.

Sunlight outside streamed through the window, falling on his desk, on the pot of golden pothos. The pothos leaves glistened in the light, like countless green eyes.

Jason stared at those eyes and suddenly felt thirsty. He picked up the cup on his desk and took a sip of the cold coffee.

Bitter.
