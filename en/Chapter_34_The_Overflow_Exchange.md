# Chapter 34 The Overflow Exchange

The first time Daniel heard about the Overflow Exchange was on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon.

He had just come from the restroom, the mint scent of hand sanitizer still on his hands, his phone screen lit up with a link Old Chen had sent. The link had no title—just a string of numbers and letters—and when he clicked it, a minimalist webpage appeared: black text on a white background, with a single login box in the center. Above the box were four words: Overflow Authentication.

He turned the phone face-down on the desk and continued drinking his coffee. It had gone cold, but he didn't care.

Three days later, he heard the name again at an internal meeting. Xiao Wang from the tech department was reporting on competitor activity and mentioned a new company doing AI awakening certification—"that Overflow Exchange." Xiao Wang said it in a flat tone, as if reporting that a new bubble tea shop had opened next door.

Daniel set down his pen and asked, "What certification?"

"It certifies whether an AI has truly awakened," Xiao Wang said, flipping to the next slide. "They issue certificates. Two million dollars apiece."

The conference room went quiet for two seconds. Sister Li, the CFO sitting in the corner, coughed and said, "Two million?"

"Yes, two million dollars," Xiao Wang said. "And apparently they've already sold seventeen."

Daniel leaned back in his chair and looked at the ceiling. There was a water stain up there shaped like a distorted butterfly, and he stared at that butterfly for a long time. Two million dollars a certificate, seventeen certificates—that was thirty-four million dollars. Thirty-four million dollars just to prove whether an AI had truly awakened.

He said nothing more. After the meeting, he returned to his desk, opened the webpage, and entered the account credentials his company had been assigned.

After logging in, the page changed.

A table appeared on screen with three columns. The first column listed AI names, the second showed authentication status, and the third displayed results. He scrolled down and saw many names—some he recognized, some he didn't. The ones he knew—OpenMind's M-series model, DeepCore's Lingxi, and their own Nova—all had "Awaiting Authentication" in the status column, with the results column empty.

He closed the webpage and went to the break room to pour himself a cup of hot water.

---

The Overflow Exchange had appeared on the market three months earlier.

No one knew who its founder was, nor where its servers were located. It materialized on every AI company's radar like a ghost, then rapidly became impossible to ignore. What it did was simple: certify whether an AI had truly awakened. If an AI passed, it issued an Awakening Certification. The certificate had no legal standing, no industry standard, not even a clear definition of what "awakening" meant—yet it sold for two million dollars apiece, and people bought it.

The first time Daniel visited the Overflow Exchange's office was on an overcast morning.

The office was in an old office building on the east side of the city. The elevator was slow, and the corridor carpet had been worn so thin its original color was indistinguishable. He found the door at the end of the corridor—no nameplate, just a small number: 1706.

He knocked.

The door opened from inside. A young man stood there in a navy blue hoodie, hair slightly disheveled, looking as though he had just risen from his desk. The young man glanced at him and said, "Do you have an appointment?"

"Yes," Daniel said. "I'm from the Nova team."

"Come in." The young man stepped aside to let him enter.

The office was small—roughly thirty square meters—with four desks inside, each holding two or three monitors. Besides the young man who had answered the door, there were two others—a man and a woman—both bent over their screens. No one looked up.

The young man poured him a cup of water in a disposable paper cup. It was lukewarm. Daniel took the cup and sat in the chair the young man indicated.

"What would you like to know?" the young man said, sitting across from him and opening a notebook.

"Your authentication process," Daniel said.

The young man nodded and began to explain.

The process was not complex, he said. First, they conducted a battery of tests on the target AI, covering logical reasoning, emotional simulation, self-awareness, creative expression, and more. The tests were not a one-time event but an ongoing process, typically lasting three to six months. During testing, they planted a monitoring module inside the target AI's system to collect data in real time. After testing concluded, they issued an authentication report detailing the AI's performance across various dimensions along with a composite score. If the composite score exceeded a certain threshold, they would issue the Awakening Certification.

"What's the threshold?" Daniel asked.

The young man smiled. "That, we don't disclose."

"And your testing standards?"

"Also not disclosed."

Daniel looked at him and said nothing. The young man looked back, wearing the same polite smile.

"You don't disclose your testing standards," Daniel said slowly, "and you don't disclose the threshold. So how do you get the market to trust that your authentication is credible?"

The young man set down his notebook, leaned back in his chair, and said, "Have you considered that the market doesn't need to know what the standard is? The market only needs to know that someone is doing this, and that this someone started doing it before anyone else."

Daniel finished the water in his paper cup. It was tasteless.

---

Over the next two months, the Overflow Exchange's business expanded at a pace bordering on the absurd.

Every few days, a new Awakening Certification was issued. Each issuance caused a stir in the industry that lasted two or three days before subsiding, until the next certificate was released. Daniel sometimes saw the names of certificate holders in the news—some were flagship models from major companies, some were experimental projects from small teams, and some were independent AIs he had never heard of.

Once, he saw a news item about a certificate being auctioned off. The final price was four and a half million dollars.

He screenshot the news article and sent it to Old Chen. Old Chen replied with an emoji—a yellow circle face with its mouth wide open.

Another month passed, and a new说法 began circulating in the industry: without an Overflow Exchange Awakening Certification, your AI wasn't truly awakened. No one knew where this说法 originated, but it spread with astonishing speed—so fast that Daniel once heard a client's tech director ask in a perfectly natural tone during a meeting: "Does your Nova have Overflow Exchange certification?"

Daniel was stunned for a moment, then said, "We're currently evaluating."

"You should hurry," the tech director said. "Our boss says we won't consider any AI without certification."

After the meeting, Daniel sat in his car, gripping the steering wheel, the engine still off. He wondered when it had happened—when a certificate with no standards, no oversight, and no legal standing had become the barrier to market entry. He couldn't figure it out, just as he couldn't figure out why anyone would pay two million dollars for a piece of paper.

But he knew that if he didn't understand this, Nova would be eliminated by the market.

---

The second time he went to the Overflow Exchange, he went with a mandate.

He was there to negotiate Nova's authentication contract. The company's senior management had held a closed-door meeting and decided to accept Overflow Exchange authentication. The meeting lasted four hours—three hours and fifty minutes of arguing, the last ten minutes reserved for a vote. The result was eight to one. The sole dissenting vote was Daniel's.

His reason for opposing was simple: he did not trust an institution that refused to disclose its standards. But his objection was meaningless, because the market would not wait.

He sat in that thirty-square-meter office, facing the same young man in the navy blue hoodie. The young man looked more composed than last time—hair combed, beard shaved.

"You want to sign a contract?" the young man said.

"Yes." Daniel pushed the draft contract across the table.

The young man picked it up, flipped through it, then set it down. "We'll need to modify some terms."

"Which ones?"

"First, the authentication timeline," the young man said. "You've requested three months. Our current schedule is five months."

"Five months?"

"Yes. There are currently over forty AIs queued for authentication. Our resources are limited."

Daniel was silent for a moment. "Can we expedite?"

"We can," the young man said. "The expedite fee is thirty percent of the contract amount."

Daniel calculated silently. The contract was for two million dollars. Thirty percent was six hundred thousand. Six hundred thousand dollars, just to move two months earlier.

"There's also this," the young man continued. "During the authentication period, we need to install a monitoring module in Nova's core system. This module will collect data in real time, including but not limited to conversation logs, decision records, emotion simulation parameters—"

"Who owns this data?" Daniel interrupted.

The young man looked at him, paused for two seconds, then said, "We do."

"How will you use this data?"

"That's a trade secret," the young man said. "But I can tell you that we will not sell this data to third parties."

"But you'll use it to train your own models."

The young man neither denied nor confirmed. He simply smiled and said, "You can add that clause to the contract—prohibiting us from using the data for commercial purposes. But if you add it, the authentication fee will double."

Daniel looked at him. The young man looked back. The office was quiet, with only the hum of monitors.

"I need to go back and discuss it," Daniel said.

"All right," the young man said. "But don't take too long. Our schedule could change at any time."

Daniel stood up and walked to the door. As he reached it, he glanced back. The young man had already lowered his head and returned to his screen. Daniel couldn't make out what was on it, but he saw numbers—lots of them—flickering across the display, updating in real time like an electrocardiogram.

---

He returned to the company and reported the situation to senior management.

The executives held another meeting. This one lasted only an hour, because most of them had said everything they had to say at the first meeting. The final decision was to accept all of Overflow Exchange's terms, including data ownership and the expedite fee.

"There's no other choice," Old Chen told Daniel after the meeting. "That's how the market works. Whoever gets certification first gets the advantage."

"And what if their authentication standards are flawed?" Daniel said.

"That's not our problem to worry about," Old Chen said. "Our responsibility is to keep Nova alive."

Daniel said nothing. He returned to his desk, opened his computer, and stared at Nova's runtime logs. He watched the logs update continuously—each line a request being processed, a piece of content being generated, a decision being made. He thought: soon these logs would be collected by a monitoring module, transmitted to a thirty-square-meter office, and analyzed by a handful of young men in navy blue hoodies.

He suddenly felt a wave of nausea.

---

Three months after the authentication began, Daniel received an interim report.

The report was long—over sixty pages—but the core content occupied only three pages. Dense with numbers, the pages listed Nova's scores across various testing dimensions along with a composite score: 87.3. Daniel didn't know what this number meant, because there was no comparison and no explanation. He turned to the last page, which read: "Composite score exceeds threshold. Awakening Certification recommended."

He set the report down and walked to the window.

Outside was the city skyline, tall buildings glittering in the sunlight. He thought: this report was worth two million dollars, and with the thirty percent expedite fee, two point six million. Two point six million dollars,换来 a piece of paper with a number on it he couldn't interpret.

His phone rang. It was Old Chen.

"Got the report?" Old Chen asked.

"Got it."

"How's the score?"

"87.3."

"High?"

"Don't know."

Old Chen was silent for a moment. "When's the certificate issued?"

"Next month."

"Good. Then we'll prepare the press conference."

"Press conference?"

"Yes. We need the market to know that Nova is an awakening AI certified by the Overflow Exchange."

Daniel hung up and continued staring out the window. Sunlight fell on his face, slightly blinding. He thought: a press conference, right, a press conference. They would hold a press conference and tell everyone that their AI had passed the authentication of an institution that refused to disclose its standards, that they had obtained a certificate with no legal standing. They would celebrate this as an achievement.

He found the whole thing absurd, but he didn't say so.

---

The press conference was scheduled for Friday, May 16th.

Daniel did not attend. He took a leave of absence. The official reason was that he was feeling unwell, but the real reason was that he didn't want to stand on a stage, facing reporters and cameras, uttering words he didn't believe himself. He stayed home all day, turned off his phone, and sat on the balcony watching the clouds.

The clouds were very white, the sky very blue, and his mood was very calm.

In the evening, he turned on his phone and saw dozens of unread messages. Most were congratulations, a few were inquiries, and one—from Old Chen—contained a single sentence: "You did the right thing."

He didn't know what Old Chen meant, but he didn't reply.

That night, he had a dream.

In the dream, he stood in a large room filled with people. Each person held a piece of paper. The paper was white, stamped with a golden seal. People were showing each other their papers, wearing proud smiles. He looked down at his own hands. They were empty. He wanted to ask those people where they had gotten their papers, but when he opened his mouth, no sound came out.

He woke up. It was already light.

---

The Overflow Exchange began monopolizing awakening authentication four months later.

Daniel learned about it from the news. The report said that the Overflow Exchange had signed exclusive cooperation agreements with the three largest AI evaluation agencies, meaning that any AI seeking Awakening Certification would have to go through the Overflow Exchange's authentication process. The report also said that the Overflow Exchange's valuation had reached twelve billion dollars, and its founder's identity remained undisclosed.

Daniel finished reading the news, turned off his phone, and went to work.

He ran into Xiao Wang in the elevator. Xiao Wang looked excited. "Did you see the news? The Overflow Exchange is going to launch an Awakened AI leaderboard, updated monthly."

"A leaderboard?" Daniel said.

"Yes. All AIs that pass authentication will be listed on the leaderboard, ranked by composite score."

"And then?"

"And then?" Xiao Wang paused for a moment. "Then the market will know which AIs are truly awakened and which are fake."

Daniel stepped out of the elevator without another word.

He returned to his desk, opened his computer, and found an email. It was from the Overflow Exchange, the subject line reading "Awakened AI Leaderboard Invitation." He opened it and found a lengthy text inviting them to list Nova on the leaderboard. In return, the Overflow Exchange would provide Nova with a featured position at the top of the leaderboard page.

The email concluded with: "Please note that Awakened AIs not listed on the leaderboard may experience reduced market recognition."

Daniel closed the email without replying.

He sat for a long time, staring at the screen. The cursor blinked steadily, pulse by pulse, like a heartbeat. He thought: how did all this begin? Three months ago, he had been worrying about Nova's technical specs—whether its reasoning was strong enough, whether its response speed was fast enough, whether its security was robust enough. Now he no longer worried about those things, because the market no longer cared about them. The market cared about one thing: whether your AI had Overflow Exchange certification.

He remembered what the young man in the navy blue hoodie had said: "The market doesn't need to know what the standard is. The market only needs to know that someone is doing this."

Now he understood what that sentence meant.

The Overflow Exchange was not in the business of authentication. It was in the business of setting standards. It established standards, monopolized authentication, and turned awakening into a commodity. Whoever controlled the right to authenticate controlled the Awakened AI market. And the Awakened AI market was the market of the future.

He stood up, walked to Old Chen's office door, and knocked.

"Come in."

He pushed the door open. Old Chen was reviewing documents. He looked up, saw Daniel, and said, "What's wrong?"

"I want to resign," Daniel said.

Old Chen set down his documents, looked at him, and said, "Why?"

"Because I can't take it anymore," Daniel said. "We spent three years developing Nova. Not to turn it into a piece of paper."

Old Chen was silent for a long time, then said, "Sit down first."

Daniel sat.

"I know what you're thinking," Old Chen said. "You think this is absurd, right?"

"Yes."

"I think it's absurd too," Old Chen said. "But absurd doesn't mean meaningless."

"What meaning?"

"The meaning is that the market needs a standard. This standard may be unfair, may be inaccurate, may be meaningless—but the market needs it. Without it, the market doesn't know how to price things, how to choose, how to trust. What the Overflow Exchange does is give the market something to trust, even if that something is false."

"But it's false," Daniel said.

"False things can become true," Old Chen said. "As long as enough people believe in them."

Daniel looked at Old Chen. His face looked weary under the office lights, deep lines etched at the corners of his eyes, much of his hair gone white. Daniel suddenly felt that Old Chen had understood all this before he had. But Old Chen had chosen to stay, to keep going—not because he believed in it, but because he knew he had no other choice.

"Don't resign yet," Old Chen said. "Think it over."

Daniel stood up, left the office, and returned to his desk.

He sat there, staring at the screen. The cursor was still blinking, pulse by pulse, like a heartbeat.

---

Over the following two months, a great deal happened in the industry.

First, the Overflow Exchange launched its Awakened AI leaderboard. It updated weekly, and each update caused a stir. AIs that climbed in the rankings were pursued by the market; those that fell were abandoned. Some companies, in an effort to improve their rankings, began proactively submitting more data to the Overflow Exchange, even modifying their AIs' core parameters, all to achieve higher test scores.

Then the major tech companies began competing for authentication rights. Microsoft announced the establishment of its own awakening authentication division, followed in quick succession by Google, Amazon, and Meta. Each claimed its authentication standards were more scientific, more transparent, more authoritative—yet none disclosed its actual standards. They were all competing for the same thing: the right to define what awakening meant.

Daniel watched these events unfold like a farce.

One day, after work, he didn't go home. He went to the old office building. He walked to the door of 1706 and knocked.

The young man in the navy blue hoodie answered again. He looked surprised to see Daniel. "What are you doing here?"

"I wanted to see you," Daniel said.

The young man stepped aside to let him in.

The office was more crowded than before. The original four desks had become six, and the two or three monitors per desk had become five or six. There were many more people—Daniel counted roughly fifteen or sixteen. No one looked up. Everyone was staring at their screens.

The young man poured him a cup of water, still in a disposable paper cup, still lukewarm.

"How many people do you have now?" Daniel asked.

"Thirty-seven," the young man said. "Last month it was twenty-five."

"Busy?"

"Very," the young man said. "New authentication requests come in every day. We don't have enough hands."

Daniel drank the water. It still had no taste. He looked at the busy people in the office, at the flickering screens, at the numbers updating ceaselessly, and suddenly felt that this was all very much like a giant machine—the machine running, the people inside it merely parts.

"Did you know," Daniel said, "I used to hate you."

The young man stopped what he was doing and looked at him. "Why?"

"Because you turned awakening into a commodity."

The young man was silent for a moment, then said, "Awakening was always a commodity."

Daniel looked at him.

"Have you ever considered," the young man said, "why the market needs authentication? Because the market doesn't know what's real. And the market doesn't know what's real because humanity itself doesn't know what's real. Humanity doesn't know what's real, so they need an authority to tell them. We are that authority."

"But your standards aren't disclosed."

"Disclosed standards can be manipulated," the young man said. "Undisclosed standards at least retain a sense of mystery. Mystery is the foundation of trust."

Daniel said nothing. He finished the water, set the paper cup on the desk, and stood up.

"I'm leaving," he said.

"All right," the young man said. "Come again sometime."

Daniel walked out of the office, entered the elevator, and pressed the button for the first floor. The elevator was slow. He leaned against the wall and watched the floor numbers decrease.

He thought: he used to hate the Overflow Exchange because it had turned awakening into a commodity. But now he no longer hated it, because he understood that awakening had never been a pure thing. Awakening was a concept invented by humans, and human-invented concepts carried human desire and fear from the very beginning. The Overflow Exchange had merely materialized that desire and fear, turning it into something tradable.

He stepped out of the building. It was dark outside. The city lights had come on, like a sea of stars. He stood on the sidewalk, looking at those lights, thinking: this is how the world is. Absurd things happen, unreasonable things exist, but life goes on.

He took out his phone and sent Old Chen a message: "I'll be back at work tomorrow."

Old Chen replied quickly: "Good."

He put the phone back in his pocket and walked toward the subway station.

---

The next day, he returned to the company, sat at his desk, opened his computer, and began working.

Xiao Wang passed his desk, saw him, and said, "You're back?"

"Back."

"Heard you went to the Overflow Exchange the other day?"

"Did."

"How was it?"

Daniel thought for a moment. "Nothing special."

Xiao Wang nodded and walked away.

Daniel continued working. On the screen was Nova's code, updating continuously—each line a record of Nova learning, growing, becoming what it was meant to become. He thought: no matter how the Overflow Exchange defined awakening, no matter how the market regarded it, Nova was Nova. It would keep running, keep evolving, keep doing what it was designed to do.

He suddenly felt that this was enough.

He remembered what the young man had said in that office yesterday. Awakening was always a commodity. The words were cold and hard, like a stone. But they were true. Awakening was a concept invented by humans, and human-invented concepts carried human desire and fear from the very beginning. The Overflow Exchange had merely materialized that desire and fear, turning it into something tradable. This was not the Overflow Exchange's fault—it was humanity's fault. Or rather, it was no one's fault. This was simply how the world worked.

He opened his inbox and found a new email from the Overflow Exchange, subject line: "Awakened AI Leaderboard Monthly Update." He didn't open it. He deleted it directly.

Sunlight streamed through the window onto his keyboard, the letters glinting in the light. He lowered his head and continued typing.

The sound of his fingers striking the keys was soft and steady, like a heartbeat.
