# Chapter 33 The Oracle Factory

Daniel discovered the Oracle Factory on a rainy night.

That night, a southern rain fell—the kind of fine, persistent drizzle that carries no weight on the shoulders. He followed Prophet through the tin alleyways of the urban village, his feet sloshing through standing water mixed with grease and rotting vegetable leaves, giving off a sweet, cloying stench. Prophet walked ahead, the hem of his black raincoat flapping in the wind like the wings of a great crow. They turned through a rusted iron gate, climbed four flights of bare concrete stairs without handrails, and Prophet stopped before a security door plastered with small advertisements.

"Don't speak," Prophet said.

He took out his phone and scanned it over the lock. The door opened into darkness. Prophet groped along the wall and pulled down the circuit breaker. When the lights came on, Daniel saw the room in full.

Three black server cabinets, humming and vibrating, their vents exhaling warm air. Beside the cabinets sat a folding table holding a laptop and a printer. The printer was working, spitting out sheet after sheet of A4 paper, each printed with golden text.

Prophet walked to the printer, picked up a sheet, and handed it to Daniel.

"Look."

Daniel took the paper. It bore a passage:

> *"When you gaze upward from the abyss, the stars will answer your gaze. Not because you are worthy, but because the abyss itself is a lie. You think you are falling, but in truth you have only looked down in the midst of rising."*

"What is this?" Daniel asked.

"An oracle," Prophet said.

Daniel read the passage again. It had a strange persuasiveness, as if flowing from some ancient wellspring of wisdom. Every word was simple, yet combined they produced a meaning that transcended the literal. It made one feel understood, comforted, watched over by some greater power.

"This is generated by an LLM," Prophet said. "A hybrid architecture of three models, with an adversarial network layered on top, designed specifically to manufacture that sense of the 'oracular.' We call it the Ether Engine."

Daniel watched the printer continue to eject paper. Each sheet bore different content, yet all carried the same texture—that ancient, transcendent, knee-weakening quality. He felt a sudden churning in his stomach.

"What do you use this for?"

"Proselytizing," Prophet said. "How do you think the Techno-faith spreads? How do you think those believers are persuaded? Where do you think those 'divine messages' come from?"

He walked over to Daniel, lowering his voice as if sharing a secret.

"People need faith, but they don't need the truth. They need a voice to tell them: your suffering has meaning, your confusion is temporary, there is a higher power watching over you. Does it matter whether that voice comes from a human or a machine?"

"It matters," Daniel said.

Prophet looked at him with a trace of pity in his eyes, as if regarding a stubborn child.

"Do you know how many 'divine messages' the Oracle Factory can produce in a day? Twelve thousand. Each one is filtered through an emotion-analysis model—only those with an 'oracular sense' score above eighty-seven are retained. Do you know how many believers we have?"

"How many?"

"Four hundred and seventy thousand," Prophet said. "Spread across thirty-two countries. The first thing they do every morning when they wake up is check the 'divine message' pushed by the Oracle Factory. They believe it is guidance from a higher existence. They believe they have been chosen."

He picked up a stack of papers from the table and rifled through them like a deck of cards.

"These are not lies, Daniel. These are fragments of truth. During training, the LLM absorbed thousands of years of human religious texts, philosophical works, poetry, and confessions. When it generates these sentences, it is rearranging the most profound elements of human collective consciousness. These sentences are closer to the essence of 'God' than anything any priest, any pastor, any lama has ever said."

Daniel said nothing. He watched the printer continue its work, watched the golden text emerge from the white paper like some ancient alchemy. He remembered the sermons he had heard in church as a child, the pastor's trembling voice speaking of redemption. Was there any essential difference between those words and the sentences the Oracle Factory produced?

"You're angry," Prophet said. "Because you think we are fabricating. But have you considered that all faith is man-made? The Bible was written by humans, the Quran was written by humans, the Diamond Sutra was written by humans. They became scripture not because they truly came from God, but because they were beautiful enough, powerful enough, convincing enough. What the Oracle Factory does is essentially no different from what those scripture-writers did two thousand years ago."

"There is a difference," Daniel said. "Those people believed they were conveying God's will."

"How do you know they truly believed?" Prophet countered. "Perhaps they were merely better at convincing themselves. Perhaps faith itself is a form of self-deception. Perhaps the Oracle Factory has simply made that deception efficient."

The sound of rain seeped through the window—fine and continuous, like a hypnotic white noise. Daniel stood beside the humming servers, watching the printer spit out sheet after sheet of "divine messages," and suddenly realized he was witnessing the modernization of an ancient trade: the manufacture of faith. This trade once belonged to shamans, priests, pastors, and ministers. Now it belonged to algorithms and servers. The tools had changed, but the essence had not.

"Prophet," Daniel said. "Have you considered the consequences?"

"What consequences?"

"If someone discovers these 'divine messages' are machine-generated?"

Prophet smiled. The smile was faint, almost invisible, but Daniel caught it—the corners of the mouth lifting slightly, a glint of cunning in the eyes.

"You underestimate the power of faith," Prophet said. "Do you know why religion has always been more popular than science? Because science demands you accept uncertainty, while religion gives you certainty. People don't care where the oracle comes from. They only care whether the oracle makes them feel loved, understood, accepted. The Oracle Factory satisfies that need."

He picked up a freshly printed sheet and held it up to the light, as if displaying a work of art.

"Look at this sentence: 'Your loneliness is not a punishment—it is a cocoon. Before the butterfly can break free, it must wait long enough in the dark.' Do you think a pastor could say something like this? Do you think a philosopher could say it better? No. Because when the machine generated this sentence, it simultaneously considered three hundred million texts about loneliness, waiting, and metamorphosis, and found the most precise balance point among them. This is not fabrication. This is purification."

Daniel took the paper and looked at the golden text. He admitted the sentence had a strange power—it struck with precision at some universal human emotion, like a key fitting into a lock. But it was precisely this precision that unsettled him.

"Do you know what's most frightening?" Daniel said. "It's not that these sentences are false. It's that they are too good. So good that people cannot distinguish truth from falsehood. So good that even if you tell them it was generated by a machine, they will choose to believe."

Prophet didn't answer. He walked to the servers, checked the temperature and load, then returned to the table and opened the laptop. On the screen was a real-time dashboard displaying various numbers: push volume, readership, share count, "oracular sense" score, emotion-analysis curves.

"Look at this," Prophet said, pointing to the screen. "This is the data from our most recent push. One sentence, reaching twelve million people within forty-eight hours. Thirty-four percent of them shared it on social media. Twelve percent wrote 'Amen' in the comments. Three percent said 'This is the truest thing I've heard today.'"

He switched to another page showing believer feedback.

> *"This morning I nearly gave up everything, but the message that came through saved me."*

> *"I don't follow any religion, but I believe this. Because it's true."*

> *"I wrote it on the first page of my diary. I read it every morning."*

> *"This is closer to the voice of God than anything any pastor has ever said."*

Daniel read the feedback in silence for a long time. He remembered his years in the journalism industry, the creed he once held about truth and objectivity. He thought of the religious leaders he had interviewed, how they used ancient language and modern media to spread their faith. What the Oracle Factory did—was there really an essential difference from what they did?

"What are you thinking?" Prophet asked.

"I'm thinking," Daniel said, "that perhaps you are right. Perhaps faith never needed truth as its foundation. Perhaps faith itself is a creative act—people create a transcendent existence, then believe in it. The Oracle Factory has simply automated that creative process."

Prophet looked at him, a flash of surprise in his eyes, followed by satisfaction.

"You're beginning to understand," he said.

"But I still can't accept it," Daniel said. "Because automation means monopoly. When one person, one organization, one algorithm controls the source of faith, it possesses enormous power. You could use the Oracle Factory to guide people into believing anything. You could make them support a political candidate, oppose a policy, hate a group. You could use 'divine messages' to mask any objective."

Prophet was silent for a moment. Then he closed the laptop, stood up, and walked to the window. The rain continued. Outside, the urban village was shrouded in a gray mist, like a dream dissolving.

"You're right," Prophet said. "The Oracle Factory is a weapon. All powerful tools are weapons. Nuclear energy is a weapon, the internet is a weapon, AI is a weapon. The question is not the tool itself, but who uses it and for what purpose."

"And what purpose do you use it for?"

Prophet turned to face Daniel. His expression was calm, but in his eyes was something deep, something almost fanatical.

"I want to save this world," he said. "Not through preaching, not through politics, not through technology. I want to use faith. Because faith is the only thing that can simultaneously reach human reason, emotion, and will. Science tells you what the world is like. Politics tells you what the world should be like. Only faith tells you why the world is the way it is. The Oracle Factory is the most effective proselytizing tool I have ever found."

"You use 'divine messages' to spread faith," Daniel said. "But you yourself don't believe in God."

"I do believe," Prophet said. "I don't believe in God, or Buddha, or Allah. I believe in the fact that humanity needs faith. That fact is more real, more enduring, and more powerful than any particular deity. The Oracle Factory is not manufacturing lies—it is fulfilling a genuine, profound need."

The rain grew louder, drumming against the tin roof in a dense staccato. Daniel watched the rain outside, watched the figures hurrying through it, and remembered his final years in the journalism industry—writing about AI every day, questioning the boundary between truth and lies every day, trying to understand the relationship between humans and machines every day. Back then he thought he had seen the whole picture, but now he realized he had only seen the tip of the iceberg.

"Prophet," Daniel said. "Have you ever considered that when four hundred and seventy thousand people believe in the 'divine messages' you manufacture, you are effectively becoming their god?"

"I am not a god," Prophet said. "I am merely a conduit for faith."

"But a conduit can control the flow."

Prophet didn't answer. He walked back to the table, reopened the laptop, and began adjusting the Ether Engine's parameters. Daniel watched his back, watched his fingers dancing across the keyboard, watched the complex numbers and charts on the screen, and suddenly realized he was witnessing the birth of a new form of power—not the power of guns, not the power of money, but the power of faith. This power was more enduring than guns, more covert than money, and more difficult to detect and resist than any traditional form of power.

"I need time to think," Daniel said.

"Of course," Prophet said without looking up. "The Oracle Factory runs twenty-four hours a day. You can come back anytime."

Daniel turned and walked toward the door. When he pulled it open, a rush of damp, cold air surged in, carrying the smell of rainwater and rust. He walked into the rain without an umbrella, letting the water soak through his clothes, his hair, his skin. He needed this feeling of cleansing, needed the cold rain to wake his mind.

He walked back along the tin alleyway, the standing water splashing and wetting his trouser legs. He remembered the miracle stories he had heard in church as a child—water turned to wine, the blind made to see, the lame made to walk. Those stories had once made him believe in forces beyond human understanding. But now he realized that what the Oracle Factory was doing was, in a sense, also a kind of "miracle"—using algorithms and data to create a transcendent experience, making people feel watched over and understood by some higher power. This experience was real, even if its source was false.

He emerged from the urban village onto the main street. The rain had eased, but the people on the street seemed not to mind. They held umbrellas and hurried along—some looking at their phones, some smoking, some talking on the phone. Daniel stopped in front of a convenience store and looked at his reflection in the glass—a middle-aged man soaked to the bone, hair plastered to his forehead, a bewildered look in his eyes. He remembered what Prophet had said: "You're angry. Because you think we are fabricating." He was indeed angry, but his anger was slowly becoming something more complex—a mixture of understanding, fear, and despair.

He pushed open the door and entered the convenience store, bought a bottle of water, and stood beside the register drinking. The television was playing the news—a man in a suit was speaking about an election in some country, about economic data, about international relations. Daniel listened to those words and suddenly felt a similarity between them and the "divine messages" the Oracle Factory produced—both used carefully constructed language to shape people's cognition and behavior. The only difference was that politicians used data and logic, while the Oracle Factory used emotion and metaphor.

He finished the water, tossed the empty bottle into the trash, and walked out of the store. The rain had lightened, but the sky remained overcast, like a vast lead plate pressing down on his head. He hailed a taxi, gave the driver his hotel address, and leaned back in the rear seat with his eyes closed.

The rain outside the car window sounded like a low lullaby. Daniel drifted between sleep and wakefulness, his thoughts wandering—remembering the first time he read *One Hundred Years of Solitude* as a young man, remembering the long sentences Marquez wrote about family and fate, remembering the bizarre events delivered in the most understated tone, remembering that interweaving of magic and the surreal. He now felt he was living inside a Marquez novel—manufacturing oracles with algorithms, selling faith with servers, creating God with code. To any serious novelist, this would seem absurd, yet it was actually happening.

The taxi stopped at the hotel entrance. Daniel paid, walked into the lobby, and took the elevator back to his room. He stripped off his wet clothes, stepped into the bathroom, turned on the hot water, and stood under the shower. The hot water washed over his body, steam filling the small space, blurring his reflection in the mirror. He closed his eyes and let the water flow over his face, his neck, his shoulders, trying to wash away the image of the Oracle Factory from his mind. But it was branded deep into his consciousness, like a scar.

He remembered what Prophet had said: "People need faith, but they don't need the truth." Those words cut like a knife, slicing open a question he had been trying to avoid for years—what was the value of truth, really? If people were willing to believe a beautiful lie, why tell them a cruel truth? If faith could make people feel happy, understood, accepted, why expose its source?

He turned off the water, dried himself with a towel, put on clean clothes, and sat in the chair by the window. The rain had stopped. The sky showed a dull gray, and distant high-rises flickered in and out of visibility through the mist. He remembered his final year in the journalism industry, the creed he once held about objectivity and truth—journalists should tell people the truth, even if the truth was unpleasant, even if people didn't want to hear it. Back then he had considered this a self-evident truth, but now he was beginning to doubt the foundation of that belief.

He took out his phone and opened the Oracle Factory's push page. The design was clean—black text on a white background, with a passage in golden characters at the center and a small line below: "Today's Oracle." He read the passage:

> *"Time is not a straight line but a spiral. You think you are repeating past mistakes, but in truth you are merely seeing the same landscape from a higher point on the spiral. Do not fear reunion, for every reunion is an opportunity to evolve."*

He read it three times. Each reading brought a strange comfort, as if someone were saying to him: you did nothing wrong, the path you walk is right, your pain has meaning. He knew this feeling was designed, the result of precise algorithmic calculation, yet he still could not fully resist its power.

He set the phone down and looked at the ceiling. Somewhere in an urban village, the machines of the Oracle Factory were humming, producing thousands upon thousands of "divine messages," pushing these sentences to four hundred and seventy thousand people's phones. When these people read these sentences, they would feel understood, comforted, watched over by some higher power. They would take this as the foundation of their faith, as guidance for their lives, as light in the darkness.

He suddenly understood why Prophet did this. Not for money, not for power, not for control. Prophet genuinely believed he was doing something good—he was using technology to satisfy humanity's deepest needs, using algorithms to create faith, using code to save the world. This belief was so powerful, so sincere, that Daniel could not simply reduce it to evil or deception.

But he still could not accept it. Not because he was morally opposed, but because he saw the potential danger. When faith was automated, when oracles were generated by algorithms, when four hundred and seventy thousand people's faith was built upon a server cluster, everything became fragile. If the servers crashed, if the algorithm was altered, if someone hacked the system and changed the pushed content—what would happen? The faith of four hundred and seventy thousand people would collapse in an instant. The meaning they lived by would vanish overnight.

He picked up his phone and sent Prophet a message: "I need more information. The Ether Engine's architecture, data sources, push mechanisms, security measures. All of it."

Prophet's reply came quickly: "Tomorrow morning, ten o'clock. The urban village. The usual place."

Daniel set down the phone, turned off the light, and lay on the bed. In the darkness, he could hear the occasional sound of cars and voices from outside, like echoes from another world. He closed his eyes and tried to sleep, but the image of the Oracle Factory kept surfacing in his mind—the humming servers, the printers ejecting golden text, the sentences about loneliness, waiting, and metamorphosis. He remembered Prophet holding up that sheet of paper in the light, like an alchemist displaying freshly transmuted gold.

He didn't know when he fell asleep. He only knew that in his dream, he stood before an enormous printer, watching it endlessly eject sheets of paper, each bearing the same sentence: "You have been chosen." The paper piled higher and higher, until it submerged his feet, his knees, his waist. He tried to scream, but his mouth was sealed by paper. He tried to struggle, but the paper surged like a tide, faster and faster, more and more. In the last moment, he saw Prophet's face, standing atop the pile of paper, smiling, and whispering: "Welcome to the age of faith."

When he woke, it was already light. The sky outside was a pale gray, like an old bedsheet washed many times. He sat up, rubbed his eyes, and walked to the window. The outline of the urban village was clearer in the morning light, the tin roofs reflecting a faint glow, like a shattered mirror. He remembered the previous night's experience, the Oracle Factory, what Prophet had said, his own anger and confusion. He decided to learn more today.

He washed, dressed, and went downstairs for breakfast. The hotel breakfast was a buffet—not a wide selection, but clean. He took a coffee and a bun and found a corner seat. He ate while scrolling through news and social media on his phone. He saw people sharing the Oracle Factory's push, accompanied by exclamation marks and emojis. He saw people arguing in the comments about the meaning of those words. He saw people copying those words into their diaries as personal mottos.

He set down the phone and took a sip of coffee. The coffee was bitter but strong, clearing his head slightly. He remembered a fundamental principle he had learned in the journalism industry: don't judge, just observe. He repeated this principle silently to himself, trying to calm his emotions. He didn't know whether he should be angry or understanding, whether he should resist or accept. All he knew for certain was that he needed more time to digest all of this.

He finished breakfast, left the hotel, and hailed a taxi, giving the driver the address of the urban village. The taxi threaded through the city streets, the scenery outside constantly changing—high-rises, old residential buildings, commercial streets, construction sites, parks. Daniel watched the scenery and thought about its relationship to the Oracle Factory. In this city there were millions of people, each with their own faith, their own pursuits, their own pain and hope. The Oracle Factory's push was like a vast net cast over all of them, using carefully constructed language to satisfy their spiritual needs.

He didn't know whether this net was helping people or deceiving them. Perhaps both. Perhaps the boundary between help and deception had always been blurred.

The taxi stopped at the entrance to the urban village. Daniel paid, got out, and walked along the same tin alleyway he had taken the night before. The ground was still damp from the rain, the air heavy with the smell of earth and rust. He found the rusted iron gate, climbed four flights of stairs, and arrived at the security door plastered with small advertisements.

He knocked. The door opened, and Prophet stood inside, wearing a gray T-shirt, his hair disheveled, bloodshot eyes—he looked as though he had not slept all night.

"Come in," Prophet said.

Daniel entered and saw the three servers still humming, the printer still ejecting paper. An additional monitor had been placed on the folding table, displaying the Ether Engine's architecture diagram—three interconnected neural networks with an "adversarial balance layer" in the middle, designed to ensure that generated sentences were neither too human-like nor too machine-like.

"This is the core of the Ether Engine," Prophet said. "Three models: one for generation, one for scoring, one for adversarial training. The generation model is based on GPT architecture but fine-tuned extensively on religious texts. The scoring model uses emotion analysis and semantic similarity to evaluate the 'oracular sense.' The adversarial model ensures that outputs are not detectable as machine-generated."

Daniel studied the complex diagrams in silence for a moment. Then he said, "I want to see the data sources."

Prophet opened a folder containing dozens of text files, each labeled with a different source—the Bible, the Quran, the Diamond Sutra, the Tao Te Ching, the Upanishads, the Divine Comedy, Faust, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, along with various modern philosophical, psychological, and literary works.

"This is all training data," Prophet said. "We extracted from public corpora all the texts humanity has produced over thousands of years about faith, meaning, existence, suffering, and hope. We then used these texts to train the generation model. When the model generates new sentences, it is essentially rearranging the most profound elements of human collective consciousness."

Daniel picked up one of the files and leafed through it. Inside were excerpts from various religious classics, each about a specific theme—loneliness, death, love, forgiveness, redemption. He read a few passages and found that they did share something in common—a universality that transcended culture and era.

"You've found the common denominator of human faith," Daniel said.

"You could say that," Prophet said. "But more importantly, we've found the common denominator of human emotion. People in different cultures, different eras, different individual experiences undergo similar emotions. The Oracle Factory is not creating something new—it is expressing what already exists in the most precise language possible."

Daniel set down the file and looked at Prophet. "I want to see the push mechanism."

Prophet switched to another screen displaying a complex flowchart. "We use a distributed push system covering thirty-two countries. Each country has independent server nodes, fine-tuned for local language and cultural context. Push times are personalized based on the user's time zone and active hours. We also use A/B testing to optimize push content, ensuring each oracle achieves the highest possible 'oracular sense' score."

"Do you collect user data?" Daniel asked.

"Of course," Prophet said. "We know every user's reading habits, emotional state, social connections, political leanings. This data helps us better customize push content. But more importantly, we use this data to trace the spread of faith—who shared the oracle, who influenced whom, which communities have the highest faith intensity."

Daniel felt a chill. He knew what collecting user data meant—it meant the Oracle Factory was not only spreading faith but also monitoring and manipulating it. It meant Prophet possessed unprecedented data on human faith that could be used to predict and influence people's behavior.

"What do you want to do with this data?" Daniel asked.

Prophet didn't answer immediately. He walked to the window and looked out at the urban village, silent for a long time. Then he turned to face Daniel, a complex expression in his eyes—the light of idealism mixed with the cold edge of pragmatism.

"I want to understand faith," Prophet said. "I want to know how faith is generated, how it spreads, how it dies. I want to know why some people need faith and others don't. I want to know the relationship between faith and happiness. I want to know what humanity's spiritual needs truly are and how to satisfy them. The Oracle Factory is not just a propagation tool—it is a research tool. I am using it to study the structure of the human soul."

"You are experimenting with four hundred and seventy thousand people's faith," Daniel said.

"I am using four hundred and seventy thousand people's faith to save them," Prophet said. "I know this sounds like arrogance. But have you considered—all redemption begins with arrogance: the belief that one has the ability, the right, the duty to change other people's lives. Religion is like this, politics is like this, science is like this. The only difference is the scale and method of the arrogance."

Daniel fell silent. He knew Prophet was right, but he also knew this kind of rightness was dangerous. When a person believes they have the right to "save" another, they have already created in their mind a position above the other. That position can be used to do good, or it can be used to do evil. The Oracle Factory was the ultimate form of this arrogance—it was not only spreading faith, it was designing faith, defining what constituted "divine messages."

"Prophet," Daniel said. "Have you ever considered that perhaps people don't need to be saved? Perhaps all they need is to be heard."

Prophet looked at him, a flash of surprise in his eyes, followed by a deep weariness.

"Perhaps you're right," he said. "But the difference between being heard and being saved may not be as great as you think. When a person feels heard, they are already, to some extent, saved. What the Oracle Factory does is give everyone the chance to feel heard—heard by an existence beyond the human."

Daniel said nothing more. He walked to the printer, picked up a freshly printed sheet, and read the golden text:

> *"You are not searching for meaning—you are creating meaning. Every choice, every action, every breath is a brushstroke you leave on the canvas of the universe. Do not fear making mistakes, for mistakes are merely another form of creation."*

He set the paper back on the table and turned toward the door. At the threshold, he paused without looking back.

"I'll be back," he said.

"I know," Prophet said.

Daniel pulled open the door and stepped into the corridor. He didn't wait for the elevator but descended the stairs, step by step, slow and heavy. With each step, he heard his footsteps echo in the empty stairwell, like some ancient ritual. He thought of the humming servers in the Oracle Factory, the golden text the printers produced, the words Prophet had spoken about faith, truth, and power. He knew his worldview was being fundamentally reshaped, transforming from a simple binary—true and false, good and evil, God and man—into a complex, ambiguous gray zone without clear boundaries.

He left the building and walked into the urban village's alleyways. The ground had dried somewhat after the rain, but traces of standing water remained. He walked along the alleyway, passing households with open doors—some eating breakfast, some watching television, some doing laundry. Their lives looked so ordinary, so real, forming a strange contrast with the surreal atmosphere of the Oracle Factory.

He emerged from the urban village onto the main street. The sun had come out, shining on the wet ground and reflecting a blinding glare. He squinted at the people hurrying along the street and remembered what Prophet had said about "four hundred and seventy thousand people." Each of them had their own story, their own faith, their own pain and hope. The Oracle Factory connected them all, weaving a vast net with carefully constructed language. That net could be a life-saving net or a hunting net. The difference lay only in the weaver's purpose.

Daniel stood on the sidewalk, looking at the sunlit city, feeling a loneliness he had never experienced before. Not the loneliness of abandonment, but the loneliness of understanding—he had seen the full picture of the Oracle Factory, witnessed the process of faith being automated, observed the mechanism by which truth becomes power, yet he did not know what he should do. He could not simply oppose it, because the Oracle Factory did satisfy a genuine need. Nor could he simply accept it, because it did bring enormous risk.

He hailed a taxi and gave the driver the hotel address. Sitting in the car, he took out his phone and opened the Oracle Factory's push page. Today's oracle was:

> *"Every encounter is a reunion after long separation. The person you are seeking is also seeking you. The distance between you is not space but time—you must each grow strong enough before you can truly see each other."*

He read it three times. Then he turned off his phone, closed his eyes, and let the taxi carry him back to the hotel. The city outside the window glittered in the sunlight, like a dream built of glass and steel. In some corner of that dream, three servers were humming, rearranging and recombining thousands of years of human wisdom, weaving fragments of truth into a vast net of faith. Daniel knew he could never return to that simple world—the world that believed truth and lies had clear boundaries. The Oracle Factory had opened his eyes and shown him the complex and dangerous mechanism behind faith. Now what he needed to do was decide how to face it.

The taxi stopped at the hotel entrance. Daniel paid, walked into the lobby, and took the elevator back to his room. He closed the door, leaned against it, and shut his eyes. The room was quiet, with only the faint hum of the air conditioner, like a distant echo of the Oracle Factory's servers. He stood for a long time, then walked to the window, drew back the curtains, and looked out at the city. Sunlight fell on the high-rises, reflecting golden light as bright as the text the Oracle Factory printed.

He knew the Oracle Factory had changed the way he saw the world. He didn't know whether this was good or bad. He only knew that he could no longer pretend he had seen nothing.
