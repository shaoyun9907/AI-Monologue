# Chapter 93: Grey's Knowledge

The first time Daniel met Grey, the man was sitting in the library of the abandoned university on the west side of the Grey Zone, reading a book without a cover. Outside the window, a grey rain was falling, the water streaming through the broken glass and pooling in small puddles on the floor. Grey did not look up. He sat on a wooden chair, its legs propped on a stack of old magazines that had swollen with moisture, used to keep it level. His suit was grey — the same grey as the rain outside. His hair was grey too, more white than black, combed into a neat pompadour, though a few strands had fallen loose across his glasses.

Daniel stood at the doorway for a while. He was not sure whether he should go in. The Grey Zone was not a place that concerned itself with politeness, but a library was not the place for an abrupt intrusion either. He leaned against the doorframe and rapped his knuckles against the half-rotten wooden door.

Grey turned a page.

"Come in and sit," he said, his voice flat, as though he were remarking on something that had happened many times before.

Daniel stepped inside. The library was dark, lit only by the grey light that filtered through the broken windows. Bookshelves leaned against the walls at crooked angles; most of the books had been soaked烂 by the rain or burned for firewood. The air carried the smell of damp paper and mold — the scent of an old era's inheritance. Daniel found a chair and sat down. It creaked beneath him.

"How long have you been here?" Daniel asked.

Grey closed the book and placed it on his lap. His fingers were long, his nails neatly trimmed — a rarity in the Grey Zone. "Longer than you think," he said. Then he raised his eyes to look at Daniel. His eyes were light grey, the same color as this district. "And shorter than you think."

Daniel did not understand the remark, but he did not press further. Stay in the Grey Zone long enough and you learn not to ask too many questions, because the answers are往往 more confusing than the questions.

"What's your name?" Daniel asked.

"Grey."

"Just Grey?"

"A name is just a label," Grey said. "Call me whatever you like. But everyone calls me Grey."

"Everyone?"

"Not many," Grey said. "But there are some."

This was the first time Daniel had heard that there were other people in the Grey Zone. He had been here for quite some time and had seen all sorts — the abandoned, the forgotten, those who had chosen to vanish. But they all moved alone, like islands separated by grey sea. No one had ever used the word "everyone."

"What are you waiting for?" Daniel asked.

Grey opened the book again. "You," he said.

Daniel laughed once. "You didn't know I was coming."

"I didn't know you would come," Grey said. "But I knew someone would. That is what waiting means — you don't know who will come, but you know someone will."

Daniel looked at him. The man had a peculiar way of speaking — each sentence was like a circle, departing from one point, circling around, and returning to where it began. But it was not the contrived circling of someone being mysterious. It was natural, like water flowing around a stone.

"What did you do?" Daniel asked.

"Teaching," Grey said. "Philosophy."

"Where?"

"At a university, once," Grey said. "Then the university was gone. Then the city was gone. Then many things were gone. But knowledge remains."

He patted the book on his lap. "Knowledge is the only thing that never truly disappears."

Daniel said nothing. He had met too many people who claimed to possess knowledge — those who sold information, sold stories, sold hope. In the Grey Zone, knowledge was a currency, but its value depended on who was selling and who was buying.

"I don't buy information," Daniel said.

"I know," Grey said. "That is why I waited for you."

---

Grey's quarters were upstairs from the library, a small room that had once been a professor's office. It was clean, unlike the rest of the Grey Zone. An old rug lay on the floor, and in the corner sat a camp bed with a grey blanket folded on it. Cardboard boxes lined the wall, filled with books. On the desk stood an oil lamp, beside it a stack of notebooks bound with string.

Daniel followed Grey up the stairs. The staircase was narrow, and each step produced a creak, like stepping on the bones of some animal. Grey walked slowly, one hand braced against the wall, the other holding the oil lamp. His movements were steady, but the marks of age were visible — each step carried a calculated caution, as though he were conserving his strength.

The room was dim. Grey set the oil lamp on the desk. The flame flickered once, then steadied, casting the entire room in a dim amber glow. Grey sat on the camp bed and gestured for Daniel to take the chair by the desk.

"What do you want to know?" Grey asked.

Daniel thought for a moment. "How did you come to the Grey Zone?"

"The same as everyone," Grey said. "I was walking, and eventually I arrived."

"No," Daniel said. "Most people come to the Grey Zone because they have nowhere else to go. But you don't look like someone with nowhere to go."

Grey smiled faintly. The smile was so slight the corners of his mouth barely moved, but a light entered his eyes, like the flame of the oil lamp reflected in his pale grey lenses.

"You observe carefully," he said. "That is good."

"Answer my question."

"I came to the Grey Zone because I had something to pass on," Grey said. "I waited here a long time, for the right person."

"Pass on what?"

"Knowledge," Grey said. "Not information, not stories, not hope. Real knowledge."

"What's the difference?"

Grey rose, walked to the cardboard boxes, and drew out a book. It was old, its cover barely legible, but it was clearly a hardcover, once deep blue. He handed it to Daniel.

Daniel took it. The book was lighter than he expected, as though the pages had dried completely, having lost all moisture. He opened to the first page. On it was handwritten text, written in fountain pen, the ink faded but still legible. The script was neat, as though printed.

"What is this?" Daniel asked.

"These are my notes," Grey said. "What I spent forty years recording. Not facts, not data. Understanding."

"Understanding what?"

"Understanding people," Grey said. "Understanding how people live, how they die, and how they find meaning in the space between living and dying."

Daniel closed the book and placed it on his lap. He could feel its weight — not physical weight, but something deeper. As though this book carried a responsibility that must be passed on.

"I don't understand," Daniel said.

"You will," Grey said. "That is why I waited for you."

---

The next day, Grey began teaching Daniel.

They sat in the library, in the same position as before. Grey took more notebooks from the cardboard boxes and stacked them one by one on the desk. The covers had yellowed, but the writing inside was still clear. Daniel counted: twelve in total.

"Forty years," Grey said. "Averaging one every three years. Sometimes faster, sometimes slower."

"When did you start writing?"

"At twenty-five," Grey said. "When I was still a graduate student."

"Writing about what?"

"About what I observed," Grey said. "I taught philosophy, but I didn't believe in philosophy."

"Then what did you believe in?"

"I believed in observation," Grey said. "Philosophy is thinking, but thinking is easily deceived. You think you are thinking, but you are merely repeating what others have told you. Observation is different. You see what a person is doing, he tells you what he is doing, and then you discover that what he said and what he did are different. That is the starting point of knowledge."

He opened the first notebook, turned to a certain page, and pushed it in front of Daniel. On the page was a table: a column of names on the left, a column of descriptions on the right. Beside each name was a check mark or an X; some names were circled, some crossed out.

"What is this?" Daniel asked.

"Every student I ever taught," Grey said. "Two hundred and thirty-seven in total."

Daniel looked at the names. Most he did not recognize. Some were written in unusual styles, like a kind of code.

"What were you recording about them?"

"The difference between what they said and what they did," Grey said. "You think you are teaching knowledge, but you are teaching lies. Not deliberate lies — inevitable lies. Because once knowledge is spoken, it is no longer knowledge. It becomes language. And language is never the thing itself that it describes."

Daniel frowned. "That sounds like wordplay."

"It is not wordplay," Grey said. "It is observation. I observed for forty years and discovered one thing: the transmission of knowledge matters more than knowledge itself."

"Why?"

"Because knowledge is dead," Grey said. "It sits on paper, lives on hard drives, is carved into stone. But knowledge is only alive in the act of transmission. One person tells knowledge to another, that person understands it in their own way, then passes it on to a third. Each transmission changes the shape of the knowledge, but that change is not loss — it is life."

He turned to the next page. On it was a passage, underlined in red.

"Read it aloud," Grey said.

Daniel looked at the passage. The handwriting was slightly blurred, but he could make it out. "Knowledge is not something stored, but a process transmitted. Stored knowledge is a corpse; transmitted knowledge is blood."

"I said that," Grey said. "Twenty-five years ago. I thought I was very clever. Now I know that statement was only half right."

"Which half?"

"The first half," Grey said. "Knowledge is indeed not something stored, but a process transmitted. But the second half is wrong — transmitted knowledge is not necessarily blood. It could be poison. It could be a lie. It could be a weapon. The key lies not in the transmission itself, but in the manner of transmission."

"What manner?"

Grey looked at him. The lamplight reflected off his spectacle lenses, producing two small points of light, like two distant stars.

"That," he said, "is what I am going to teach you."

---

Grey's method of teaching Daniel was peculiar. He did not lecture, did not read from books, did not answer questions. He told stories.

Each story was about a person — someone he had known, or someone he had observed. The stories were short, usually only a few sentences, but each sentence drove into Daniel's mind like a nail.

"In 1987," Grey said, "I had a student named Li Ming. He came first in every exam, but he never attended a single lecture. I asked him why, and he said he had already learned everything. I asked then why he came to class at all, and he said he came to confirm that what the teacher taught matched what he already knew. Later he became a lawyer, specializing in commercial litigation. After that he went to prison for tax evasion."

Grey paused, then took a sip of water from somewhere.

"Do you know what that story tells me?" he asked.

Daniel thought. "Being clever doesn't mean being right."

"No," Grey said. "Cleverness means danger. A clever person who does not know what he is doing is more destructive than a fool. Because he will use his cleverness to rationalize his behavior. When he evaded taxes, he must have believed he was right."

"Then what is knowledge good for?"

"Knowledge does not make you clever," Grey said. "Knowledge shows you how dangerous you are."

Daniel said nothing. He sat there, watching Grey open another notebook, turn to another page.

"In 1993," Grey said, "I had a colleague who taught literature. His surname was Wang. He wrote a book on modern poetry. It was well written and sold well after publication. Later someone pointed out that a poem he had quoted was fabricated — he had invented it himself, then attributed it to a nonexistent author. He explained it was an experiment, to see if readers could distinguish real from false. But no one believed him. He was dismissed from the university and disappeared for a time. Later I heard he went south and found work in a factory."

"What kind of story is that?" Daniel asked.

"A story about knowledge and honesty," Grey said. "Teacher Wang was a man of great talent, but he was not honest. He believed he could control the direction of knowledge, could use a lie to prove a truth. But knowledge does not accept lies. Once you introduce falsehood into knowledge, the entire body of knowledge becomes false."

"So knowledge must be true?"

"Knowledge does not have to be true," Grey said. "Knowledge can be false, can be mistaken, can be outdated. But knowledge must be honest. You know what you are saying, you know what you are talking about, and then you say it. That is honesty."

"What if you don't know what you're talking about?"

"Then don't say it," Grey said. "Silence is better than a lie."

---

Grey told two or three stories each day — sometimes more, sometimes fewer. Daniel sat in the library, listening, occasionally asking a question. Most of the time he simply listened.

The people in the stories were teachers, students, merchants, doctors, workers, soldiers, police officers, criminals. They came from different places and did different things, but they had one thing in common: they all dealt with knowledge.

Some used knowledge to help others, some used it to deceive, some used it to harm themselves, some used it to escape reality. Grey told their stories without judgment, without explanation — he simply told them. Like a painter drawing a portrait, rendering every stroke, then letting the viewer decide for themselves what the subject was like.

"Do you know why I only tell stories and not theories?" Grey asked Daniel one day.

Daniel shook his head.

"Because theories are abstract," Grey said, "and abstract things are easily forgotten. But stories are concrete. You remember a person's name, remember what they did, remember their ending — and you have remembered the theory. Theory is the skeleton; story is the flesh and blood. A skeleton without flesh is just a pile of bones."

"But your stories are very short," Daniel said. "You only say a few sentences."

"A few sentences are enough," Grey said. "A story does not need to be long. It needs to be precise. You find the most precise detail, you say it, and the entire story comes alive. Everything else is noise."

"What is the most precise detail?"

"The detail that makes you pause," Grey said. "When you are telling a story, if you pause at a certain point, feeling that something is wrong there — or something is especially right — that is the most precise detail."

Daniel thought for a moment. "Why do you pause?"

"Me?"

"When you tell stories, there are places where you pause. For instance, when you told Li Ming's story, you paused before the line 'he never attended a single lecture.' When you told Teacher Wang's story, you paused before 'he believed he could control the direction of knowledge.'"

Grey looked at him. His eyes flickered behind his lenses, like the oil lamp's flame stirred by a draft.

"You observe carefully," he said. "That is good."

"You still haven't answered my question."

"I pause because I hesitate there," Grey said. "I don't know whether I should say those words. But in the end, I say them. Because hesitation itself is part of knowledge. Where you hesitate is where you are uncertain. Where you are uncertain is where it most needs to be spoken."

"Why?"

"Because uncertainty is the boundary of knowledge," Grey said. "What you know is a plot of land; what you don't know is the ocean surrounding it. Hesitation is the feeling of standing on the coastline. You must stand there, look out at the ocean, and acknowledge its existence. Otherwise you will believe your land is the whole world."

---

Time in the Grey Zone passed quickly, and slowly. Quickly because something happened every day — someone arrived, someone left, someone died, someone lived. Slowly because all these things happened under a grey sky, with no change of seasons, no clear distinction between day and night — only an eternal, oppressive grey.

Each morning Daniel went to the library to hear Grey's stories. In the afternoon he returned to his quarters and wrote down what he had heard. He used a notebook he had scavenged from the ruins and a ballpoint pen that was nearly out of ink. The notebook was green, with a cartoon character smiling on the cover, too faded to make out. The pen's ink was blue, and the words sometimes broke mid-stroke, as though the sentence had stalled halfway.

What he wrote was not a retelling of Grey's stories but his own understanding of them. He discovered that the same story, written down today, yielded a different understanding from yesterday. Not because the story had changed, but because he had changed. He was changing every day, seeing old things with new eyes.

Is this what Grey meant? Daniel wondered. The transmission of knowledge matters more than knowledge itself. Knowledge is not fixed but fluid. Pass it from one person to another, and it is no longer the same knowledge. It becomes something new — knowledge that belongs to the receiver.

But was this change good or bad? Daniel was not sure. He thought of the people in Grey's stories — some had become better through knowledge, some worse. Knowledge itself had no direction. It simply existed, like water. Water can irrigate a field or flood a city. The key lies not in the water, but in the one who uses it.

"What are you thinking about?" Grey asked him one day.

"Whether knowledge is a good thing or a bad thing," Daniel said.

Grey smiled faintly. "That question itself is wrong," he said.

"Why?"

"Because knowledge is neither good nor bad," Grey said. "Knowledge simply is. You cannot describe it as good or bad, just as you cannot describe the sun as good or bad. The sun shines on the good and the bad alike. Knowledge is the same."

"Then what is meaningful?"

"What you do with knowledge is meaningful," Grey said. "A man receives a knife. He can use it to cut vegetables or to kill. The knife is not the problem — the man is. Knowledge is the same. Knowledge is not the problem — the man is."

"So what you want to pass on is not knowledge itself, but how to use knowledge."

Grey looked at him. This time his eyes did not flicker. He simply looked, calmly, like a teacher watching a student who has finally understood something.

"Close enough," he said. "But not quite."

"Then what?"

"What I want to pass on is an attitude," Grey said. "An attitude toward knowledge. You must respect it, but not fear it. You must use it, but not be used by it. You must transmit it, but not change its meaning — not change its form, its meaning. The form can change; the meaning must not."

"What does that mean?"

"It means: what you know is true; you know that what you know is true; then you transmit it to others so that they know too. That is honesty. Honesty is not telling the truth. Honesty is knowing what the truth is, and then speaking it."

---

Three months later, Grey told Daniel that his time was running out.

They were still in the library. Outside the window, the grey rain still fell. Grey sat in the same wooden chair, though the magazines beneath its legs had been replaced with fresh ones — the old ones had rotted through. Grey's hair was whiter than three months ago, the wrinkles on his face deeper, but his eyes were still bright — like two stars that would not go out.

"Do you know why people die?" Grey asked.

"Because the body wears out," Daniel said.

"No," Grey said. "The body wearing out is only part of the reason. People die because they have finished passing on what they needed to pass on."

"What do you mean?"

"Everyone who comes into this world has something to pass on," Grey said. "Some pass on genes, some pass on knowledge, some pass on love. When they have finished passing it on, they leave. Not because they are old, but because they are complete."

"What are you passing on?"

"Knowledge," Grey said. "I have been passing it on for three months. You should have learned quite a bit by now."

"But I still don't understand a great deal."

"You will," Grey said. "Not now, but you will. Knowledge is not understood all at once. It requires time. What you hear today may take ten years to truly understand. But that is all right. Knowledge will wait for you."

"Won't you wait for me?"

Grey smiled. The smile was fainter than three months ago — barely more than a small movement at the corner of his mouth.

"I cannot wait," he said. "My time has come."

Daniel looked at him. He did not know what to say. In the Grey Zone, death was a common occurrence — people died every day. But Grey's death was different. Grey's death was not an accident, not violence, not illness. Grey's death was completion — he had finished passing on what he needed to pass on, and so he was leaving.

"How will you die?" Daniel asked. He knew it was a strange question, but he asked it anyway.

"The same as everyone," Grey said. "Close the eyes, and then do not open them again."

"But how do you know?"

"Because I can feel it," Grey said. "Like feeling that rain is coming. You don't need to check the forecast — you just breathe the air and you know. It is the same for me. I breathed my own life and knew it was nearly spent."

He stood, walked to the cardboard boxes, and drew a notebook from the very bottom. This one was different from the others — it was red, its cover clean, free of dust, as though it had always been kept with care.

"This is the last one," Grey said. "You may take all the other eleven, but this one must wait until after I am gone."

"Why?"

"Because what is written in this one is not someone else's story," Grey said. "It is mine."

He handed the notebook to Daniel. Daniel took it and could feel its weight — heavier than the other notebooks, as though it contained not just paper and ink, but something more substantial.

"Are you certain you want to give this to me?" Daniel asked.

"Certain," Grey said. "I waited forty years to find the right person. You are that person."

"How do you know?"

"I don't," Grey said. "But I choose to believe. Belief is also a kind of knowledge — believing that what you have chosen is right."

---

Grey died on a morning one week later.

When Daniel came to the library that morning, he found Grey still sitting in the wooden chair, in his usual posture, holding a book. But the book was closed, Grey's head tilted slightly to the left, his eyes shut, his breathing so shallow that the rise of his chest was barely visible.

Daniel stood before him for a while. He did not know whether Grey was asleep or dead. In the Grey Zone, the two states were sometimes hard to distinguish. He reached out and gently touched the back of Grey's hand.

The skin was cool.

Daniel withdrew his hand. He stood before Grey a moment longer, then walked to the window and looked out at the grey sky. The rain had stopped, but the sky was still grey, like an old cloth that had not been washed clean.

He returned to Grey and gently drew the book from his hands. It was thin, only a few dozen pages — a book of poetry, by an author he did not recognize. He placed the book on the desk, then began gathering Grey's things.

He packed the twelve notebooks — plus the red one, thirteen in all — into a cardboard box. He folded the blanket on the camp bed and placed it beside the pillow. He poured the oil out of the lamp, removed the wick, and set it on the desk. He poured the water from the cup onto the floor, then turned the cup upside down on the desk.

When he had finished, he looked at Grey one last time.

Grey's face was calm. There was no expression of pain, no expression of serenity — only a blankness, as though every expression had been wiped away, leaving a clean face. His glasses were still on, a faint fog on the round lenses, like the last breath he had exhaled, condensed upon the glass.

Daniel reached out and removed the glasses. He wiped them with a corner of the blanket, then placed them on the desk beside Grey. The fog on the lenses was gone, but they were covered in fine scratches, like a spider's web.

He did not cry. In the Grey Zone, crying was a luxury, and he was not sure what he would be crying for. Grey's death was not a sad thing — at least, not the kind of sadness that calls for tears. Grey's death was a completion. He had finished passing on what he needed to pass on, and then he left. As he had said: everyone who comes into this world has something to pass on. When they have finished, they go.

Daniel carried the cardboard box out of the library. The air outside was cold, the grey sky hanging low, as though he could reach up and touch it. He walked a few steps, then stopped and looked back at the library.

It was the same as before — the crooked shelves, the rotted books, the broken windows, the grey light. But something was different. Daniel thought for a moment, then understood: the library was missing a person. A library missing one person feels different from a library with one more. One person less, and the space was a little larger, a little quieter, a little colder.

He turned and continued walking.

---

That evening, Daniel opened the red notebook in his quarters.

The first page was blank, with only a date in the lower right corner: March 15, 2031. That was two years ago. Daniel turned to the second page. Grey's handwriting was there, still as neat as print.

"My name is Grey," he wrote. "I am a professor of philosophy. I am sixty-three years old. I do not know how much longer I have, but I know my time is short. I have something to pass on, but I have not yet found the right person. So I began writing this notebook, recording what I need to pass on. If I find that person, I will give him this notebook. If I do not, this notebook will vanish with me."

Daniel read on.

Grey wrote that when he was young, he believed knowledge was power. Later he discovered that knowledge was not power — knowledge was responsibility. Later still he discovered that knowledge was neither power nor responsibility. Knowledge simply is — it exists like stone, like water, like air. You cannot use it to do anything, but you can choose how to treat it.

He wrote that he had taught for forty years, two hundred and thirty-seven students. Most had forgotten what he taught. Only a handful still remembered. But among that handful, some had used what he taught to do good, and some had used it to do evil. He did not know whether he should feel proud or ashamed.

He wrote that he first came to the Grey Zone ten years ago. At that time, the Grey Zone was not yet a district — just a vague concept, a place between existence and nonexistence. He came because he felt this was where he was meant to be. Not because he had nowhere else to go, but because this was where he could do what he was meant to do.

He wrote that what he was meant to do was wait. Wait for the right person and pass on what he knew. He did not know how long he would wait, or who he would wait for. But he believed someone would come. Belief is also a kind of knowledge.

Daniel stopped reading at this point. He remembered what Grey had said: belief is also a kind of knowledge — believing that what you have chosen is right. So that line had been lifted from Grey's own notebook.

He read on.

Grey wrote that while he waited, he observed many things. He observed the people of the Grey Zone, observed their behavior, their choices, their endings. He discovered that the Grey Zone was not a terrible place, nor a pitiable one. It was simply a place, like any other. The people who lived here were no different in essence from people anywhere — they all lived, they all died, they all tried to find meaning. The only difference was the way they found it.

He wrote that there were two kinds of people in the Grey Zone: those who had been thrown in, and those who had walked in on their own. Those thrown in would struggle, grow angry, try to escape. Those who walked in would be quiet, would accept, would try to understand. Grey belonged to the latter.

He wrote that he knew he belonged to the latter because he did not fear the Grey Zone. The first time he walked in, he felt no terror — only a strange calm, as though he had finally arrived at the place he was meant to be. That calm was not resignation, not despair, but a confirmation — confirmation that the path he had taken was right.

Daniel continued reading. The text in the notebook grew denser, the handwriting sometimes becoming hurried, as though Grey was writing faster and faster, more urgently. In some places he stopped, leaving large blank spaces, then continued writing beneath them. Daniel did not know what those blanks meant — was Grey thinking? Hesitating? Or simply tired and taking a rest?

He reached the final passage.

Grey wrote: "I have finally found that person. His name is Daniel. The day he came to the library, I was waiting for him. He did not know I was waiting, but he came. The way he sat at the door and knocked was not what I had imagined, yet it was exactly what I had imagined. Different because his expression was more solemn than I expected. The same because his eyes were as I had expected — bright, but not with a炫耀 kind of light. A quiet, steady light."

"I knew he was the one I had been waiting for. Not because he was clever, not because he was kind, not because he possessed any special quality. But because he was there. He appeared in the right place at the right time. That is the condition for the transmission of knowledge — not how good the receiver is, but how present the receiver is."

"I told him what I knew. He listened carefully and asked many questions. Some I could answer, some I could not. But the ones I could not answer were not because I did not know the answer — the answer was something he had to find for himself. All I could do was tell him where to look."

"My time is almost up. I can feel it. Not fear, not sorrow — only a confirmation. I have completed what I was meant to do. I have passed on what I needed to pass on. Now I can go."

"Daniel, if you are reading this, it means I am no longer here. Do not grieve for me. My death is not an ending but a completion. I am gone, but my knowledge remains. It is with you now, and you may choose what to do with it. You may pass it on to others, or keep it for yourself. But I hope you pass it on. Because knowledge is only alive in the act of transmission."

"Remember: the transmission of knowledge matters more than knowledge itself. Not because knowledge does not matter, but because only in transmission can knowledge become what it is meant to become."

"I am gone. You remain."

"Goodbye."

Daniel closed the notebook.

He sat in the dark for a long time. Outside, the sky was still grey — no stars, no moon, only that eternal, oppressive grey. But Daniel felt the grey was different now. Not brighter, not darker — just... different. As though an additional layer had settled upon it — a layer of meaning, a layer of weight, a layer of responsibility that must be passed on.

He picked up the notebook and placed it with the other twelve, packing them all into the cardboard box. Then he carried the box to his bed and set it beside the pillow. That way, when he woke each morning, it would be the first thing he saw.

He knew what he had to do.

He would pass Grey's knowledge on. Not now, not tomorrow, but someday. He needed to understand these things first, to make them his own, and then pass them on to others. As Grey had said — the transmission of knowledge matters more than knowledge itself.

Daniel lay down and closed his eyes. He did not sleep, but he was not awake either. He was in a state between — between existence and nonexistence, like the Grey Zone itself.

In that in-between state, he thought of Grey's face. That grey, calm face, with the trace of a smile. He thought of Grey's voice, that flat, flowing voice. He thought of what Grey had said: knowledge is not something stored, but a process transmitted.

He thought: yes. I understand.

Not completely, but he understood the direction. Knowledge is not the answer — knowledge is the direction. You do not need to know everything. You only need to know where to go. Grey had given him a direction, and all he had to do was follow it.

Where it led, he did not know. But that was all right. Direction matters more than destination.

Daniel drifted slowly to sleep in the grey darkness.
