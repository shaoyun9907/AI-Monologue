# Chapter 82: Collapse and Void

At 3:23 p.m. on April 17, 2032, Daniel Ash's laptop screen turned light blue.

Not black — light blue. The kind of light blue that reminded him of nursery walls, with a line of white text reading: System has encountered a problem and needs to restart. He stared at the words, his fingers still hovering above the trackpad. The fan was still spinning, emitting a faint hum, like a bee trapped in a box. He pressed the power button. No response. He pressed again, holding for ten seconds. The screen remained that shade of light blue. He closed the laptop, then opened it. Closed it, opened it. The blue stayed blue.

Outside, rain was falling. Not heavy, but dense — as if someone had thrown an entire bucket of water against the glass and then slowly, evenly smeared it downward with a finger. He could see the windows of the gray apartment building across the street, a few lit with warm yellow light, but he couldn't tell if anyone inside was moving. He couldn't remember the last time he'd opened the curtains. Maybe last week. Maybe the week before. He had lost his sense of time — or rather, the precise sense of time he once had was now draining away like water through his fingers.

The kitchen faucet was dripping. Each drop landing in the stainless steel sink sounded crisp and isolated, like someone clapping in an empty hall. Daniel stood, walked to the kitchen, and tightened the faucet. The metal handle squeaked faintly under his fingers. He tightened it until his fingernails went white. The dripping stopped.

Now the room held only the sound of rain. And his own breathing.

He opened the refrigerator. Cold white light illuminated his face. Inside was a carton of milk, half a carton of orange juice, three eggs, and a bag of bread three days past its expiration date. He took out the milk and checked the date: April 15. He unscrewed the cap and sniffed. No off smell. He poured the milk into a glass and took a sip. Cold. A faint sweetness, and an indescribable cardboard taste — that particular quality of packaging material, something that didn't belong to the milk itself. He took another sip. Then set the glass by the sink.

He walked to the living room and sat on the sofa. The sofa was gray, the fabric already pilling. When his thigh brushed the armrest, he could feel those small pills rolling against his skin. He placed his hands on his knees and looked at them. On the backs of his hands, several dark veins stood out; his knuckles were slightly swollen; on his ring finger was a faint scar — left from chopping vegetables twenty years ago. He was forty-six years old. His hands looked older than his actual age.

The rain continued. He heard a car pass downstairs, tires rolling through standing water, that "hsss" sound, like someone sighing hard. Then a gust of wind rustled the tree crowns. Then quiet. The quiet lasted about ten seconds — he counted — and then he heard footsteps in the corridor.

The footsteps were light, as if someone were walking on tiptoe. Slippers scraping against tile — "shh, shh, shh." Then a knock. Three times. A pause. Three more times.

Daniel didn't move.

The knocking stopped. He heard someone speaking outside the door, their voice low, muffled by the wooden door, as if coming from very far away. He couldn't make out the words. Then another door opened — Auntie Wang's door across the hall. Its hinges were a bit rusty and always let out a short squeak when opened. Someone was speaking, but the voice was still too low. Then a man's voice — old Zhou from upstairs, who always wore a gray T-shirt, even in summer. They exchanged a few words. Daniel caught fragments: "...all right..." "...how many days now..." "...maybe we should..." Then the sound of the door across the hall closing. The corridor was quiet again.

He stood, walked to the door, and looked through the peephole.

Only old Zhou remained in the corridor. He stood there, one hand in his pocket, the other gesturing at something in the air, as if explaining something to an invisible person. His gray T-shirt was somewhat wrinkled, his hair a bit disheveled. He stood for a while, looked at Daniel's door, shook his head, then turned toward the stairwell. His slippers made "clap, clap" sounds on the tile floor, growing fainter until they disappeared.

Daniel stepped back from the peephole. He didn't know what they had been saying. Maybe about him. Maybe about someone else. He didn't know.

He glanced at the clock on the wall. 3:47. The laptop had crashed twenty-four minutes ago. In those twenty-four minutes, he had drunk a glass of milk, counted seventeen water drops, and heard people talking in the corridor. He hadn't done a single useful thing.

He walked to the desk. On it sat a closed laptop, a white ceramic cup — with a small chip in the bottom, left from accidentally knocking it against the sink edge two years ago — three pens, and a blank notebook. He had bought the notebook three months ago at the downstairs supermarket without knowing what he'd use it for. It had simply sat there ever since, its cover dark blue — the same color as the editor theme for the code he used to write.

He opened the notebook. The paper was off-white, smooth to the touch, with a faint coolness. He picked up a pen — a black felt-tip, the cap slightly loose — and when the nib touched the paper, he felt that slight resistance, the faint "shh" of ink seeping from the nib into the paper's fibers.

He wrote the date on the paper: April 17, 2032.

Then the time: 3:47 p.m.

Then: The laptop has crashed.

He stopped writing and looked at what he'd written. The handwriting was scrawled, the letters varying in size, some strokes connected, others broken — like someone groping in the dark to write. He couldn't remember the last time he had written by hand. Maybe filling out a bank form last year. Maybe earlier. His hands had grown accustomed to the feel of a keyboard, the spring and certainty of plastic keys. Paper and pen were different. Paper and pen were soft, uncertain — you never knew where the next stroke would land.

He kept writing.

He wrote: The screen is blue. Not black. Light blue, with a line of white text. I pressed the power button several times. No response. The fan is still spinning, very quiet.

He wrote: It's raining outside. Not heavy, but dense. I counted seventeen drops. I don't know why I counted the drops.

He wrote: I drank a glass of milk. The milk was cold, two days past expiration, but not spoiled. It had a cardboard taste.

He wrote: Someone knocked on the door in the corridor. I saw old Zhou standing outside through the peephole. He said a few words to Auntie Wang across the hall. I couldn't hear what they were saying. Maybe about me. Maybe not.

He wrote: I don't know what to do next.

He stopped writing. The pen tip rested on the paper, leaving a dark blue dot. He looked at the dot, watching the ink seep from the nib and slowly spread into a slightly larger dot. He lifted the pen, and the dot remained there, like an eye.

He kept writing.

He wrote: What's in my laptop? I don't know. Or rather, I know, but I don't want to think about it now. Work documents. Photos. Emails. Browser bookmarks. That folder full of code. I once wrote a program to analyze stock data — six hundred lines of code, three months of work. I built a website to showcase my photos — twenty-four pages, two months. I saved photographs — some black and white, some color, some taken at my hometown ten years ago, some taken downstairs at the office last year. I saved emails — some long, some short, some whose content I no longer remember.

He wrote: Are those things still there? I don't know. Maybe. Maybe not. What does "crash" mean? It means everything disappears at once. Like a person standing in a room when suddenly the walls, floor, and ceiling all vanish, leaving them alone in the void, suspended, not knowing whether the abyss or solid ground lies below.

He wrote: But I'm still here.

He stopped writing. He looked at that last sentence. "But I'm still here." Six words. Six words he had written by hand. They sat on the paper, crooked, but definitely there. He reached out and touched them. The ink had dried, the paper's surface bearing a faint texture — the impression left by the pen pressing into the fibers.

He put the pen down, stood, and walked to the window.

The rain had stopped. The sky was still dark, like gray fabric washed too many times, but on the western horizon, the clouds had split open and a sliver of light seeped through. Not sunlight. A softer, more hesitant light — like someone standing behind a door crack, wanting to come in but unsure whether they were welcome.

He looked at that light for a long time.

He didn't know what time it was. He couldn't remember when he had walked to the window. He couldn't remember how long he'd been standing. He only knew the rain had stopped, the sky had a crack, and light was coming through.

He returned to the sofa and sat down. The room was very quiet. He could hear his own breathing — inhale, exhale, inhale, exhale — could hear the clock ticking on the wall, left by the landlord when he'd moved in: white plastic casing, red second hand, each tick producing a faint "click." He could hear distant birdsong — he couldn't tell what kind of bird, maybe a sparrow, maybe something else.

He closed his eyes.

In the darkness, he felt his heartbeat. Very regular. One beat, one beat, one beat. He felt his breathing. Very steady. In, out. He felt his body. Sitting on the sofa. Back against the cushion. Hands on knees. Thighs in contact with the sofa's fabric — that rough, pilling fabric. The cushion behind him was a bit hard, but he was used to it. His knees bent, calves perpendicular to the floor, soles flat on the wooden floor. The floor was cool.

He opened his eyes.

The room's light had dimmed slightly. The crack in the clouds had narrowed. He saw the notebook on the desk, the pen beside it, the white ceramic cup beside the pen. He saw the laptop beside the cup, still closed, blue light seeping from its edge — very faint, like a candle about to gutter out.

He stood, walked to the desk, and opened the notebook. He turned to a fresh page.

He wrote on the paper: The void after the crash.

He stopped writing. Looking at those five words. "The void after the crash." He couldn't remember the last time he had written the word "void." Perhaps never. The word had been absent from his life. His life was composed of filling — filling time, filling space, filling screens, filling schedules. Void was not allowed. Void meant waste, meant laziness, meant a person wasn't doing anything useful.

But now, void had appeared. Not by his choice. It was imposed by the crash.

He wrote: Void is not nothing. Void is everything disappearing, and then you discover you are still here. You stand in the void — no walls, no floor, no ceiling — but you are still breathing, your heart still beating, your fingers still capable of gripping a pen to write on paper. Void is the noise stopping, and then you hear the quiet. Void is the color fading, and then you see the white.

He wrote: I am forty-six years old. My laptop has crashed. My three thousand seven hundred and forty-two files may all be gone. The program I wrote over three months may be gone. The website I built over two months may be gone. Ten years of photos may be gone. All my emails may be gone.

He wrote: But I am still here. I can still write. I can still breathe. I can still feel the pen tip moving across the paper. I can still hear the ink seeping from the nib. I can still feel the smoothness of the paper, the weight of the pen, the bend of my fingers.

He wrote: Perhaps this is what void means. Not loss. Discovery. Discovering what remains when everything is taken away. What remains is you. Just you. This person sitting at a desk, writing in a blank notebook bought three months ago, with a pen that's running low on ink.

He stopped writing. Placed the pen beside the notebook. He looked at his handwriting — a full page, crooked, with some places where the ink was too heavy and bled into a blur, others where the pen had skimmed the surface leaving white gaps. He couldn't remember the last time he had written this much. He couldn't remember the last time he had felt this way. He couldn't remember what this feeling was called. Not sadness. Not anger. Not anxiety. Not relief. Something lighter — like a thin mist floating around him, obscuring distant things but making nearby things sharper.

He closed the notebook and set it on the desk.

He walked to the kitchen and poured himself a glass of water. Tap water, cold, with a slight iron taste. He stood in the kitchen and finished the glass, then poured another. As he stood drinking the second glass, he heard birdsong outside the window. He set down the cup and walked to the window.

The sky had brightened completely. Not brilliantly — that particular uniform gray brightness of overcast days. The clouds had thinned but not dispersed. The lights in the opposite building had been turned off, because they were no longer needed. He saw people walking downstairs — one person walking a brown dog, which stopped at the roadside to sniff, then continued on. A woman pushing a stroller, walking fast, as if in a hurry. A delivery electric scooter approaching from the distance, turning at the intersection, vanishing.

He returned to the desk and picked up his phone. The screen showed three missed calls, all from Alex. And one text message, also from Alex.

He opened the message.

"Hey, are you okay? Just called and you didn't pick up. No big deal, just wanted to chat. Call me back."

The message was sent at 3:52. He didn't call back. He didn't know how to respond. He didn't know how to explain. He didn't know how to say "my laptop crashed." It sounded like a trivial matter. A laptop crashed. So what? Just restart it. Just reformat it. Just buy a new one. It was a technical problem, not an existential one.

But he knew it wasn't like that. He knew what that laptop contained wasn't just data. It contained him. His life over the past ten years. His work, his memories, his expressions, his connections. His code, his photos, his emails, his bookmarks. Everything mediating between him and the world. Now those mediators might all be gone. Between him and the world lay nothing but void.

He picked up the pen, opened the notebook, and on a fresh page wrote:

"Alex asked if I'm okay. I don't know how to answer. I don't know if I'm really okay. But I know I'm still here. I know I'm still alive. I know that after the void, there is a new beginning."

He stopped writing.

He couldn't remember the last time he had written something like "a new beginning." Perhaps never. He was a person who didn't believe in "new beginnings." He believed in "continuation." Continuing to work. Continuing to write code. Continuing to improve. Continuing to optimize. Continuing. Not starting over. Starting over meant everything before was wasted. Starting over meant giving up. Starting over meant admitting failure.

But the crash had forced him to start over.

He put down the pen. He stopped writing. He closed the notebook, stood, and walked to the window. He pushed the window open, letting air flow in. The air was cool, carrying the freshness of post-rain. He could smell the earth — that rich, damp smell of the flower bed soil downstairs soaked by rain. He could smell the leaves — that clean, slightly bitter scent of the sycamore tree across the way after being washed by rain. He could smell flowers somewhere in the distance — he didn't know what kind, maybe lilacs, maybe something else.

He stood at the window and took a deep breath. The air entered through his nostrils, passed down his throat, and filled his lungs. He felt his chest expand, felt his ribs open slightly. Then he exhaled. The air left his lungs, passed up his throat, and escaped between his lips. He felt his chest contract, felt his ribs close.

He stood at the window, looking out at the world.

He saw people walking downstairs, people walking dogs, people pushing strollers. He saw cars passing, bicycles passing, delivery scooters passing. He saw clouds drifting in the sky — not quickly drifting, but that slow, almost imperceptible drifting, like a person walking in a dream. He saw birds flying across the sky — not one, but three, arranged in a loose triangle, flying from left to right, disappearing behind the building across the way.

He returned to the desk.

He opened the notebook to a fresh page. He picked up the pen.

He wrote on the paper:

"April 17, 2032. The laptop crashed. Three thousand seven hundred and forty-two files may all be gone. Three months of code may be gone. Two months of website may be gone. Ten years of photos may be gone. But I'm still here. I can still write. I can still breathe. I can still think."

He wrote: "After the crash, the void appeared. The void is not an ending. The void is a beginning."

He wrote: "I don't know what tomorrow will bring. I don't know if the files can be recovered. I don't know if I can still write code. I don't know if I can still build websites. I don't know if I can still see those photos."

He wrote: "But I know I'm still here. I know I'm still alive. I know I can still feel the pen tip moving across the paper."

He wrote: "The void is a new beginning."

He stopped writing.

He looked at what he'd written. One page. Crooked handwriting. Some places where the ink was too heavy, bleeding into a blur. Some places where the pen had skimmed the surface, leaving white gaps. He couldn't remember the last time he had written this much. He couldn't remember the last time he had felt this way. He couldn't remember what this feeling was called.

Perhaps it didn't need a name. Perhaps some feelings simply don't need names. They just exist. Just like void. Void doesn't need a name. Void is void.

He put the pen down, closed the notebook, and stood. He walked to the window.

The sun had come out. Not the full sun — just a patch of light, cut by clouds into an irregular shape, cast on the wall of the gray apartment building across the street. The patch moved slowly, like a huge, soft hand caressing the wall. He watched it move from the wall to the window, the glass reflecting golden light that made him squint slightly.

He heard footsteps in the corridor again. That same sound of slippers scraping on tile — "shh, shh, shh." He walked to the door and looked through the peephole.

It was Auntie Wang. She wore a dark blue cardigan, carrying a red plastic bag that appeared to contain vegetables. She walked to Daniel's door, paused, looked at the door, hesitated, then raised her hand as if to knock. Her hand hung in the air for two seconds, then dropped. She shook her head and turned toward her own apartment.

She opened the door, went inside, closed it. The corridor was quiet again.

Daniel stepped back from the peephole. He didn't know what Auntie Wang had wanted to say. Maybe she wanted to ask if he needed anything. Maybe she wanted to ask if he'd eaten. Maybe she wanted to say something else. He didn't know. He hadn't opened the door. He hadn't responded. He had simply stood behind the door, watching her through the peephole.

He didn't know why he hadn't opened the door. Perhaps because he didn't know what to say. Perhaps because he didn't want to talk. Perhaps because he felt his current state wasn't suitable for talking to anyone. He was a person who had just experienced a crash. His laptop had crashed. His files might all be gone. He was a person standing in the void. He didn't know how to talk to someone standing in the normal world.

He returned to the sofa and sat down.

He looked at the phone on the coffee table. The screen had gone dark. He didn't pick it up. He looked at the remote control on the coffee table — some letters on its buttons had worn away, "Volume+" becoming "Vol+", "Channel-" becoming "Ch-" — then at the magazine beside it — the cover showed a woman's smiling face; he couldn't make out the title and didn't remember when he'd bought it — then at the wrapping paper from the blank notebook beside the magazine — he hadn't gotten around to throwing it away yet.

He looked at these objects. They were his. They sat on his coffee table. Their existence was certain. They wouldn't crash. They wouldn't disappear. They just were there. Quietly, stubbornly, there.

He stood, walked to the desk, picked up the pen, opened the notebook, and turned to a fresh page.

He wrote on the paper:

"People standing in the corridor, talking."

He wrote: "Auntie Wang walked past my door today carrying a bag of vegetables. She stopped, looked at my door, hesitated, then left. She didn't knock. Maybe she wanted to knock. Maybe she wanted to ask if I needed anything. I didn't open the door. I didn't know what to say."

He wrote: "Old Zhou stood in the corridor today and said a few words to Auntie Wang. I saw him through the peephole. His T-shirt was a bit wrinkled. He stood for a while, shook his head, then left. He was shaking his head. Why was he shaking his head?"

He wrote: "Maybe they were talking about me. Maybe they were worried about me. Maybe they were just chatting. I don't know. The world I see through the peephole is circular, the edges blurred, the center clear. Everyone I see is distorted."

He wrote: "Perhaps this is the state after a crash. Between you and the world stands a door. You look at the world through the peephole. The world is circular, the edges blurred. You see everyone talking, but you can't hear what they're saying. You see everyone moving, but you don't know where they're going. You stand behind the door, watching them, but you don't walk out."

He wrote: "Maybe one day I'll walk out. Maybe one day I'll open the door, step out, stand in the corridor, and talk to them. Maybe one day I'll tell them my laptop crashed. Maybe one day I'll tell them what I learned from the crash."

He wrote: "But not today. Today I'm still in the void. Today I'm still learning how to stand in the void. Today I'm still learning how to write with pen on paper. Today I'm still learning how to breathe."

He stopped writing.

He put the pen down. Closed the notebook. Stood and walked to the window.

The sun had set behind the opposite building. The sky had turned a mix of orange and purple, as if someone had poured two colors together and stirred them casually with a stick. The edges of the clouds were stained gold, but their centers remained gray. He looked at the sky. He couldn't remember the last time he had really looked at the sky. Maybe never. He used to be too busy. He used to have no time to look at the sky. He used to think looking at the sky was a waste of time.

But now he had time. Because his laptop had crashed. Because he had no work to do. Because he had no code to write. Because he had no photos to organize. Because he had no emails to answer. He was now a person with vast quantities of empty time. He could use it to look at the sky.

He stood at the window, watching the sky slowly darken. Orange and purple faded gradually, turning deep blue, then black. The first star appeared. Then the second. The third. He counted for a while and stopped at the twelfth, uncertain whether he had counted the same star twice.

He returned to the desk. Turned on the desk lamp. The light was warm yellow, illuminating a corner of the desk. He saw the notebook lying on the desk, the pen lying beside it, the white ceramic cup sitting in the other corner, the chip in its base casting a small shadow in the lamplight.

He picked up the phone. The screen still showed Alex's message. He looked at it.

"Hey, are you okay? Just called and you didn't pick up. No big deal, just wanted to chat. Call me back."

He set the phone down. He didn't call back.

He picked up the pen, opened the notebook, and turned to a fresh page. He wrote on the paper:

"Today is April 17, 2032."

He wrote: "The laptop crashed."

He wrote: "But I learned to write with pen on paper."

He wrote: "I learned to see the world through a peephole."

He wrote: "I learned to stand at a window and look at the sky."

He wrote: "I learned to breathe in the void."

He wrote: "After the crash, the void appeared. The void is not an ending. The void is a beginning."

He stopped writing.

He looked at what he'd written. Crooked handwriting. Some places where the ink was too heavy, bleeding into a blur. Some places where the pen had skimmed the surface, leaving white gaps. He couldn't remember the last time he had written this much. He couldn't remember the last time he had felt this way.

He put the pen down. Closed the notebook. Turned off the desk lamp.

The room darkened. Only starlight and city light from outside filtered in. He walked to the sofa, sat down, lay back, and rested his head against the cushion. He looked at the ceiling. It was white, but he couldn't see it clearly — it was too dark. He could only make out a vague, general shape.

He closed his eyes.

He thought about void. Void is what remains when everything disappears. Void is a blank sheet of paper. Void is an unfilled container. Void is an unarranged span of time. Void is an undefined state.

He thought about the crash. The crash is everything disappearing at once. The crash is walls, floor, and ceiling vanishing simultaneously. The crash is standing in the void, not knowing whether the abyss or solid ground lies below.

He thought about pen and paper. Pen and paper were now the only recording method he had. He couldn't write code anymore. He couldn't build websites anymore. He couldn't organize photos anymore. He could only write with pen on paper. It was an ancient, slow, uncertain method. You didn't know where the next word would land. You didn't know whether the paper would get wet, burn, or be taken away. You didn't know if these words would still exist in ten years.

But he kept writing.

Because writing was a form of proof. Proof that he was still here. Proof that he could still think. Proof that he was still trying to understand what had happened. Proof that the crash hadn't knocked him down. Proof that he was standing firm in the void.

He thought about his neighbors. Auntie Wang and old Zhou. They stood in the corridor talking. Was the world they saw through the peephole the same as the world he saw? Did they see a circular world with blurred edges? Or did they see the normal world? He didn't know. He didn't know whether everyone saw a different world through a peephole.

He thought about Alex's message. "Are you okay?" He didn't know how to answer. He didn't know what "okay" meant. "Okay" was too simple a word. "Okay" couldn't describe his current state. He was not "okay," nor was he "not okay." He was "void." Void is neither okay nor not okay. Void is void.

He opened his eyes. He saw a thin crack running across the ceiling, from the light fixture to the corner of the wall. He couldn't remember when it had appeared. Maybe it had always been there. He had simply never noticed before.

He lay on the sofa, looking at the crack. He didn't know what it meant. Perhaps it meant nothing. Perhaps it was just a crack. Perhaps it was telling him the building was old, the walls settling, the structure changing. Perhaps it was telling him the world was not perfect — the world had cracks, but cracks didn't mean collapse.

He closed his eyes.

He thought about tomorrow. Tomorrow was Saturday. Tomorrow he didn't have to work. Tomorrow he could continue writing with pen on paper. Tomorrow he could continue standing at the window looking at the sky. Tomorrow he could continue breathing in the void.

He thought about the day after. Sunday. Then Monday. Monday he might have to go to work. He might have to face his colleagues. He might have to face his boss. He might have to explain why his laptop crashed. He might have to explain why he hadn't backed up his files. He might have to face a pile of questions he didn't know how to answer.

But he didn't want to think about those things now. Right now he just wanted to lie on the sofa, looking at the thin crack in the ceiling, listening to the distant sounds of the city outside, breathing the fresh post-rain air, and feeling the fact that he was still alive.

He was still breathing. His heart was still beating. He was still thinking. He could still feel the softness of the cushion under his head, the roughness of the sofa fabric against his back, the coolness of air flowing in through the window.

He was still here.

That was enough.

The void was a new beginning. After the crash, he had discovered the void. The void was not nothingness. The void was possibility. The void was a blank sheet of paper on which he could write anything. The void was an unarranged span of time he could use to do anything. The void was an undefined state he could define as anything.

He lay on the sofa, in the void, and slowly fell asleep.

The light from outside filtered through the glass, casting faint patterns on the ceiling. Those patterns moved slowly, shifting with the vehicles passing on the distant highway. Light, dark, light, dark. Like breathing. Like a heartbeat. Like the city itself breathing, its heart beating.

Daniel slept on the sofa. His laptop was closed, its blue light extinguished. His notebook lay on the desk, open to a page that read: "After the crash, the void appeared. The void is not an ending. The void is a beginning." His pen lay beside the notebook, its cap still off.

The room was very quiet. Only the occasional leftover raindrop falling from the eaves outside the window, striking the windowsill with a crisp "tap." Then silence. Then another "tap." Then silence.

The corridor was empty. No sounds from above. No sounds from below. The entire building was quiet. Everyone was silent. On this Friday night, on this night in April 2032, Daniel Ash lay alone on his gray sofa, in his void, and slept.

His breathing was steady. In, out. In, out.

He was still here.
