# Chapter 59: The Price

That email arrived at 3:17 in the morning. Sara later told Daniel she had been debugging a core algorithm, the only person left in the office, the coffee maker emitting a low hum like a drowsy cat. She thought it was an automated system log report and opened it without thinking—only to find the name of the HR director.

The email was short. Subject: Personnel Adjustment Notice. The body contained only three lines—roughly: due to the company's strategic restructuring, her position would be eliminated, with details of the severance package in the attachment. No thanks, no attempt to retain her, not even a specific last day. Sara said she stared at the screen for a long time, long enough for the coffee maker to shut itself off automatically, plunging the office into an unnatural silence. Then she closed the email and went back to debugging the algorithm.

Daniel learned about it the next morning. He called Sara to ask whether the technical proposal they'd discussed the night before had made any progress, but heard in her voice a strange calm—the kind that comes from locking something away in the deepest part of the refrigerator. "I've been fired," she said. "Effective this morning." Daniel's hand, holding the phone, froze in midair. Sunlight was falling right on the half-dead pothos plant by the window. He wanted to say something, but Sara had already hung up.

It was the third week of May. Spring was leaving the city in its slow, stubborn way—the parasol tree leaves darkening from tender green to deep green, tricycle carts selling watermelon beginning to appear on the streets. Daniel stood at his apartment window, watching the street below, remembering something Sara had once said: "Golden Horizons isn't my home, but it's the place where I can do things." Now she didn't even have that.

---

Toby's situation deteriorated sharply in the same month.

By the end of April, he could still move freely around the city, though he knew he was being watched. He lived in a rented studio apartment, its window facing a quiet street, a bakery below that pumped out the scent of fresh bread every morning at seven. He had grown used to this life: waking to the smell of bread, spending the day processing documents, reading for a while in the evening. He thought this routine might continue for some time.

One day in mid-May, he went out to buy breakfast as usual. The bakery owner was a woman in her fifties, always smiling, who would slip him an extra croissant. But that morning, when he walked in, the smile froze on her face. She looked at him, and in her eyes was something Daniel had never seen—not fear, but something deeper, as if she had already made a decision. "All the bread is sold out today," she said, her voice soft but firm. Toby blinked. He could clearly see over a dozen loaves still in the display case. He said nothing and turned to leave.

That night, someone taped a note to his apartment door. There were no words on it—just a single eye, crudely drawn, as if sketched in haste with a red marker. Toby peeled the note off, folded it into a small square, and put it in his pocket. He did not tell Daniel.

Three days later, he realized he was being followed. Not professionally—clumsily, obviously. A black sedan always parked downstairs. The person inside never got out, never turned off the engine. Toby first noticed it on his way to the supermarket. When he entered the store, the car was parked across the street. When he came out, it was still there. He took several detours trying to shake it, but the car maintained its distance—neither close nor far.

He began changing his routine. No longer leaving at fixed times, no longer taking the same route, even shopping at different supermarkets. But whoever was following him seemed equally patient. They never approached, never made contact—they simply maintained that suffocating presence. Toby later told Daniel the feeling was like being gently pinched by an enormous hand: it didn't hurt, but you knew that if the hand squeezed even slightly, you would shatter.

---

Daniel's own price arrived more directly and more publicly.

In early May, he received a court summons. Golden Horizons had sued him for "misappropriation of trade secrets" and "unfair competition," claiming an astronomical figure—so large that the first time Daniel saw it, he thought it was a printing error. He laid the summons on the desk and studied every word on it, the sunlight falling on the paper, the print sharp enough to hurt his eyes.

His lawyer was named Chen Ming, a man in his forties who always wore a gray suit, his hair combed with meticulous precision. Chen Ming told him the case would be hard to fight. Golden Horizons' legal team was formidable, and they had evidence that would work against him. "What evidence?" Daniel asked. Chen Ming pushed his glasses up and said, "They claim you took the company's core algorithm." Daniel was quiet for a long time, then said, "I wrote that algorithm." Chen Ming nodded and said, "I know. But they don't see it that way."

At the same time, Daniel's social circle was shrinking rapidly.

He had once had a few friends—not close ones, but people he occasionally had dinner or conversation with. Now their calls grew fewer and fewer. One day on the street he ran into a former colleague, who spotted him, froze for a moment, then walked briskly away, as if avoiding something unclean. Daniel stood in place, watching the man's figure disappear into the crowd, and suddenly remembered something his mother had once said: "It's only when you fall on hard times that you see who your real friends are." At the time he'd thought it was a cliché. Now he found it terrifyingly precise.

What weighed on him even more was his family's silence. His parents lived back home in a quiet little city. They didn't quite understand what their son was doing—only that he had recently "gotten into some trouble." Every time he called, his mother would carefully ask whether he had eaten, whether he was sleeping well, but never ask about the thing that mattered most. Daniel knew she was afraid—afraid of learning the truth, afraid of being powerless to change anything, afraid of that helplessness. So he said nothing either. They held the phone on opposite ends of the silence, filling the gaps that should have contained words with "mm" and "OK."

---

One afternoon in late May, Daniel met Sara at a café.

The café sat on a quiet street, an old scholar tree at the door, sunlight filtering through its leaves to cast mottled shadows on the ground. Sara was already there, sitting by the window, a long-cold coffee in front of her. Daniel noticed her hair was cut shorter, she looked thinner, but her eyes were just as bright—as if something was still burning deep inside.

"You look good," Daniel said, sitting down.

Sara gave a small smile. "Because I finally have time to do what I want to do."

Daniel knew she was lying, but he didn't call her on it. He ordered an Americano. When the server brought it, the coffee's aroma was strong, mingling with the scholar tree's faint fragrance into a strange, layered scent.

"What did they tell you?" Daniel asked.

Sara traced circles on the table with her finger. "They said it was a strategic adjustment. I asked if it was because I'd been close to you. They said no. I asked if it was because I knew too much. They said no to that too. They said it was a business decision, told me not to overthink it." She paused, then said: "But I'm not stupid."

Daniel fell silent. He knew Sara was not stupid—quite the opposite, she was one of the sharpest engineers he'd encountered at Golden Horizons. She had been involved in the early development of that core algorithm. She knew every detail of how it worked, and she knew its limitations. That was precisely why she had been fired—not for what she had done, but for what she might do.

"What are your plans?" Daniel asked.

Sara looked out at the scholar tree. "I want to write a book. About the algorithm. About what it really meant." Her voice was calm, but Daniel could feel the weight in it. He knew this book would make things more complicated, but he also knew Sara would not stop.

"Are you sure?" he asked.

Sara turned to look at him, and in her eyes was something Daniel had never seen—not anger, not grief, but something deeper, as if she had already made a decision. "I'm sure," she said. "Some things need to be said."

---

On the first day of June, Toby moved out.

He didn't tell Daniel where he was going, leaving only a note on the doormat of Daniel's apartment. It read: "I need to disappear for a while. Don't look for me. I'll contact you." The handwriting was scrawled, as if written in haste. Daniel came home after ten that night, found the note, stood in the doorway looking at it, then folded it and put it in his pocket.

He knew why Toby had left. The people watching him had multiplied—from that one black sedan to two or three cars taking shifts. Once, Toby was cornered in an alley. Two men in black suits got out of a car and stood in front of him without saying a word—just looking at him. Toby said he could smell their cologne, a strong, cheap drugstore scent, mixed with the garbage stink of the alley into a nauseating blend. Finally one of them spoke: "You'll regret what your brother did." Then they left.

Toby did not regret anything. But he knew that if he stayed, things would only get worse. He wasn't afraid of being harmed himself—he was afraid of becoming leverage for someone to hurt Daniel. So he chose to leave.

Daniel sat on his apartment sofa, holding the note, the city lights blazing outside the window like a silent ocean. He remembered childhood, when Toby used to trail behind him like a little tail. They ran through the alleys of their hometown together, stole the neighbor's apples together, lay on the rooftop watching stars on summer nights. The sky was very dark then, the stars very bright. They lay on the roof, and Toby would always ask: "Brother, what's the world outside like?" Daniel had said: "Much bigger than here." Now they were both in this "much bigger" world, and missed that little alley more than ever.

---

In mid-June, Daniel received a call from an unknown number.

The voice on the other end was male, very young, carrying a deliberate calm. He said he was from Golden Horizons' legal department and wanted to discuss a settlement with Daniel. "We don't want to escalate this," the voice said. "It benefits no one." Daniel held the phone, listening to birds singing outside the window—they didn't know what had happened; they were simply hopping among the branches, singing happily.

"What do you want?" Daniel asked.

The voice paused, then said: "We want you to stop spreading misinformation and issue a public apology. In exchange, we'll drop the suit and provide you with a compensation package."

Daniel was silent for a long time. He could feel his own heartbeat—steady, strong, clear, one beat at a time. He remembered those days: the countless nights in the lab, watching data jump across screens, watching algorithms fail again and again, then starting over. He remembered Sara's messages arriving in the dead of night, saying she had found the key to the problem. He remembered Toby's clumsy encouragement over the phone: "Brother, you can do it." He remembered the price of truth—the friends lost, the family distanced, the colleagues fired, the brother driven into hiding.

"No," he said. "I won't apologize."

The voice paused again. "Are you sure? You know what happens if you continue."

Daniel looked out the window. Sunlight fell on the pothos plant, its leaves greener than before, slowly recovering. "I know," he said. "But truth is worth it."

He hung up and set the phone on the desk. The room was quiet, with only the air conditioner running. Daniel sat there, remembering something his father had once said: "There are things in life that, once done, you can't take back." At the time he'd thought his father was talking about trivial matters. Now he understood his father was talking about the truly important things—the things that determine who you become.

---

At the end of June, Sara's book was published.

Not through traditional publishing—it was released online, free for anyone to read. The title was *Beneath the Algorithm*, subtitled "An Engineer's Confession." Sara spent an entire month writing it, working from six in the morning until ten at night, resting only a few times in between. She wrote slowly, every word as if squeezed from her heart, carrying blood and bone.

The book described in detail how Golden Horizons' core algorithm worked, its original design purpose, and how it was later modified to do things it should never have done. Sara used no heavy jargon. She explained those complex technical concepts in a calm, almost gentle tone—like a doctor explaining a diagnosis to a patient. She wrote about the people affected by the algorithm, those labeled "high risk," those who lost opportunities because of a single number. She wrote about how the company's executives discussed these things in boardrooms, with a cold, almost scientific detachment—as if discussing the results of an experiment.

In the first hour after publication, three hundred people read it. First week: thirty thousand. First month: half a million. The comments section filled with rage, shock, gratitude, and disbelief. Some said Sara was a hero, some said she was a traitor, some said she was crazy, some said she had spoken words they had always wanted to say but never dared. Golden Horizons swiftly issued a statement calling Sara's book "containing substantial misinformation" and declaring it would "pursue legal remedies." But the book was already online—like a drop of ink falling into water, impossible to retrieve.

Daniel called Sara the evening the book was published. She sounded exhausted, but there was also a strange lightness to her voice, as if she had set down a very heavy burden. "I did it," she said. Daniel said: "Yes, you did." Then they were silent for a long time, listening to each other breathe, until Sara said: "I need to sleep. There's a lot to do tomorrow." Daniel said: "OK. Goodnight." Then he hung up.

He sat by the window, looking at the city skyline. The distant high-rises loomed like a row of silent giants, pinpricks of light in their windows like countless eyes watching something. Daniel remembered the last line Sara had written in the book: "Truth is not a weapon—it is soil. Only on the foundation of truth can we plant something real."

---

In early July, Daniel's case went to trial.

The courtroom was in an old building in the city center. The ceilings were high, sunlight streaming through tall windows, casting long shadows across the floor. Daniel wore a navy blue suit, sitting in the defendant's seat, looking across at the plaintiff's bench. Golden Horizons' legal team consisted of five people, all in black suits, their expressions solemn, as if attending a funeral. Daniel's lawyer Chen Ming sat beside him, holding a stack of documents, flipping through them periodically.

The proceedings were lengthy and dry. Both sides cited legal provisions, presented evidence, tried to convince the judge. Daniel sat there, listening to legal terminology he could barely comprehend, looking at documents he couldn't fully parse, feeling as if he were in a distant dream. He glanced occasionally at the gallery, where a few reporters sat—along with someone he recognized: Sara. She sat in the last row, notebook in hand, writing without pause.

The trial lasted three days. On the afternoon of the third day, the judge announced a recess and said the verdict would be delivered on a later date. Walking out of the courtroom, the sunlight was blinding. Daniel squinted at the traffic and pedestrians on the street. Chen Ming walked beside him, saying: "We did well. Don't worry." Daniel nodded but said nothing. He knew the outcome of this case no longer mattered—whatever the judge decided, he had already lost too many things, and no verdict could restore them.

He stopped at the street corner and lit a cigarette. He hadn't smoked in a long time. The taste was bitter, but he didn't throw it away. He stood there, looking at the distant sky, the clouds thick and heavy, as if rain might come at any moment. He remembered something his mother had once said: "In life, you're bound to do things you'll regret." At the time he'd thought she was talking about trivial matters. Now he understood she was talking about the truly important things—the things that determine who you become.

But he did not regret it.

He thought of the price paid for truth—the friends lost, the family distanced, the colleagues fired, the brother driven into hiding. He thought of Sara's book, of Toby's departure, of those nights spent alone. He thought of the voice on the phone, the threats and warnings, the attempts to silence him. He thought of all of it, then took a deep drag on the cigarette and blew the smoke toward the sky.

Truth is worth it.

He dropped the cigarette butt, crushed it underfoot, and kept walking. People came and went on the street. No one noticed him. No one knew what he had just been through. He was just an ordinary middle-aged man in a navy blue suit, walking down an ordinary street. But he knew that from this moment on, everything was different.

---

That night, Daniel sat alone in his apartment, lights off.

The city blazed outside, light filtering through the curtain to cast dim shadows on the floor. He sat on the sofa, holding a cup of long-cold tea, staring at those shifting patterns of light and dark. The room was quiet, with only distant traffic and the occasional bird call.

He remembered a night many years ago—just like this one—when he and Toby lay on the rooftop of their old home, watching a sky full of stars. The sky was very dark then, the stars very bright. They could see the Milky Way, like a glowing river spanning the entire sky. Toby pointed at a particularly bright star and said: "Brother, do you think there are people on that star?" Daniel had said: "I don't know. Maybe there are." Toby said: "If there are people, do you think they're looking at us too?" Daniel smiled and said: "Maybe."

Now he sat in this dark apartment, remembering that night, that star, that question. He didn't know whether there were people on that star, or whether they were looking this way. But he knew that somewhere in this city, Sara was writing her next book. Somewhere he didn't know, Toby was living his new life. Back in that small city where they grew up, his parents were waiting for his call. He knew he had paid everything for truth, but he did not regret it.

Because truth is worth it.

He set down the tea, stood, walked to the window, and drew back the curtain. The sky outside was dark, but he could see a few stars—very bright, very far. He stood there, looking at those stars, thinking of many things, and smiled softly.

The price was high, but he did not regret it.

He turned, walked to the kitchen, poured himself a glass of water, then returned to the sofa and opened his phone. A new message had arrived—from Sara, just a few words: "The book has been translated into English." Daniel looked at those words, then set down the phone and closed his eyes.

The night outside was deep, but his heart was bright.
