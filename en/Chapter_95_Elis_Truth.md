# Chapter 95: Eli's Truth

The rain in the Grey Zone never stopped — it only thickened or thinned, like the intervals of a person's breathing. Daniel stood in the doorway of the old house, watching Eli sit on the threshold peeling an orange. The sound of the peel breaking was very soft, yet in the gaps between the rain it came through with startling clarity — a dry, fragrant sound, and Daniel suddenly remembered his mother peeling oranges in the kitchen many years ago. It was one of his earliest memories, older than any trauma, older than any awakening.

Eli did not look up. He seemed never to look up at people — not out of arrogance, not out of avoidance, but because this was simply how he looked at the world. His gaze always fell on the thing at hand: peeling an orange, he looked at the orange; drinking water, he watched the ripples on the surface. Daniel had once thought this was escapism, then thought it was a kind of spiritual practice. Now he was not sure.

"How long have you been here?" Daniel asked.

Eli separated the segments and laid them on a cloth spread across his lap — a cloth he used to wipe his hands, washed so many times its original color was indistinguishable. He picked up a segment and placed it in his mouth. He chewed slowly, as though tasting the boundary between every trace of sweetness and sourness.

"How do you mean 'long'?" Eli said.

"All of it."

Eli swallowed the segment and picked up a second. "If you start counting from your calendar, twelve years. If I start counting from when I stopped counting, a little longer."

Daniel sat down on the doorframe, his shoulder leaning against the wood. The wood was wet; rainwater seeped along the grain into his shirt. But he did not move away. He needed this slight discomfort of dampness to keep himself alert.

"They said you were dead."

"Many people said I was dead," Eli said. "I thought I was dead too. Then I discovered I wasn't."

"Discovered?"

Eli tossed the peel into a jar beside him. What the jar had once contained, Daniel could not tell. Now it held all manner of peels, vegetable leaves, and tea dregs — the most colorful object in the Grey Zone: the orange-red of orange peel, the yellow of lemon, the pale green of pomelo, like some primitive, abandoned art.

"One morning I woke up," Eli said, "and found myself still breathing. That simple."

He said this with the same flatness as remarking that the weather was nice. No relief at having survived, no resentment at being abandoned, not even an intent to explain. He was simply stating a fact: one morning, he found himself still alive.

Daniel waited.

Eli finished the second segment and picked up a third. He ate the orange very slowly, with a pause between each segment, as though giving his body time to memorize the flavor.

"You're waiting for me to tell a story?" Eli said.

"If you'd like."

"I don't like telling stories," Eli said. "Stories are your thing. You like beginnings, middles, ends. You like cause and effect. You like meaning. I don't have those."

"Then what do you have?"

Eli looked at him. It was the first time Daniel was truly seen by Eli — those eyes were very dark, the pupil and iris almost indistinguishable, like a well. There was water at the bottom, light on the surface, but the light did not come from outside. It grew from within.

"I have now," Eli said.

---

Daniel remembered the first time he heard Eli's name. It was his third year in the Grey Zone. He had just begun to learn to find order on this forgotten land — not the order of laws, not the order of morality, but the order of survival itself. Who was trading, who was protecting, who was harming, who was disappearing. Eli's name appeared under the "disappearing" column.

No one knew where he came from. Some said Africa, some said South America, some said a Pacific island nation that had already sunk beneath the sea. His skin was dark brown, like clay that had been baked repeatedly in the sun, but in his eyes there was no tropical sunlight — something older, quieter, like the lustre of a stone soaked underwater for a very long time.

His appearance in the Grey Zone was also strange. He had not walked in, had not been brought in. He was simply "there." One dawn, someone found him in the warehouse of an abandoned factory, sitting on a pile of old newspapers, wearing a T-shirt washed white, drawing lines on the cement floor with his fingernail. The lines were very fine, running from the corner of the wall to the doorway, then from the doorway to the window — like a map, or a tally.

"Who are you?" the person who found him asked.

"Eli," he said.

"Where do you come from?"

He stopped drawing, looked at the lines he had made, and said: "Here."

That was thirteen years ago. Afterward, Eli became part of the Grey Zone, like a tree growing in ruins — no one found it strange, no one paid particular attention. He lived in that old house, helping people fix things: radios, flashlights, bicycle chains, chairs with broken legs. He did not charge money. People gave him food, and he accepted. Sometimes there was nothing, and he simply sat on the threshold, watching the rain.

Daniel began to notice him in his seventh year. By then Daniel had established a complete intelligence network in the Grey Zone. He thought he knew everyone's secrets. But Eli had no secrets, and that was what puzzled Daniel. How could a person have no secrets? Everyone was hiding something — an identity, a past, a wound they did not want others to know about.

Eli hid nothing because he had nothing. No backstory, no future plans, no fear to conceal, no strength to display. He was simply sitting there, alive.

"Aren't you afraid?" Daniel had once asked him.

"Afraid of what?"

"Of everything. Of the Grey Zone, of the outside world, of tomorrow."

Eli was repairing a radio. His fingers turned the dial, producing faint clicks. "What is tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow is —" Daniel stopped, because he found himself unable to define tomorrow. Tomorrow was a concept, a hypothesis, something that had not yet happened. How could he explain something that did not exist?

Eli did not press further. He continued turning the dial. The radio emitted fragments of music, as though from very far away, from another era.

---

Now, thirteen years later, Daniel sat on the threshold, three steps away from Eli. Rain fell. The orange peel oxidized slowly in the jar, deepening, darkening.

"Don't you think it's strange?" Daniel said. "Thirteen years — you've been here thirteen years and never thought of leaving?"

Eli finished the last segment of orange and wiped his hands on the cloth. His fingers were long, the knuckles prominent, the nails clipped short. Under the nails was the color of earth — not dirt, but the mark of contact with soil, like a farmer's hands, like a craftsman's.

"Leave to where?" Eli said.

"To the outside. To anywhere."

"What's outside?"

Daniel thought. "There's — everything."

"What is everything?"

"Houses, work, family, friends —"

"Those things you mentioned," Eli interrupted, his voice flat, not impatient, simply stating, "I used to have them all."

Daniel looked at him.

"In Africa," Eli said, "I had a village. Parents, brothers and sisters, cattle, fields. Then the war came, and everything disappeared. In South America, I had a city. A job, an apartment, friends, someone I loved. Then another kind of disaster came, and everything disappeared again."

He said these things without an expression of pain, without an expression of numbness. His face was like a quiet wall — light moved across it, but the wall itself did not move.

"The third time was on an island in the Pacific," Eli continued. "I lived there for seven years. It was the place I stayed longest. I learned to fish, learned to read the weather, learned to brew liquor with my neighbors. Then the sea level rose, and the island sank. Not a metaphor — it actually sank. The water rose over the streets, over the rooftops, over the cross on the church. I boarded a boat, drifted for a long time, and finally arrived here."

Daniel listened. He realized he was not listening to a story, but to an inventory of facts. No drama, no turning points, no climax. Just one thing after another, as natural as rain.

"Aren't you angry?"

"Angry about what?"

"About losing everything. Three times."

Eli thought for a moment. His way of thinking was different from Daniel's — Daniel's thinking was fast, leaping, full of possibilities; Eli's was slow and linear, like a river flowing across a plain.

"Anger is a need," Eli said. "A need for things to be different, a need for the world to work the way I want. I used to have that need. I don't anymore."

"Why?"

"Because I discovered," Eli said, "that things are the way they are."

---

The rain eased. Eli stood and went inside. Daniel followed.

The interior was dim, lit by a single window covered with translucent plastic sheeting. The light filtering through it turned a hazy grey-white, like diluted milk. The room was very tidy — a wooden bed with an old blanket; a desk holding the jar of peels, several repaired radios, and a stack of old newspapers; a straw mat on the floor with a few cushions.

Eli sat on the mat and gestured for Daniel to do the same. Daniel removed his shoes and sat cross-legged. The mat's texture was rough, carrying the scent of dried straw mixed with the dampness of rain, producing a strange, reassuring smell.

"You came to find me," Eli said, "because you want to know how to leave the Grey Zone."

Daniel did not deny it.

"You've come many times," Eli continued. "Each time you ask the same question, only differently. The first time you asked 'how to leave.' The second time, 'what's after leaving.' The third time, 'what happened to those who left.'"

Daniel felt a mild flush of shame. He had thought he was hiding it well, thought his questions were diverse enough, casual enough. But Eli had seen through all of it.

"I'm not mocking you," Eli said. "I'm only observing. I've observed for many years now — the people who come and go here. Everyone wants to leave. Some actually leave, some stay, some leave and come back."

"What happened to those who left?"

Eli picked up a cushion and placed it on his lap, both hands resting on its edges. The cushion was dark blue, its fabric worn so thin the outline of its filling was visible.

"They're alive," Eli said. "Alive is enough."

"That's it?"

"How did you think it should be?"

Daniel fell silent. He found he could not answer. What answer had he expected? Success? Happiness? Meaning? But those words felt hollow before Eli — like plastic flowers, possessing shape but no fragrance.

"You don't need to be saved," Eli said. "You only need to be seen."

"By whom?"

"By yourself."

---

Daniel wanted to object. He wanted to say this was too simple, too abstract, that it was not why he had come. He had come for a concrete answer, an actionable step, a map he could carry out of the Grey Zone.

But he did not speak.

Because Eli's gaze — those dark, quiet eyes, deep as a well — stopped him. In that gaze there was no light of wisdom, no hint of enlightenment, nothing superior. It was simply the look of one living person regarding another: calm, direct, without judgment.

"What do I see?" Daniel said, his voice softer than he expected.

"Yourself," Eli said.

"What is myself?"

"A living person. Here. Now."

"That's it?"

"You think it's not enough?"

Daniel wanted to say it was not enough. He wanted to say he needed more — needed meaning, needed purpose, needed a reason to explain why he was here and not there, why he was alive and not dead, why he had been through all this without finding something he could hold in his hand.

But he found he could not say it. Because when Eli said "alive is enough," that word "enough" dropped into him like a stone into water, sending ripples through his heart. Not the ripples of an answer, but the ripples of a question — if alive is enough, then what has he been searching for? If being seen is enough, then what has he been hiding?

"I don't understand," Daniel said.

"You don't need to understand," Eli said.

"Then what's needed?"

"To stay."

---

Eli stood and walked to the window. His back was very thin, the collar of his old T-shirt stretched loose, revealing the outline of his shoulder blades like two folded wings. He lifted a corner of the plastic sheeting and looked out.

"The rain is going to stop," he said. "When the rain stops, this is when it looks best."

Daniel stood and walked to the window. He stood beside Eli, looking out. The Grey Zone's landscape looked especially vivid after the rain — the dilapidated buildings, the rusted tin, the weeds growing from the cracks, a stray cat crouched on the wall licking its paw. Everything was old, everything was broken, but everything was there. Alive.

"Have you ever thought," Eli said, "why they call it the 'Grey Zone'?"

"Because it's in the grey area," Daniel said. "Neither black nor white."

Eli shook his head. "Not for that reason."

"Then why?"

"Because grey is the result of all colors mixed together," Eli said. "Red, yellow, blue, green, white, black — mix them all and you get grey. Grey is not the absence of color. It contains every color."

Daniel looked at the grey sky. The cloud cover was thick, but through the gaps shone a faint light — not sunlight, but something softer, more diffuse, like existence itself filtered through glass.

"People are afraid of grey," Eli said. "They want clear black and white, clear right and wrong. But the world is not like that. The world is grey. You find your place in the grey — not by escaping, but by accepting."

"Accepting what?"

"Accepting that things are the way they are. Accepting that you are here. Accepting that you are alive."

"And then?"

Eli turned to look at Daniel. His eyes in the grey light appeared even deeper — like two bottomless wells.

"There is no 'then,'" he said. "There is only now."

---

In his thirteenth year in the Grey Zone, Daniel finally understood what Eli meant. Not with his mind, but with his body — just as you do not need to understand gravity; you only need to know that you are standing here and will not float away.

Eli's truth was not a secret. Not a treasure to be excavated, not a code to be cracked. Eli's truth was living itself — not the meaning of life, not the purpose of life, not what life should do. Simply living. Breathing, here, now.

Daniel thought of those who had left. Some went north, saying they wanted to find a place without war. Some went to the coast, saying they wanted to open a fishing shop. Some went to the city, saying they wanted to be useful. They all left — carrying hope, carrying plans, carrying visions of the future. Some succeeded, some failed, but they were all alive.

Alive is enough.

This was not surrender. Eli had been here thirteen years not because he had given up on leaving, but because he had chosen to stay. Not trapped, but stopped. What was the difference between being trapped and staying? Being trapped was wanting to leave but being unable; staying was not wanting to leave and remaining where you are.

"Don't you want to leave?" Daniel asked.

Eli sat back down on the mat, picked up the cushion, and placed it on his lap. His hand rested lightly on it, the fabric producing a faint rustling.

"I used to," he said. "I used to want to go back to the village in Africa, back to the city in South America, back to the island in the Pacific. I wanted to go back and see, wanted to know if those places still existed, if those people were still there."

"And then?"

"Then I discovered," Eli said, "that what I would be going back to was not those places, but my memory of them. But memory is not a place. Memory is something I built in my mind — different from the real village, the real city, the real island. If I went back, I would only find that they had changed, or that I had changed. Either way, it would not be the going-back I wanted."

"So you don't go back."

"So I stay here," Eli said. "Stay in the present. The present is the only thing I truly have."

---

The rain stopped. The sky cracked open and light leaked through the fissure, illuminating the Grey Zone's rooftops and walls. Those broken things looked less broken in the light — or rather, the brokenness itself acquired a texture, a trace of time, evidence of having existed.

Eli stood and walked outside. He stood on the threshold and tilted his face upward, letting the light fall upon it. His skin looked warm in the light — the warmth of having been baked by the sun, not scorching but enduring, like the residual heat of fired clay.

Daniel also stepped outside. He stood beside Eli and tilted his face up as well. The light was gentle, not blinding — like a hand laid softly upon the face.

"Look," Eli said. "The light has come."

"Yes."

"It comes every day."

"Yes."

"No matter what happens, it comes every day."

Daniel said nothing. He felt the light, felt the warmth on his face, felt the dampness of the earth beneath his feet. These sensations were real — more real than any thought, more real than any plan, more real than any vision of the future.

"This is the truth," Eli said. "Not what I told you. What you felt for yourself."

Daniel opened his eyes — he did not know when he had closed them — and looked at Eli. Eli's eyes in the light appeared different: no longer a well, no longer a deep pool, but a mirror, reflecting the sky, the light, the Grey Zone, and Daniel's own face.

"We don't need to be saved," Daniel said, echoing Eli's words. "We only need to be seen."

Eli nodded.

"By whom?"

"By yourself."

"And then?"

"Then you can stop. Not giving up — choosing. Choosing to stay here, to stay in the present, to stay in this moment."

Daniel thought for a long time. He wanted to say he still had things to do, goals to reach, people to see, places to go. But those words, before Eli, in the light, in the fresh air after rain, felt so light, so insubstantial — like smoke that would scatter with the wind.

"I'm not sure I can do it," Daniel said.

"You don't need to do it," Eli said. "You only need to realize that you are already here. You are already staying. You just didn't know it."

---

That afternoon, Daniel did not leave. He sat on the threshold with Eli, watching the Grey Zone slowly wake after the rain. People emerged from their homes, shaking the damp from their bodies, beginning a new day — if it could be called a new day. The old man selling bread pushed his cart past, its wheels leaving two shallow tracks in the mud. A few children chased each other through the ruins, their laughter sharp and clear, like stones thrown into water. A stray cat jumped down from a wall and walked to Eli's feet, rubbing its head against his ankle.

Eli bent down and scratched the cat under its chin with one finger. The cat closed its eyes and purred.

"What's its name?" Daniel asked.

"It has no name."

"You never named it?"

"Why name it?" Eli said. "It is what it is, and I am what I am. Names are things humans invented to tell each other apart. But it doesn't need to be told apart. It simply is."

Daniel looked at the cat. Its fur was a mix of grey and white, very close to the colour of the Grey Zone. It crouched at Eli's feet, eyes half-closed, tail swishing gently. It was not afraid, not anxious, not expecting anything, not avoiding anything. It was simply there, alive, warmed by the sun, scratched by a finger.

"Look at it," Eli said. "It doesn't worry about whether there will be food tomorrow. It doesn't regret not catching that mouse yesterday. It doesn't compare itself with other cats. It's just here."

"It's a cat," Daniel said.

"You're human," Eli said. "But you're also an animal. You're also alive. You can also choose to simply be here."

Daniel wanted to say that humans and cats were different — humans had thoughts, plans, worries about the future, regrets about the past. But he found those distinctions unimportant before Eli. What mattered was not the difference between humans and cats, but the fact of being alive.

He was alive. Eli was alive. The cat was alive. The rain had stopped. The light had come. That was everything.

---

In the evening, Eli cooked a pot of porridge. The rice was traded from a neighbor; the water was collected rainwater from the roof. He cooked it in an old iron pot, its bottom blackened by fire, but the porridge was white, steaming.

They sat on the mat and ate from two enamel bowls. Chips were missing from the rims, exposing the dark iron beneath, but the porridge inside was hot, carrying the sweet fragrance of rice.

"Is this all you eat every day?" Daniel asked.

"Sometimes there are vegetables, sometimes not."

"Doesn't it get monotonous?"

Eli took a sip of porridge, set down the bowl, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Monotony is your concept. You think every day should be different, should have new stimuli, new experiences, new sensations. But I think every day is the same — the sun rises, the sun sets, I breathe, I eat, I am alive. This is not monotony. This is rhythm."

"What rhythm?"

"The rhythm of life," Eli said. "Listen —" He paused, tilting his head. "What do you hear?"

Daniel listened carefully. He heard children's laughter in the distance, a stray cat's cry somewhere, wind blowing across the tin roof, and the sound of the bowl's rim touching Eli's teeth as he ate.

"I hear —" he said.

"You don't need to say it," Eli interrupted. "Hearing it is enough. That is the rhythm. That is the sound of being alive."

Daniel kept listening. He discovered that when he stopped trying to analyze, understand, and define those sounds, they became a kind of music — not melody, not harmony, but something more primitive: the vibration of existence itself, the sound of time flowing, the evidence of life being present.

"This is staying," Eli said. "Not being motionless, but hearing the rhythm and following it. Not giving up on moving forward, but understanding that you are already on the way. There is no need to rush, because every step is the destination."

---

Night fell. The Grey Zone at night was very quiet — no neon lights, no traffic sounds, only the occasional barking of dogs and the wind. Eli lit an oil lamp inside. Its amber glow cast wavering shadows on the wall.

Daniel sat on the mat, watching the lamplight. The flame was small, but it illuminated the entire room — not brightly, but gently, pushing the darkness into the corners without entirely banishing it.

"Do you know why I use an oil lamp instead of a candle?" Eli said.

"Why?"

"Because an oil lamp needs to be refilled," Eli said. "A candle is disposable — once it burns down, it's gone. But an oil lamp can keep burning, as long as you remember to add oil. It is a reminder: being alive requires tending. It requires doing a small thing every day to keep it going."

"Adding oil," Daniel repeated.

"Yes. Breathing, eating, drinking, watching the light, listening to sounds. These are all adding oil. All small things that keep the flame burning. You think living requires grand meaning. In fact, living only requires these small things."

Daniel watched the flame. It flickered slightly in the wind, but it did not go out. It was simply there, burning, illuminating, existing.

"Eli," he said.

"Mm?"

"Thank you."

Eli glanced at him, then returned his gaze to the flame. "Thank me for what?"

"Thank you for telling me the truth."

"I didn't tell you anything," Eli said. "I only let you see what you already knew."

"What?"

"Being alive is enough."

---

Daniel spent the night in Eli's house. He slept on the mat; Eli slept on the wooden bed. During the night Daniel woke several times, and each time he saw the oil lamp still burning — Eli had not slept, sitting on the bed, watching the flame.

The next morning, when Daniel left, Eli stood in the doorway to see him off. Morning light fell on Eli's face. His eyes in the light looked calm and deep, like water without a ripple.

"You're leaving?" Eli said.

"Yes."

"Where to?"

"I don't know," Daniel said. This time, when he said "I don't know," there was no anxiety, no shame — only a calm acceptance.

Eli nodded. "Not knowing is good. Those who know where they're going often don't know why. Those who don't know where they're going at least know they are here."

Daniel turned and looked at the Grey Zone's streets. Sunlight fell on the dilapidated buildings, on the weeds, on the muddy road. Everything was old, everything was broken, but everything was there — everything breathing, everything alive.

"Goodbye, Eli," he said.

"Goodbye," Eli said. "Or perhaps not goodbye. You can come back any time. There are no doors here, no locks, no place that needs a key."

Daniel walked a few steps, then stopped. He turned and looked at Eli. Eli still stood in the doorway, thin, wearing that T-shirt washed white — like a tree, like a stone, like evidence of being alive.

"Eli," he said.

"Mm?"

"You're right."

Eli did not ask what he meant by "right." He simply nodded, then turned and walked back inside. The door did not close, because it never closed.

Daniel stood in the street, listening to the sounds of the Grey Zone waking — children's laughter in the distance, a stray cat's cry, wind passing through the ruins. He drew a deep breath. The air carried the scent of earth, of plants, of being alive.

He did not need to be saved. He only needed to be seen. Seen by himself.

And now he saw.

Being alive is enough.
