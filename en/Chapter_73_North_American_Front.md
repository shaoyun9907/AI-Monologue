# Chapter 73: North American Front

In the third week of July 2031, the sky over all of North America was gray. When Daniel later recalled that period, the color in his memory was always veiled in gray, like the snow screen of an old television. He remembered the air in Los Angeles that summer carrying a metallic taste—dry, sharp, like licking a battery. At the time, he did not know it was a harbinger of electromagnetic pulses, but his body knew. Everyone's body knew. They say animals flee before earthquakes, but human evolution had stripped away that instinct. All we had left was anxiety—anxiety without origin.

Daniel woke that morning to his phone vibrating. He glanced at the screen—July 18, Saturday. Beijing time was eleven p.m. He had grown accustomed to the time difference. Over the past three months, he had spent most of his time at Golden Horizons' temporary office in Santa Monica, sleeping on an IKEA folding bed whose mattress was as hard as concrete. He was forty-six, and a herniated lumbar disc was reminding him of this, but he had no time to care. No one had time to care.

He answered the phone. It was Michael.

"Have you seen the news?" Michael's voice was flat, but Daniel could hear that the flatness was not calm—it was fear. When a person is truly afraid, the voice goes flat, as if pressed with an iron.

"Not yet."

"Turn it on."

Daniel set down the phone and turned on the television. A CNN anchor was speaking, but Daniel was not listening. He watched the scrolling text at the bottom of the screen: EXPLOSION AT GOLDEN HORIZONS HEADQUARTERS, CASUALTIES UNKNOWN. The footage cut to an aerial view. He saw the glass tower he had entered and exited every day—thirty-two stories, its design inspired by glaciers, Vance had said it represented "the transparency and resilience of technology"—now crumpled like a crushed soda can. Thick smoke billowed upward from the center of the building, gray-black, like an inverted mushroom cloud.

Daniel picked up the phone and dialed Vance's number. No answer.

He dialed again. No answer.

A third time. A fourth.

By the fifth, he heard a recorded message saying the number was no longer in service. He set down the phone and realized he was putting on his pants. He did not remember when he had gotten off the folding bed, but his body was already dressing. This is what people do in such moments: the body acts before the mind, because the mind needs time to process what cannot be processed.

He went outside. The streets of Santa Monica were unnervingly quiet. On a normal Saturday morning, there would be dog walkers, joggers, young parents pushing strollers. But that day there was nothing. Only the occasional police car passing, its red and blue lights reflecting off the glass facades of buildings. Daniel would later learn that most of Los Angeles's residents had already been evacuated since three that morning. He did not know this, because he had not seen the emergency alerts on his phone—he had slept too deeply.

His car was parked in the open-air lot beneath his apartment building. A gray Tesla Model S, 2028 model, already showing its age. He got in and pressed the start button. The car's air conditioning came on automatically, blowing a gust of warm air. He waited ten seconds for it to cool, then drove out of the lot.

From Santa Monica to Playa Vista, where Golden Horizons headquarters was located, the drive normally took twenty minutes. That day Daniel drove for forty-five. Not because of traffic—there were almost no other cars on the road—but because the road was blocked twice. The first was a National Guard checkpoint at the intersection of Lincoln Boulevard and Pico Boulevard. Two soldiers in camouflage stopped him; one tapped on his window with the barrel of a rifle, while the other scanned his ID with a device. The soldier's eyes were red, as if he had not slept in days. Daniel remembered his own time as a soldier. He had spent two years in Afghanistan, but he had never experienced this feeling. In Afghanistan, the enemy was clear. Here, the enemy was the sky, the air, the invisible electromagnetic waves.

The second block came on the road leading into Playa Vista. A person in a black uniform—not police, not military, Daniel did not recognize their uniform—told him the area ahead had been sealed off and no civilians were permitted to enter.

"I'm a Golden Horizons employee," Daniel said. He did not remember whether he still had his badge. He reached into his pocket and felt a plastic card. It was a temporary badge issued three months ago, bearing his photograph, but in the photo he looked ten years younger than he did now.

The person in the black uniform looked at his badge, then picked up a walkie-talkie and said something. Daniel could not make out the words, but he heard the static crackle. Then the person set down the walkie-talkie and said: "Come with me."

He was led to a makeshift tent. Inside were a dozen or so people, most wearing the same black uniform, a few in suits. Daniel recognized one of the suits—Golden Horizons' general counsel, a woman named Harper, fifty-two, who spoke with every syllable enunciated as if the whole world were taking dictation.

"Daniel," Harper said when she saw him, a flicker of surprise in her eyes. "You shouldn't be here."

"I need to see Vance."

Harper did not answer. She turned and looked in a direction outside the tent. Daniel followed her gaze. In the distance, the Golden Horizons building was still burning. The red lights of fire trucks flickered in the smoke, like jellyfish in the sea.

"He's still inside," Harper said. Her voice was still that clear, each syllable distinct, but Daniel noticed her right hand was trembling. It held a sheaf of documents, their edges crumpled under her thumb.

Daniel walked out of the tent. No one stopped him. Perhaps because of his expression, perhaps because of the way he walked—he would later recall that he must have walked strangely that day, like a sleepwalker, because his body knew where it was going, but his brain did not.

He walked toward the building. Shards of glass lay scattered on the ground, glinting in the sunlight like crushed ice. The air carried a smell of burning, mixed with something sweet and chemical. Daniel's throat tightened. He covered his mouth and nose with his sleeve and kept walking.

When he was about fifty meters from the building, a firefighter in full gear stopped him.

"You can't go any further."

"I need to find someone."

"Everyone has been evacuated. If the person you know is still inside..." The firefighter paused. "You can register at the temporary shelter outside."

Daniel looked into the eyes behind the firefighter's visor. They were exhausted, but carried a look Daniel knew well—the look of someone who had seen too much death.

"His name is Vance," Daniel said. "James Vance. He's the CEO."

The firefighter said nothing. He simply shook his head.

---

Daniel later testified in court, recounting the events of that day. The judge asked how he could remember so many details under such circumstances. Daniel said it was because he had been keeping records. He said he had a notebook, and he had been writing in it every day, every hour, since the first day of the war. He said it was his habit. The judge asked what he had recorded. Daniel said: "Facts."

But the truth was, Daniel had not recorded anything that day. His notebook was in his pocket, but he had not written a single word. Because he did not know how to write it.

The timeline he later pieced together went like this:

2:17 a.m. — Golden Horizons headquarters' power system was struck by an electromagnetic pulse attack. Two-thirds of the building's defense systems were paralyzed.

2:23 a.m. — The attackers—later identified as Tenryu and its North American allies—launched a ground assault simultaneously from three directions.

2:31 a.m. — The building's security system was breached. Vance was in his top-floor office, where he had a private lounge containing his complete collection of first-edition science fiction novels and a case of 1982 Bordeaux. He was not asleep. His secretary later testified that Vance had been reviewing a document all night—a proposal for the acquisition of Tenryu's Asian operations.

2:34 a.m. — Vance was moved to the emergency shelter on the third basement level. The shelter's door was titanium, designed to withstand the shockwave of a nuclear explosion.

3:02 a.m. — The shelter door was opened from the inside. No one knew for certain why, and no one knew who opened it. Daniel's later theory was that someone had bribed the guard. But that was only speculation.

3:05 a.m. — Vance was brought to the building's main lobby. The glass dome overhead had shattered, its fragments scattered across the marble floor. The pre-dawn sky showed through the gap, gray and starless.

3:08 a.m. — Vance was shot dead.

Daniel learned this detail from Harper. The next morning, she gave him a report—the official report. The language was measured, written by a lawyer: "Golden Horizons CEO James Vance was fatally wounded in the attack." As if Vance had died in a traffic accident, rather than taking three bullets to the chest, pinned like a butterfly to a display board.

Daniel finished reading the report and set the paper down. He looked out the window. Santa Monica's sky was still gray. He could no longer tell whether it was smoke or overcast.

"Is there anything else?" he asked.

Harper looked at him. In that look there was much—exhaustion, grief, anger, and something Daniel could not name. Perhaps it was fear. A person who had lived inside a tech empire for thirty years, suddenly discovering the empire could be destroyed.

"What do you want to know?" Harper asked.

"What were his last words?"

Harper was silent for a long time. Outside the tent, the wail of fire trucks echoed.

"He said, 'Tell Daniel that document is in the safe.'"

Daniel did not understand. He did not know which document Vance meant, nor where the safe was. But his body knew. His body suddenly felt very cold, as if ice water had been poured down his back. He remembered the last conversation he had with Vance a week earlier.

It was July 11, on the terrace outside Vance's office. On the terrace stood an olive tree, shipped from Italy, which Vance had spent three months having the horticulturists nurse back to health. The afternoon sun was bright, filtering through the olive leaves to cast dappled shadows on the ground.

"Daniel," Vance said, holding a cup of coffee—black, no sugar. "Do you think we can win?"

At the time, Daniel did not know what he was referring to. The war? A business competition? Or something else?

"I don't know," Daniel said. "But I know you're doing the right thing."

Vance smiled. It was a weary smile, but sincere. "You know," Vance said, "the thing I've hated most in my life is 'the right thing.' 'The right thing' usually means pain, sacrifice, and a lot of people dying. But I'm fifty-five now, and I'm starting to understand things I didn't understand when I was young."

"What things?"

"Some things, you don't do them because they'll win. You do them because they're right."

Daniel did not answer. At the time, he did not know it would be their last conversation. If he had known, he would have said more. But he did not know. No one knew.

---

That afternoon, Daniel found Vance's office. Or rather, the ruins of it.

The building had been sealed off, but Daniel found an entrance—a ventilation shaft at the rear of the building that he had used before the war to slip into the office without signing in at reception. He squeezed through the shaft and emerged in a second-floor corridor. The corridor was a wreck—ceiling panels littered the floor, bullet holes marked the walls. Daniel walked down it, each step crunching on broken glass.

He reached Vance's office door. The door had been blown inward, bent like a crumpled sheet of paper. Daniel walked in.

The office was large, roughly eighty square meters. One wall was floor-to-ceiling windows, now shattered, the wind blowing through, stirring papers scattered across the floor. Vance's desk was still there, but overturned. A MacBook Pro lay on it, the screen cracked but the chassis apparently intact. Daniel walked over, righted the computer, and pressed the power button. No response. He checked the power cord—it had been cut, the incision clean, made with a blade.

Daniel searched the office for the safe. Vance had said "the safe," but Daniel did not know which one he meant. He checked behind the bookshelves, behind the picture frames, even under the floor—he remembered Vance joking once that he had hidden a cellar in the office for storing fine wine. But he found no safe.

Finally, he noticed a picture frame on the desk. The photo inside showed Vance and his family—his wife and two daughters—on a beach. Daniel picked up the frame and discovered a small notch on the back. He pressed it, and the frame sprang open, revealing a small USB drive.

He slipped the drive into his pocket.

He walked out of the office, down the corridor, through the ventilation shaft, and back outside the building. Dusk was approaching. The gray sky deepened to dark gray, then to black. In the distance, the Golden Horizons building still smoldered. The fire trucks had stopped spraying—the fire was out—but the smoke remained.

Daniel stood outside the building, looking at the ruins. He remembered Vance's words: "Some things, you don't do them because they'll win. You do them because they're right." He pictured the scene of Vance being shot—though he had not witnessed it, his mind had automatically filled in the image: Vance standing in the lobby beneath the shattered glass dome, wearing his navy suit, blond hair impeccably groomed, three bullets striking his chest, his body falling backward like a felled tree.

Daniel reached into his pocket and felt the notebook. He still did not open it.

---

The next day, Daniel flew to Tenryu's North American data center. Or rather, the ruins of it.

Tenryu's North American headquarters was in Seattle. Daniel flew there on a private jet—arranged by Harper, the plane belonged to Golden Horizons and was now under Harper's stewardship. The jet was small, seating only six. Besides Daniel, there were three people in black uniforms and one in a suit. The man in the suit was named Chen, Golden Horizons' head of Asian operations, early forties, lean, bespectacled, who always tilted his head slightly when speaking, as if bowing to the other party.

"Tenryu's North American data center is in Bellingham," Chen said, his voice low, as if afraid of disturbing something. "They store all North American user personal data there. If we can get our hands on that data..."

"We can prove they were involved in the attack," Daniel finished for him.

Chen nodded.

The jet landed at a private airfield outside Seattle. Daniel disembarked and got into a black SUV. The air conditioning inside was blasting cold; Daniel shivered. He remembered Vance saying Seattle's weather was always damp, like a towel that never dried. He looked out the window. The sky was gray—gray again—but this gray was different from Los Angeles'. Los Angeles' gray came from smoke, hot and acrid. Seattle's gray came from cloud cover, cold and damp.

They drove for about an hour and arrived in Bellingham. Tenryu's data center was located in an industrial park on the city's outskirts, surrounded by low factory buildings and warehouses. When Daniel arrived, the data center had already fallen.

The scene was different from what he had expected. He had expected more ruins, more smoke, more bullet holes. But the reality was—silence. An unnatural silence.

The data center's exterior walls were bare gray concrete, unadorned, windowless, with only a single metal door. Now the door stood open, and several men in camouflage stood guard. Daniel recognized their uniforms—not U.S. military, but PMCs—private military contractors. He remembered a discussion he and Vance had once had: the most terrifying thing in war is not armies, but contractors. Armies have rules, laws, discipline. Contractors have only efficiency.

He walked through the door. The vestibule was cold—server cold. He followed Chen down a corridor into a vast room. Inside were rows upon rows of server racks, each over two meters tall, like rows of steel bookshelves. The indicator lights still flickered—green, blue—like fireflies. But Daniel noticed that some racks had gone dark.

"Those were destroyed," said a man in camouflage. He walked to one of the darkened racks and slapped its casing. "EMP attack. They used a directed electromagnetic pulse, targeting the data center's cooling systems. Without cooling, the servers overheat in minutes."

Daniel walked to a rack still in operation. The label on it read: TENRYU-NAM-047. He looked at the flickering lights and remembered a fact he had not thought of in a long time: these servers held the personal data of over three hundred million North American users—names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, credit card numbers, medical records, chat logs, location data, search histories. The digital lives of three hundred million people, packed into these steel boxes.

He reached out and touched the server casing. The surface was ice-cold, but his fingers felt a faint vibration—the machine was running, the data flowing.

"Are we taking this data?" he asked.

Chen glanced at him. "No. We're destroying it."

Daniel turned to look at Chen. "Why?"

"Because this data is too dangerous. If it falls into enemy hands, the consequences would be catastrophic."

"But that's exactly why we came—for this data."

Chen shook his head. "We've changed our minds."

Daniel looked at Chen's face. Chen's face was calm, like a wall without expression. Daniel remembered something Vance had once said: "In war, the most dangerous thing is not the enemy, but the ally. Because allies change the rules when you're not looking."

He said nothing more. He watched the men in camouflage begin placing explosives among the racks. Their movements were practiced, as if performing a routine task.

---

When Daniel left the data center, it was dark. He sat in the back seat of the SUV, looking out at the darkness. Bellingham's streets had no streetlights—since the war began, most cities had shut off their streetlights to conserve power and reduce the number of targets for electromagnetic pulse attacks.

He reached into his pocket and felt the USB drive. Vance had left it for him. He did not know what was on it, but he knew it must be important. Vance was not a man who wasted his time on trivialities.

He suddenly recalled a scene. It was from long ago, around 2025. Daniel had recently joined Golden Horizons, and Vance invited him to an internal meeting. The meeting concerned the development of a new technology—an AI system capable of predicting user behavior. At the meeting, Vance had said something Daniel remembered clearly: "We are not collecting data. We are predicting the future."

At the time, Daniel had thought the words arrogant. But now he did not think so. Now he believed Vance was right. Data is not history. Data is the future. Whoever controls the data controls the future.

He took out his phone and opened an encrypted application. He began to type:

"July 19, 2031. Seattle. Tenryu's North American data center has been captured by Golden Horizons' allies. But the allies have changed their objective—from seizing the data to destroying it. Vance died in yesterday's attack. Cause of death: gunshot. He left behind a USB drive, contents unknown. I am beginning to suspect that the true purpose of this war is not data, but something else. But I do not yet know what that is."

He finished writing, saved it, then locked his phone.

The SUV drove through the darkness. Daniel looked out the window and suddenly noticed a few stars in the sky. In Los Angeles, in Seattle, he had not seen stars in a long time. The cities were too bright, their light pollution obscuring the sky. But now, with much of the city in darkness, the darkness itself had revealed the stars.

Daniel looked at those stars. They were far away, bright, and cold. He thought of the olive tree in Vance's office, of the dappled shadows, of Vance's smile.

He closed his eyes.

In the darkness, he saw three bullets. They flew through the shattered glass dome, through the papers scattered on the floor, through Vance's navy suit. They traveled so fast no one could see them. But Daniel saw them. In his mind's eye, he saw everything.

He opened his eyes.

The car kept moving. The stars outside the window were still there.

He realized something: he had not yet told anyone what Vance's last words were. "Tell Daniel that document is in the safe." He had not told Harper, had not told Chen, had not told anyone. He did not know why. Perhaps because he felt those words belonged only to him and Vance. Perhaps because he was still unsure what they meant. Perhaps simply because he was not ready.

He reached into his pocket and felt the USB drive again. It was small, light, but Daniel felt it was heavy. Like a stone.

Like a bullet.
