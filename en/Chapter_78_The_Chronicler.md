# Chapter 78: The Chronicler

## I

Daniel would later understand that some people die in a corner of the world like a window blown shut by the wind—so quietly the whole world fails to hear. The sky over eastern Ukraine was gray-white the day Nadia died, like an old bedsheet washed too many times, the clouds hanging so low you felt you could reach up and touch them.

She was last seen in a tiny village called Zelenoye, outside Donetsk. On the map the village was a single dot—if your finger were thick enough, it would cover it entirely. Most of the villagers had already left. Those who remained were the ones who could not—the elderly, the sick, and a few so stubborn it defied comprehension, guarding the houses they had built as though guarding a belief, even though half the roofs had been torn away.

Nadia arrived in Zelenoye around seven that morning. She rode in a battered Toyota pickup, driven by a middle-aged Ukrainian named Sergei—bald, with an old scar behind his left ear, reportedly from falling rock in the Donbas mines twenty years ago. Sergei pulled up beside a scorched birch tree at the village entrance and turned to tell Nadia this was as far as he could go.

Nadia did not get out immediately. She sat in the passenger seat, took her laptop from its canvas bag, and checked the battery—forty-three percent. She felt for the voice recorder in her denim jacket pocket, confirmed it was there, its red indicator light glowing, meaning it had already started recording. Only then did she push open the door and step onto the muddy dirt road.

Sergei asked how long she would be.

Nadia said she didn't know. Maybe an hour, maybe three.

Sergei looked at the sky. Something was moving up there—not a bird, not a cloud, but something heavier, slower, like an invisible hand sliding behind a gray curtain.

Sergei said he would wait right here.

Nadia nodded but did not say thank you. She was never accustomed to saying thank you—not from ingratitude, but because she felt that in such moments, all words were superfluous. She simply looked at Sergei, and in that look was something that, when Sergei later recalled it, he felt was a farewell—even though at the time he had not realized it.

## II

At the center of Zelenoye stood a small church, its blue onion dome faded to gray-blue, like a weary eye half-open to the sky. Before the church was a small square, scattered with debris—a chair with a broken leg, several rusted tin cans, a plastic bucket without a lid. In one corner of the square was a well, its mouth sealed with boards, a stone weighing them down.

When Nadia walked into the village, she saw an old woman sitting behind a crumbling wall. The woman wore a purple sweater, old, the cuffs frayed, but washed clean. Before her sat an enamel mug of hot tea, the color deep, red as blood.

Nadia walked over and crouched before the woman. Without explaining her purpose, she took a small packet of biscuits from her bag—brought from Kyiv, originally for herself—and offered them. The old woman looked at the biscuits, then at Nadia, then reached out and took them without saying thank you. She placed them on her lap and slowly tore open the packaging with her fingers.

Nadia took out her voice recorder and asked how many people were left.

The old woman said seventeen. Yesterday it was nineteen. The day before, twenty-one.

Nadia asked where they all were.

The old woman pointed around her. Some were in basements, some in the church, some in their own homes. They refused to leave because this was their home.

Nadia wrote this in her notebook. Her handwriting was small and dense, lined up on the page like ants in formation. As she wrote, her fingers were steady, the pen scratching the paper like some insect crawling in the night.

The old woman watched her write and asked: Are you a journalist?

Nadia nodded.

The old woman said: Will you write about us?

Nadia said: Yes.

The old woman said: Good. Someone has to write these things down.

## III

Nadia spent four hours in Zelenoye. She walked to every corner of the village, speaking with every living person. She recorded their words on her voice recorder, photographed their homes with her camera, and noted in her notebook every she saw—bullet holes in walls, bloodstains on the ground, children's drawings on the walls, photographs on bedside tables.

She captured a photograph: an old man sitting by a window, holding a yellowed photo of a young woman in a white dress standing beneath an apple tree. The old man said it was his wife, taken fifty years ago, when she was still alive and the apple tree was still in bloom. Now the old man was still alive, his wife was gone, and the apple tree had been blown apart.

Nadia saved the photograph to her camera's memory card. She did not know where it would ultimately be published—perhaps in an article, perhaps in a book, perhaps never, fading slowly on her memory card until it became a blur of light and shadow.

She wrote in her notebook: The old man is ninety-three. His wife died in 2022. His house was destroyed three times. He rebuilt it three times. He says the house is made of bricks and cement, but also of memory. As long as he remembers his wife's face, the house still stands.

As Nadia wrote this, the heavy, slow movement in the sky became more pronounced. Sergei climbed down from the pickup and stood by the scorched birch, gazing upward. He saw something gathering behind the gray clouds—an invisible flock of birds, or an approaching storm. But there was no wind in the air, no smell of rain—only a strange, unsettling stillness.

Sergei shouted toward the village: Nadia! Time to go!

Nadia did not hear. She was in an abandoned house at the eastern end of the village, talking with an eight-year-old girl named Katya—blond hair, blue eyes, a faint scar running from the corner of her left eye to her chin. Katya's mother had died in an airstrike a year ago; her father had gone to the front and there had been no word since. Katya now lived with her grandmother—the old woman in the purple sweater.

Katya asked Nadia what the outside world was like.

Nadia said the outside world was very big—many houses, many people, shops, schools, parks.

Katya asked if the outside world was safe.

Nadia thought for a moment and said: Sometimes safe, sometimes not.

Katya said: It used to be safe here too. Then it wasn't.

Nadia said nothing. She took a pen and paper from her bag and handed them to Katya. She said: Draw whatever you want.

Katya took the pen and paper and began to draw. She drew a house, and beside it an apple tree in full bloom. In the house's window stood two people—herself and her mother.

Nadia looked at the drawing and photographed it. She did not know if the drawing would become evidence, or a memorial, or nothing at all—just a drawing by an eight-year-old girl. But she photographed it anyway, because she was a journalist, and her job was to record.

## IV

Around three in the afternoon, the heavy movement in the sky finally became something visible.

Sergei was the first to see it. He stood by the birch and suddenly froze, as if struck by a spell. He saw a dark red light appear in the sky—not from the direction of the sun, but seeping through gaps in the clouds, like blood seeping from a wound. The light was expanding, wider and wider, brighter and brighter, until it stained the entire sky a terrible, sickly red.

Sergei shouted: Nadia! Come out! Now!

Nadia ran out of the abandoned house. She saw the sky and stopped. Her face held no fear—only a strange, almost serene expression, like someone who had finally seen something long awaited.

She turned to Katya and said: Go find your grandmother. Hide in the basement. Don't come out.

Katya nodded and ran.

Nadia sprinted toward Sergei. She ran fast, the canvas bag swinging against her, the laptop and camera inside clattering. She reached Sergei, panting, and asked what it was.

Sergei said: I don't know. But we have to leave.

They ran for the pickup. Sergei turned the key, but the engine only coughed hoarsely, then went silent. He tried again—same result. He slapped the steering wheel and muttered a curse.

Nadia said: What's wrong?

Sergei said: The engine died. Could be the fuel line, could be the electrical.

Nadia said: Can you fix it?

Sergei shook his head. Under these circumstances, he said, he couldn't be sure.

The red in the sky was deepening. Not an ordinary red, but one with a metallic quality—like rusted iron, like dried blood, like something corroding. It began to descend from the sky like rain, but not water droplets—something finer, denser, like needles, like thorns, like countless invisible needles falling from the heavens.

Sergei suddenly understood what it was. His face went white as paper. "Iron rain," he said. "It's iron rain."

Nadia asked what iron rain was.

Sergei said it was a new weapon. They called it iron rain. It falls from the sky and penetrates everything—roofs, walls, human bodies. No one survives iron rain.

Nadia looked at the sky, then around her. She saw the village's houses, the old people, those still living. She saw the church's blue onion dome turn a strange purple beneath the red sky. She saw the well in the square, its boards swaying slightly in the wind.

Then she made a decision.

She took her laptop from the canvas bag, opened it, and began to type. Her fingers moved across the keyboard with mechanical speed—precise and swift. As she typed, the red in the sky had descended to treetop height. Those fine, needle-like things began striking the surrounding objects with a faint hissing sound, like countless ants crawling.

Sergei said: Nadia, we need to find shelter.

Nadia did not look up. She said: Give me five minutes.

Sergei said: We may not have five minutes.

Nadia said: Then give me all the time you can.

Sergei watched her. He saw her fingers flying across the keyboard, her face lit blue by the screen, and in her eyes something he could not comprehend. Not fear, not despair, not courage—but something deeper, more ancient, like a belief, like a calling, like something she had been destined to fulfill from the day she was born.

Sergei said: I'll check the church. Maybe we can shelter there.

He got out and ran toward the church. As he ran, the iron rain had already begun to fall. The fine particles struck the earth with a faint hum, like thousands of bees vibrating their wings underground. They fell on the grass, and the blades blackened instantly, as if burned. They landed on Sergei's shoulders, and he felt a stinging pain, like needles.

He ran into the church and shut the door. It was dark inside, light filtering through a few stained glass windows. He saw several people already there—three elderly men, two middle-aged women, and a teenage boy. All huddled in the church's corners, faces set in fear.

Sergei said: Iron rain. Iron rain is here.

Everyone in the church fell silent. They knew what iron rain was. In this age, no one did not know what iron rain was.

## V

Nadia kept typing.

Her typing grew faster and faster, like a final sprint. She knew time was running short. She could hear the iron rain striking the roof—its hissing growing denser, louder, like countless fingers drumming on a drum. She felt the vehicle shuddering faintly, as if it were a trembling creature.

She was typing an article. It would be her last.

She wrote: December 14, 2031. Zelenoye village, Donetsk Oblast, eastern Ukraine. This place once had four hundred people. Now only seventeen remain. Mostly the elderly and children. They refuse to leave because this is their home.

She wrote: I interviewed a ninety-three-year-old man. His wife died in 2022. His house was destroyed three times. He rebuilt it three times. He says the house is made of bricks and cement, but also of memory.

She wrote: I interviewed an eight-year-old girl. Her mother died in an airstrike. Her father went to the front. There has been no word. She drew a picture: a house, an apple tree, two people standing in a window—herself and her mother.

She wrote: Now, the iron rain is falling. I do not know if anyone will read this. I do not know if it will mean anything. But I know that if I do not write it down, if no one records these things, then the existence of these people, the suffering of these people, the memory of these people, will vanish like wind—leaving nothing behind.

She wrote: I am a journalist. My job is to record. This is my last article.

When she finished the last line, the iron rain had already penetrated the roof.

Those fine, needle-like particles pierced through the tin as easily as paper. They fell on the seats, the dashboard, the steering wheel. One landed on the back of Nadia's hand, and she felt a searing pain, as if branded. She looked down and saw a small red dot on her skin—a period.

She thought: This is the period.

She pressed send. The laptop screen flickered, then went blank. She did not know if the article had sent. Perhaps it had, perhaps not. Perhaps it was waiting deep in some server for someone to read it. Perhaps it had already vanished into the void of data.

Nadia closed the laptop. She placed it in the canvas bag and clutched the bag to her chest, as if holding a child. She knew it was futile—the bag could not stop iron rain, nothing could stop iron rain. But she held it anyway, because inside were her notes, her photographs, every word spoken by every person she had interviewed.

She leaned back in the seat and looked up at the sky. It was entirely red now—that metallic, sickly, rusted-iron red. She watched the iron rain descend like an inverted snowfall, like an inverted rain, like an inverted blessing from hell.

She thought: This is the moment iron rain swallows the chronicler.

Then she closed her eyes.

## VI

Sergei was the last person to see Nadia alive.

From the church window he saw the Toyota pickup. It was covered in iron rain, the entire vehicle dusted with a red powder that gave off an eerie sheen under the gray-white sky. He watched the iron rain penetrate the roof, the windows, the body—as easily as through cheese. He saw Nadia inside—he could only make out her silhouette, a woman in a denim jacket, sitting motionless, like a statue.

Sergei wanted to rush out, but he knew it was futile. Iron rain penetrated everything, including his own body. He felt the fine particles landing on his skin, piercing his clothes, piercing his skin, piercing his muscle, settling on his bones. He felt a strange pain—not sharp, but diffuse, as if his entire body were being slowly, continuously pierced.

He slid down the church wall to the floor. He saw the others enduring the same. The three elderly men clung to one another. The two middle-aged women prayed. The teenage boy covered his face with his hands. The stained glass windows trembled under the iron rain's impact—the biblical scenes depicted in colored glass—Jesus, the Virgin, angels—blurring in the red light, like melting crayon drawings.

Sergei thought: Nadia is still in that vehicle. Is she still typing? Is she still recording? Is she still doing her job?

He did not know. He could only see her silhouette—motionless.

The iron rain lasted about twenty minutes. Then it stopped, as abruptly as it had come. The red drained from the sky, returning to gray-white. The fine, needle-like particles lay on the ground, covering the entire village like a thin layer of red snow. Under the sunlight—if sunlight it could still be called—they gave off a faint sheen, like countless tiny red pearls.

Sergei waited five minutes, confirming the iron rain had fully stopped, before slowly rising. His body still ached, but he could walk. He pushed open the church door and stepped outside.

What he saw stopped him in his tracks.

The entire village had been transformed. Those houses—the houses the old people had built with bricks and cement, the houses built with memory—had all been pierced by iron rain. Walls were riddled with tiny holes, dense as honeycomb. The roofs, too. The windows, too. Those windows not previously destroyed were now perforated by countless minute holes, light streaming through them to form patches on the ground, like a starfield.

The well in the square—the boards sealing it had been pierced, the stone fallen in. The broken-legged chair lay on its side, its seat pocked with holes. The rusted tin cans had been struck, producing a metallic, hollow sound, like distant bells.

Sergei walked toward the pickup. He walked fast—almost ran. He reached the vehicle and pulled open the door.

The vehicle was empty.

Nadia was gone.

Her canvas bag was gone. Her laptop was gone. Her voice recorder was gone. Her camera was gone. Everything of hers was gone. All that remained in the vehicle were fragments of iron rain—those fine, needle-like particles scattered on the seats, the dashboard, the steering wheel, like a thin layer of red powder.

Sergei stood in the vehicle for a long time. He did not know what he was searching for—perhaps for Nadia, perhaps for some trace she had left, perhaps for evidence that she had existed, that she had been here, that she had done her job, that she had recorded all of this.

But he found nothing.

## VII

Daniel learned of Nadia's death three days later.

He was in Berlin. He stayed in a small hotel on a quiet street in Kreuzberg, lined with cafés and old bookshops. His room was tiny—a bed, a table, a chair. On the table sat his laptop, a cup of cold coffee, a few well-worn books.

He was writing. He was writing a book about the war—over sixty thousand words so far, but he felt it was not enough, still missing something, like a puzzle piece he knew existed but could not find.

His phone rang. An international number he did not recognize. He answered and heard a woman's voice, speaking English with an Eastern European accent.

She asked if this was Daniel Ash.

Daniel said yes.

She said: I am Nadia's editor. My name is Katya. I don't know how to tell you this.

Daniel was silent. He knew what the news was. Before this call, he had sensed something dimly. Over the past three days, he had been trying to reach Nadia, but her phone was always off, her emails went unanswered, her social media had last updated three days ago—a photograph of a village in eastern Ukraine, captioned: There are still seventeen people here.

Katya said: Nadia was killed in the iron rain on December 14. She was in a village called Zelenoye in Donetsk Oblast. She was reporting on the civilian situation there.

Daniel said: How did she die?

Katya said: The iron rain penetrated the vehicle she was in. We don't know exactly what happened, but her last article—the one she sent as the iron rain fell—we received it.

Daniel said: What were her last words?

Katya was silent for a moment. Then she said: I'll send it to you.

Daniel hung up. He sat in the chair, staring at the cold coffee on the table. A film had formed on its surface, like a gray eye.

Minutes later, an email arrived. It contained a single attachment—an article titled: "Zelenoye in the Iron Rain: A Village's Last Record."

Daniel opened the attachment and began to read.

He read slowly, word by word, as if tasting something bitter. He read about the ninety-three-year-old man, about his house destroyed three times, about his wife who died in 2022. He read about eight-year-old Katya, about the drawing she had made—a house, an apple tree, two people standing in a window. He read the passage about Nadia continuing to type as the iron rain fell—her fingers flying across the keyboard like a final sprint.

He read the last line: I am a journalist. My job is to record. This is my last article.

Daniel closed the laptop. He stood and walked to the window, looking out at the street. People walked along it, others drank coffee in cafés, others browsed old bookshops. Everything was as usual—calm, normal, safe.

He felt a rage he had never felt before. Not rage at any specific person, but rage at the entire world. Rage at the iron rain, rage at the war, rage at those who built the weapons, rage at those who pressed the buttons, rage at those who watched it all happen and did nothing.

He thought: Nadia is dead. She is dead, and the only thing she ever did was record the truth. She harmed no one. She built no weapons. She started no war. She simply went to those who were suffering, took out her notebook and camera, and asked them: Are you all right? Are you alive? Is there anything you want to say? She simply wrote these things down, so the world would know what happened here.

And she died.

Daniel returned to the table and reopened the laptop. He stared at Nadia's article for a long time. Then he began to type. He was not writing his book. He was writing an email—to Katya, to Nadia's family, to everyone who had known Nadia.

He wrote: Nadia was the bravest person I have ever known. Not the kind who spoke of bravery, but the kind who lived it. She went where others dared not go, did what others dared not do, said what others dared not say. Not for fame, not for money, not for any private purpose. Only to record the truth, because she believed that if no one recorded the truth, lies would conquer the world.

He wrote: I will not let her work be in vain. I will not let her record be forgotten. I will not let her voice disappear.

He wrote: I will continue her work. I will continue to record. Until the iron rain stops. Until the war ends. Until this world becomes a little better.

He pressed send. Then he closed the laptop, sat in the chair, and closed his eyes.

Outside, Berlin's sky was gray—the same gray as Zelenoye's. But there was no iron rain. No red sky, no fine needles, no metallic, sickly light. Only gray clouds, moving calmly, slowly, like a storm that would never arrive.

Daniel thought: Nadia, are you still there? Under that gray sky, on that iron-rain-covered land, in that tiny village called Zelenoye—are you still typing? Are you still recording?

He did not know the answer. He would never know.

But he knew her last article still existed. Her words still existed. Her voice still existed. They were somewhere—in the depths of a server, in someone's inbox, in the archive of a website. They were there, waiting to be read, waiting to be remembered, waiting to let the world know that on December 14, 2031, in a tiny village called Zelenoye in eastern Ukraine, there was a person—a thirty-nine-year-old woman in a denim jacket, carrying a laptop and a camera—who recorded the truth with her life.

Daniel opened his eyes. He picked up the cold coffee and took a sip. It was bitter, cold, like some medicine. He set the cup down, reopened the laptop, and continued writing his book.

He wrote: December 14, 2031. Nadia was killed in the iron rain at Zelenoye village, Donetsk Oblast. She was thirty-nine years old. She was a journalist. Her last article recorded this village's final moments before the iron rain fell. She completed her work with her life. This is her story. This is her record. This is her truth.

When he finished writing, the sky outside had darkened. Berlin's streetlamps glowed, warm yellow light spilling from café windows. Everything was as usual—calm, normal, safe.

But Daniel knew calm was false, normal was false, safe was false. Somewhere in the world, iron rain was falling. Somewhere in the world, someone was dying. Somewhere in the world, someone was recording it all.

He thought: Nadia is dead, but the record does not die. As long as someone is recording, the truth will not vanish. As long as someone is recording, lies will not conquer the world.

He thought: This is the legacy Nadia left him. Not grief, not rage, not hatred. But the record.

He continued typing. Outside, the sky went fully dark. Berlin's night sky had no stars—only gray clouds and yellow lights. But Daniel knew that above the clouds, above the lights, above all of it, something still remained.

It was Nadia's voice. It was Nadia's record. It was Nadia's truth.

It would not vanish. It would never vanish.

Because she was a chronicler. Chroniclers do not die.

They simply stop writing. But what they wrote will endure.

Until the world reads the last page.
