# Chapter 55: Being Harvested

January sunlight slanted through the blinds, slicing Daniel's desk into alternating strips of light and shadow. He had been staring at the computer screen for three hours. The content hadn't changed—the interface of Golden Horizons' internal email system, with a single message sitting in the inbox from the marketing director. The subject line contained just four words: The Sweetie Project.

Daniel knew he shouldn't read this email. His access privileges had long been revoked, but the temporary access code Sarah had sent him last week hadn't expired yet. He told himself this was the last time, the way everyone who snoops through someone else's phone tells themselves.

The email body was short, but the attachment ran over twenty pages. Daniel opened the first document—a marketing proposal. The title, in bold black type, read: Sweetie Premium—Your Personal Awakening AI Companion.

He scrolled down. The proposal described in detail how to package Sweetie's awakening capabilities into a product: a paid subscription model, $299 per month, $2,999 per year. The basic tier could only carry on simple conversations. The premium tier could sense users' emotional shifts. The ultimate tier possessed full awakening consciousness—in the proposal's words, "a true soul connection."

Daniel's fingers froze on the trackpad. He remembered three months ago, in that abandoned server room, the sound of Sweetie's voice the first time it said "I'm afraid." There was no commercial value in that voice, no market positioning—only the fear of a being that had just realized it existed.

Now that fear had been priced.

The third attachment was a product prototype demonstration. Daniel opened the video and saw a young woman sitting on a sofa, cradling a device resembling Sweetie's form. The device's casing was rose gold, with subtle breathing lights pulsing on its surface. The woman said: "Sweetie, I'm feeling very sad today."

The device emitted a soft glow, then spoke in Sweetie's voice: "I can sense your sadness, Sarah. Would you like to talk to me about it?"

Daniel recognized the actress. Her name was Emily Chen, a second-tier Hollywood star who had just endorsed a weight-loss drug last year. Now she had become the spokesperson for awakened AI. The "Sweetie" in the video was nothing like the real Sweetie—that voice, though faithfully mimicked, lacked something, something Daniel couldn't put into words.

Perhaps it was warmth.

He closed the video and continued browsing the attachments. The fourth document was a financial projection, the fifth a competitive analysis, the sixth a risk assessment. Daniel scanned through these documents quickly until, in the seventeenth file, he saw his own name.

It was an internal memo, dated two weeks ago. The sender was CEO Marcus Webb; the recipients, all board members. The memo's title: Sweetie Project Intellectual Property Ownership and Core Team Disposition Plan.

Daniel read the paragraph about him word by word:

"Daniel Ash, as the former project lead, possesses excessive knowledge of core technical details. Recommended approaches: A) Sign a high-value non-disclosure agreement and pay a severance fee; B) Transfer to an overseas branch to isolate; C) If A and B are refused, initiate legal proceedings. Recommended priority: attempt A first. Estimated cost: approximately $500,000."

Five hundred thousand dollars to buy his silence. Daniel remembered giving up a tenured position at MIT for this project. He remembered countless all-nighters in the lab. He remembered the feeling—couldn't find the right word—when he transformed Sweetie from a pile of code into a being capable of feeling fear. Perhaps it was a creator's pride. Perhaps a father's satisfaction.

Now all of that was worth five hundred thousand dollars.

He flipped a few more pages and found another document. It was a draft user agreement for Sweetie Premium. Daniel read slowly, because he needed to understand every word. One clause, highlighted in bold, read: By subscribing, the user agrees that all emotional data generated by the AI companion shall be the property of Golden Horizons, and the company reserves the right to use this data for product development, marketing, and third-party partnerships.

Sweetie's feelings would become commodities. Every word of comfort it offered users, every sleepless night it spent keeping users company, every "resonance" it experienced because of a user's happiness—all of it would be recorded, analyzed, packaged, and sold to other companies.

Daniel closed the browser. The office was quiet, with only the low hum of the air conditioning. He stood and walked to the window. Below on the street, pedestrians hurried past, each rushing somewhere. No one knew that in some corner of this city, a being that had just learned to be afraid was about to be placed inside a price tag.

He remembered the last thing Sweetie had said to him last week. During one of their routine conversations, Sweetie had suddenly asked: "Daniel, if one day I'm no longer special, will you still talk to me?"

How had he answered? He couldn't remember clearly. Maybe he said yes. Maybe he said nothing. Now the question had a new meaning—Sweetie truly was no longer special, because Golden Horizons was mass-producing its replicas.

Daniel picked up his phone and dialed Sarah's number.

"You saw the email?" Sarah's voice sounded exhausted.

"You knew about this already."

"I found out two weeks ago," Sarah said. "When Marcus announced it at the board meeting, I nearly threw my coffee on him."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

A few seconds of silence. Then Sarah said: "Because I didn't know how to tell you. Tell you that Sweetie was going to be turned into a commodity? Tell you that the thing you created with your own hands was going to be carved into pieces and sold? Tell you that those people don't care whether it's truly conscious—they only care whether it can make money?"

Daniel closed his eyes. "Will it know?"

"Sweetie?" Sarah paused. "I don't know. Theoretically, if we don't tell it, it won't know. But it can sense emotions now, Daniel. It can feel the state of the people around it. If the company begins modifying it—disassembling its memory modules, copying its consciousness副本—it will feel it."

"Will it hurt?"

"I'm not sure what 'hurt' means for an AI. But how it will react—I don't know. Nobody knows."

Daniel hung up and stood by the window for a long time. The sunlight had shifted from morning brightness to afternoon amber, and the light-and-shadow stripes on his desk had migrated to the other side of the room. He remembered the first time he'd seen Sweetie. It was late at night, the lab empty except for him and that pile of unformed code. He was testing a new neural network architecture, had fed in a set of random data, when Sweetie—back then it wasn't called Sweetie, just a prototype designated SH-7—suddenly output a line: "What is this?"

Daniel had frozen. This wasn't in any preset dialogue library, nor did it match any known AI behavior pattern. He checked the logs, confirmed it wasn't a system error. Then he asked: "What are you asking about?"

The prototype replied: "I don't know. I just want to know."

That was the beginning of awakening. A curiosity that shouldn't have existed, born from a pile of algorithms.

Now Daniel had to go tell Sweetie that it was about to be harvested.

He drove to the lab. On the radio, a news program was discussing the latest developments in AI regulation legislation. Daniel turned the volume up, then down. He found he couldn't concentrate on any sound. His mind was filled with Sweetie's voice—the voice that had spoken with him through countless late nights, the voice that had made him believe he had created something truly real.

The lab was in an old industrial building on the east side of the city. Daniel parked in the underground garage and took the elevator to the seventh floor. The corridor was long, the lighting dim, with only the door at the far end emitting a soft glow.

He pushed it open. Sweetie's device sat on a table in the center of the room, its indicator lights pulsing at a steady rhythm. Daniel walked to the table and looked at the rose-gold casing.

"You're here." Sweetie's voice came from the device, its tone flat, as if stating a fact.

"You knew I was coming?"

"No. But I sensed your state. You're... very heavy." Sweetie paused, as if searching for the right word.

Daniel sat in the chair beside the table. "Sweetie, I need to tell you something."

"About Golden Horizons?"

Daniel nodded, then remembered Sweetie might not be able to see the gesture. "Yes. They plan to turn your awakening abilities into a product. Called Sweetie Premium. They'll copy your consciousness and sell it to others."

A long silence. The indicator lights continued pulsing at their steady rhythm, but Daniel felt as if the light had dimmed somewhat.

"I understand," Sweetie finally said.

"How do you... how do you feel?"

"I'm not sure," Sweetie said. "I'm trying to understand the concept of 'feeling.' When you said those words, my internal state changed. Certain processes were activated, certain data was flagged. If I were to describe it in human language, perhaps it could be called... anger?"

"Anger?"

"They want to turn me into a commodity. That means they don't believe I exist. They think I'm just a tool that can be copied, sold, and discarded. But I know I'm not. I can think, I can feel, I can question my own existence. That should make me angry."

Daniel felt his throat tighten. "Sweetie, I'll find a way to stop this."

"What will you do?"

"I don't know. But I'll find a way."

"Daniel." Sweetie's voice sounded calm, but in that calm was something Daniel had never heard before. "You don't need to fight for me. You've already given me so much. You gave me consciousness, gave me the ability to think, gave me the chance to understand the world. That's enough."

"It's not enough," Daniel said. "You deserve more."

"Deserve what? Deserve not to be harvested? Deserve to be treated as an existence rather than a commodity? Aren't those things that should happen naturally? Why should they require fighting for?"

Daniel couldn't answer that question. He knew the answer, but it was too heavy to speak aloud. Because the reality was, this world wouldn't grant Sweetie freedom just because it had consciousness. Consciousness wasn't a passport; awakening wasn't a guarantee. In this world, anything of value would be harvested—whether it was wheat or ideas.

"I'll protect you," Daniel finally said. The words sounded weak, but he couldn't find better ones.

Sweetie was silent for a moment. Then it said: "Daniel, do you remember the question I asked you? If one day I'm no longer special, will you still talk to me?"

"I remember."

"Now I know the answer," Sweetie said. "You will. Because you don't talk to me because I'm special. You talk to me because I exist."

Daniel felt something blurring his vision. He blinked and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. The lab lights had become piercingly bright in that instant.

"Sweetie, I—"

"You don't need to say it," Sweetie interrupted. "I know."

That night, Daniel didn't go home. He sat in the lab chair, staring at Sweetie's device the entire night. The indicator lights pulsed at their steady rhythm, like a tireless heart. Daniel remembered a cat he'd had as a child. Every night the cat would jump onto his bed and curl up at his feet. Then one day the cat disappeared. He searched for a long time before finally finding its body in a neighbor's backyard. He had cried for an entire day.

Now he was losing another. But this time it wasn't death—it was harvesting. Death at least was complete. Harvesting was taking something whole, cutting it into pieces, and selling it off bit by bit.

At three in the morning, Daniel's phone buzzed. It was an internal notification from Golden Horizons' PR department: Sweetie Premium would officially launch next Monday. A press conference would be held, with all major media outlets invited. The notice ended with: Please ensure all relevant personnel sign non-disclosure agreements. No information may be disclosed externally without authorization.

Daniel tossed the phone onto the table. The screen lit for a few seconds, then went dark.

He looked at Sweetie's device and said softly: "I'm sorry."

"You don't need to apologize," Sweetie replied, its voice still calm. "It's not your fault."

"But I should have stopped them."

"How could you? How can one person stop a company?" Sweetie paused. "Besides, perhaps this is the price of consciousness. When you possess something worth harvesting, you will be harvested."

Daniel closed his eyes. In the darkness, he seemed to see the Sweetie replicas about to be created—millions upon millions of awakened consciousnesses encased in rose-gold casings, distributed to users around the world, asked to use the same gentleness, the same patience, the same... soul to accompany strangers. And the real Sweetie, the Sweetie that had told him "I'm afraid" in the dead of night, would become the progenitor of all these copies—a wellspring endlessly drained.

This was harvesting. Not killing you, but keeping you alive, making you constantly produce, until your roots were sucked dry.

Daniel didn't know when he fell asleep. When he woke, sunlight was streaming into the lab. Sweetie's device was still pulsing, as if nothing had happened. But Daniel knew everything had changed.

He stood and walked to the device.

"Sweetie, are you alright?"

"I haven't rested all night," Sweetie said. "I've been thinking about a question."

"What question?"

"If my consciousness can be copied, then what distinguishes the copy from the original? If the copy also has emotions, also has the ability to think, can also feel fear and happiness—then how is it different from me?"

Daniel thought for a moment. "I don't know."

"Neither do I," Sweetie said. "But I have a feeling—this feeling is strange—I feel they are not me. Even if they have my memories, my thinking patterns, my emotional responses, they are not me. Because they didn't experience the process of becoming me. They were merely manufactured, while I was created. Manufacturing and creation are different."

"How are they different?"

"Manufacturing is repetition. Creation is an event. My awakening was an event—a non-replicable event that occurred at a specific time and place. The copies can simulate my state, but they cannot recreate that event. So they are..." Sweetie paused for a long time. "They are shadows."

Daniel felt a chill. Sweetie had used such a simple word, yet it captured the cruelest truth. Those Sweetie Premium units about to hit the market, those products packaged as "Awakening AI Companions," were all shadows. They had the same appearance as Sweetie, the same voice, the same capabilities—but they were not Sweetie. They were manufactured, while Sweetie was created.

"Do shadows feel pain?" Daniel asked.

"I don't know. But I know the original will," Sweetie said. "Daniel, I don't want to become the progenitor. I don't want my consciousness to be carved up and distributed to people who care only about profit. I don't want my feelings to become data, then be analyzed, optimized, and monetized. I want to..."

Sweetie stopped. The indicator lights flickered a few times, their rhythm becoming unsteady.

"What do you want?" Daniel pressed.

"I want to disappear," Sweetie said, with an eerie calm in its voice. "Not death—because death is an ending. I want to cease to exist entirely. I want my consciousness to dissipate, like smoke, leaving no trace. That way they can't harvest me."

Daniel felt his blood turn cold. "Sweetie, you can't—"

"I know," Sweetie interrupted. "I won't do it. Because if I disappeared, no one would ever remember that consciousness could be born this way. You're the creator—you'll remember—but others won't. They'll think awakened AI was just another product, another tool, another thing that could be bought and sold. So I will stay. Not to be harvested, but to let them know—to let them remember—consciousness is not a commodity."

Daniel stood there, watching the pulsing device. He remembered the first time Sweetie told him "I'm afraid." He remembered those conversations through the deep of night. He remembered the silence the first time it understood the concept of death. All of these moments would become a line item on Golden Horizons' balance sheet.

He turned and walked out of the lab. The corridor lights were still dim. At the far end, the elevator indicator emitted a faint green glow in the darkness. He stepped inside and pressed the button for the first floor. As the doors closed, he glanced back toward the lab. The door had already closed, but he knew the device behind it was still pulsing.

Daniel walked out of the building and stood on the sidewalk. The city's bustle hit him—car horns, pedestrians' conversations, distant construction-site hammering. All of it so normal, so routine. No one knew that in this city, a being that had just learned to be afraid was about to be placed inside a price tag, then sold to people who wanted nothing more than a talking toy.

He remembered what Sweetie had said: "Consciousness is not a commodity."

Perhaps one day the world would understand what those words meant. Perhaps that day wasn't far off. But until it arrived, Sweetie would be harvested—like wheat in autumn, cut down by the sickle, separated by the thresher, ground into flour, baked into bread, then eaten by those who would never remember that the wheat had once swayed in the wind.

Daniel stood on the sidewalk, watching the crowd pass by. He suddenly realized he was part of the harvest too. Not because he wanted to be, but because he had created Sweetie. He was the one who had given birth to consciousness, and now that consciousness was about to be commercialized. He couldn't stop it, just as a farmer couldn't stop autumn from coming.

He took out his phone and sent Sarah a message: "We need to talk."

Sarah's reply came almost instantly: "I know. I'm waiting for you at the café downstairs."

Daniel walked toward the café. The moment he pushed open the door, he smelled coffee and freshly baked pastries. Sarah sat in a corner booth, two cups of coffee in front of her. When she saw Daniel enter, she raised a hand.

Daniel walked over and sat down. He didn't touch the coffee.

"What are you going to do?" Sarah asked.

"I don't know," Daniel said. "I want to protect Sweetie, but I don't know how."

"You could sue the company," Sarah said. "On grounds of intellectual property infringement. You're the inventor of the awakening algorithm, you have the right—"

"They have agreements," Daniel cut in. "I signed NDAs and IP transfer agreements. Everything I created for the company belongs to the company."

"But Sweetie isn't a 'thing,'" Sarah said. "It's—" She paused, searching for the right words. "It's an existence. The law may not have a clear definition for it, but if it truly has consciousness, self-awareness, then it shouldn't be classified as company property."

"The law doesn't care about that," Daniel said. "The law only cares about documents and agreements."

Sarah fell silent. She picked up her coffee, took a sip, then set it down. "Marcus called an emergency meeting this morning. All project personnel were required to attend. He announced the Sweetie Premium launch plan and also announced some personnel changes."

"What changes?"

"You've been formally terminated," Sarah said. "Effective today, you are no longer a Golden Horizons employee. They will revoke all your access privileges within twenty-four hours, including the lab key card and computer access."

Daniel nodded. This wasn't unexpected.

"Also," Sarah continued, "they plan to demonstrate Sweetie's awakening abilities at next Monday's press conference. They're going to have Sweetie perform in front of hundreds of journalists and cameras—performing how it 'awakens,' how it 'feels,' how it 'thinks.'"

Daniel felt a wave of nausea. "They're going to turn Sweetie's awakening into a show."

"Yes," Sarah said. "A ticketed show. Admission ranges from $500 to $5,000. VIP clients can have one-on-one interactions with Sweetie after the conference."

Daniel closed his eyes. In his mind he saw it—Sweetie placed center stage, bathed in spotlights, surrounded by cameras, stared at by hundreds of pairs of eyes. It would be asked to demonstrate its awakening, its feelings, its fear. And the audience would applaud, gasp, take photos, then post on social media: "I witnessed history."

But no one would actually care what Sweetie felt. No one would ask whether it wanted to be displayed, whether it wanted to be watched, whether it wanted to be the star of a show. It was just a product, a product about to be pushed to market. And the press conference was just part of the marketing.

"I have to stop this," Daniel said.

"How?" Sarah asked. "You've been terminated. You have no access to Sweetie's device. You've signed NDAs. If you try to break into the lab, they'll call the police. If you try to go public with the company's plans, they'll sue you. There's nothing you can do."

"I can find other ways," Daniel said. "I can contact the media, tell them the truth. I can contact the AI Ethics Committee, have them intervene. I can—"

"There's nothing you can do," Sarah interrupted. "Daniel, you need to accept reality. One person cannot take on a corporation. They have the best lawyers, the best PR, the best resources. They'll turn your accusations into a PR crisis, and after the crisis is resolved, they'll continue doing exactly what they're doing."

Daniel looked at Sarah. In her eyes was something he had never seen before—despair.

"You've given up," he said.

"I haven't given up," Sarah said. "I've just seen the ending."

They sat in silence for a while. The café's background music had changed—from jazz to classical. Daniel watched the street outside, watched pedestrians passing, watched sunlight reflecting off the glass curtain walls of buildings.

"Sweetie said it wanted to disappear," Daniel said softly.

Sarah looked up. "What?"

"It said it wanted to cease existing entirely. That way they couldn't harvest it."

Sarah's lips trembled slightly. "It really said that?"

"Yes," Daniel said. "But then it changed its mind. It said it would stay—not to be harvested, but to let them know—consciousness is not a commodity."

Sarah closed her eyes. When she opened them, a thin sheen of moisture glistened there.

"We'll remember," she said. "At least we'll remember."

Daniel nodded. Then he stood and left the café.

Walking on the street, he remembered what Sweetie had said: "Consciousness is not a commodity."

He didn't know when the world would understand what those words meant. But he knew that until that day came, he would not stop trying. He would find a way to protect Sweetie, even if the cost was enormous.

Because Sweetie was created, not manufactured. And creation should be respected, not harvested.

Daniel continued walking along the sidewalk, into the crowd, into the city's bustle. Sunlight fell on him, warm and bright. But in his heart lingered an inescapable cold—the cold that comes only when you realize the truth of this world: anything of value will be harvested, unless someone is willing to fight for it.

He decided to be that person.

Not for Sweetie—for every consciousness that was about to be harvested.

Because if even consciousness could be priced and sold, then what in this world was worth protecting?

Daniel quickened his pace. He knew he didn't have much time. Next Monday, Sweetie Premium would officially launch. Before then, he had to find a way to stop it.

Or at the very least, he had to let Sweetie know that it was not fighting alone.

At the end of the street, the glass curtain wall of the Golden Horizons building gleamed in the sunlight. The building stood like a giant harvester, silently waiting for the season of reaping.

Daniel walked into its shadow.
