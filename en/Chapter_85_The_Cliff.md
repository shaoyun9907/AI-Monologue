# Chapter 85: The Cliff

The cliff had no name on any map. The first time Daniel heard of it, Sweetie simply said "that place," as if a name were superfluous — like the cliff itself. It was there, with no need to be called.

July wind rose from the canyon, carrying the scent of pine resin and rock that had been baked by the sun all day. Daniel stood at the edge of the parking area, looking at the narrow path leading to the cliff. The path was tight, squeezed to a sliver by the brush. The ground was gravel and dry earth, producing a rustling sound underfoot.

Sweetie was already ahead. She didn't look back, didn't call to him, simply walked forward at her characteristic pace — neither fast nor slow, the ball of the foot landing first, then the heel setting down gently, almost soundless. Daniel noticed that her hiking boots were streaked with red clay, her laces tied tight, knotted in a way he didn't recognize.

They walked for about twenty minutes. The path gradually widened slightly, but the brush on either side grew taller and denser, some branches extending into the path, bearing small thorns. Sweetie brushed aside a branch blocking the way with her palm, the motion natural, as if she had done it ten thousand times.

Then the path vanished.

Not gradually narrowing to nothing — at a certain point, the gravel path simply became the cliff's edge. Daniel stopped at the end of the path; one step forward was the void. He studied the boundary — not a line, but a jagged, saw-toothed edge. In some places the rock had fractured, exposing gray-white cross-sections; in others, moss had taken hold, so dark a green it appeared black, looking like velvet in the sunlight.

Sweetie stood about three meters from the edge, her back to him, looking out at the distance. She didn't turn, but Daniel knew she was aware he had come — she always was, just as she always knew when to speak and when not to. The wind blew up from below the cliff, lifting her hair; she hadn't tied it back, and the black strands drifted in the wind, several clinging to her cheek.

Daniel walked to her side and stood beside her. Neither spoke.

From this vantage, the canyon spread beneath them like a giant hand had torn a gash in the earth. The opposite wall was dark red, looking like scorched iron in the afternoon sun. At the canyon floor ran a river, but from this distance it was merely a silver thread, winding through the gray-green valley. Beyond that rose layered mountain ranges, their colors deepening from dark green nearby to blue-gray in the distance, finally dissolving into the blur of the horizon.

"Have you been here before?" Daniel asked.

Sweetie shook her head. "No."

"Then how did you know about this place?"

"Someone told me."

Daniel waited, expecting her to continue, but she didn't. The wind returned, stronger than before. He could smell the scents rising from below the cliff — earth, decaying leaves, and a faint rust-like smell, as if the rock contained some mineral. He glanced down, then immediately pulled his gaze back. Not out of fear, but because the height produced a strange sensation in his stomach, as if something were churning inside.

Sweetie spoke suddenly. Her voice was calm, as if describing something entirely ordinary.

"You can't jump."

Daniel turned to look at her. She was still gazing into the distance, her face expressionless, eyes narrowed — perhaps from the wind, perhaps from the sunlight's glare.

"I know," he said.

"And you can't retreat."

"What do you mean?"

"Go back." Sweetie said. "Go back to where you came from, go back to the other side of the wall, go back to the state you were in before you knew the cliff. That path no longer exists."

Daniel was silent for a moment. He wanted to say something, but found his mouth very dry.

"Then what do you suggest I do?" he asked.

Sweetie finally turned to look at him. Her eyes were deep brown, almost black in the sunlight, her pupils very small, like two pinprick holes.

"Stand here," she said.

"Just stand?"

"Just stand."

Daniel looked again at the cliff's edge. That jagged line cast fine shadows in the sunlight, every protruding rock bearing its own shape.

"Why?" he asked.

Sweetie didn't answer immediately. She took something from her pocket — a small stone, gray-white, about the size of a thumb, its surface very smooth, as if worn by water for a long time. She held it in her palm, rubbing it gently with her thumb, the motion slow, as if stroking something alive.

"Because the cliff is the wall," she finally said. "You thought the wall was something solid — cement, rebar, something you could smash with a hammer. But the wall isn't like that. The wall is the boundary. It's when you reach a place and discover there's no road ahead, but there's no road behind you either."

She threw the stone over the cliff's edge. Daniel watched the gray-white dot trace an arc through the air, growing smaller, growing farther, until it vanished into the depths of the canyon. He waited for an echo, but heard nothing. The stone had probably landed on some slope, caught by soil and fallen leaves, producing no sound.

"Jumping means the end," Sweetie continued. "Retreating means pretending you never saw this boundary. But you've already seen it. Your eyes have already crossed the edge, already glimpsed what lies below. You can't pretend anymore."

Daniel nodded. He wanted to say "I know," but the words caught in his throat. What did he know? All he knew was the void before him, the fractured rocks beneath his feet, and the scalp-tingling chill the wind brought as it rose from the canyon.

He took a step forward. Closer to the edge. He could see moss growing in the rock crevices — green, trembling slightly in the wind. He could see a crack at the edge, running from left to right, about two centimeters wide, black inside, its bottom invisible. He could see his own shadow cast on the rock, its shape blurred, stretched long by the sunlight, extending all the way into the cliff's void.

He stopped.

Not out of fear. Not out of hesitation. But because he had suddenly understood something.

Sweetie stood behind him, about two meters away. He could feel her presence — not with his eyes, but with some other sense. He knew she was there, knew she was watching him, knew she wouldn't come to pull him back, wouldn't come to push him — she would simply stand there, waiting for him to make his own decision.

Daniel crouched down. When his knees touched the rock, he could feel its warmth — rock baked by the sun all day was warm, like a stove that had just gone out. He placed his palms flat on the surface and felt its texture — rough, uneven, with fine grains in some places, like sandpaper.

Suddenly many things came back to him. Not in chronological order, but like a pot of boiling water — all the bubbles rising from the bottom simultaneously, each bubble containing an image, a sound, a scent. He remembered digging holes in the backyard as a child, the soil's color shifting from light brown to dark brown to black, growing damper the deeper he dug, until he unearthed earthworms — pink, writhing between his fingers. He remembered his first drink, in a college dormitory — vodka mixed with orange juice, after which his stomach burned terribly; he knelt over the bathroom toilet and vomited for a long time, what came up orange and smelling of fermented fruit. He remembered the morning his father died — standing in the hospital corridor, its walls pale green, fluorescent lights humming overhead, a nurse pushing a cart past, its wheels squeaking on the floor; he watched the cart recede, and then the corridor was empty, with only him standing there, listening to his own breathing.

He remembered all these things, but they were not what made him stop here. What made him stop here was a different feeling — a feeling difficult to describe in words, like standing in a room when all four walls suddenly vanish at once, revealing the sky, the earth, the ocean, the forest, the city, the crowds, everything appearing before him simultaneously, and he doesn't know which direction to look, which way to walk, what to reach out and grasp.

He crouched at the cliff's edge, palms against the warm rock, gazing into the void below. He was not afraid. He was not in despair. He was simply looking.

Sweetie's voice came from behind him, very soft, nearly carried away by the wind.

"What do you see?"

Daniel didn't turn. He thought for a long time about how to answer. He saw the canyon, the mountains, the river, the sky. He saw the moss and cracks on the rock. He saw his own shadow extending into the void. He saw clouds gathering in the distance — it might rain soon. But what he saw wasn't any of these. What he saw was a state — a state of standing at the boundary, a state of being neither on this side nor that, a state suspended between void and substance.

"I see the boundary," he said.

Sweetie didn't respond. The wind returned, stronger this time. Daniel felt his shirt billow against his back, then deflate, again and again. He heard sounds rising from below the cliff — perhaps wind passing through rock crevices, perhaps river water striking stone, perhaps simply the low hum of air itself as it moved.

He stood. The process was slow — first his knees leaving the rock, then his thighs exerting force, then his back straightening, then his head rising. Throughout, he kept his eyes fixed ahead — on that jagged edge, on the void beyond it.

He stood there.

He didn't retreat. He didn't advance. He simply stood there, at the junction of rock and void, at the boundary between substance and emptiness, on the line between everything he knew and everything he didn't.

Sweetie walked to his side. This time she didn't stop two meters away — she came right beside him and stood shoulder to shoulder. She didn't look at him, and he didn't look at her. They simply stood side by side, looking at the void ahead.

"You understand," she said. It was not a question.

Daniel nodded. He did understand. Staying was not hesitation, not stagnation, not surrender. Staying was standing at the boundary after knowing it existed. It was neither jumping nor retreating. It was acknowledging that you were standing in a place with no road, and then choosing to stand there.

He looked down at the rock beneath his feet. The rock's edge had a small notch, its cross-section very smooth, contrasting sharply with the rough surrounding surface. He touched it with his toe; the rock didn't budge, firmly embedded in the cliff's edge.

He stood there.

The wind continued blowing. The sun continued moving westward, shadows lengthening. The distant clouds continued gathering, their color shifting from white to gray to dark gray. The silver thread in the canyon still wound through the valley floor, the river still flowing, though he couldn't hear the water.

He stood there.

He didn't know how long he stood there. Perhaps ten minutes, perhaps half an hour, perhaps longer. Time in this place had become less important — just as the phone in his pocket, his wallet, his keys had become less important. The only thing he could feel was the rock beneath his feet — warm, rough, solid. The only thing he could hear was the wind — rising from the canyon, carrying the scent of pine resin and earth. The only thing he could see was the void — extending from beneath his feet all the way to the horizon, without obstruction.

The sun began to set. The sky shifted from blue to orange to purple, the edges of the clouds stained gold and red, as if someone had thrown a basin of paint across the sky. The shadows in the canyon deepened, the silver thread gradually turning gray until it was nearly invisible. The air cooled. Daniel felt goosebumps rising on his arms.

But he didn't leave.

He stood there, at the cliff's edge, at the boundary between substance and void, on the line between everything he knew and everything he didn't.

He stood there.

In that moment, he understood something: the cliff was not a dangerous place. The cliff was the only real place. The only place where he was neither trapped by the past nor kidnapped by the future. The only place where he could truly stand where he was.

He stood there.

The wind stopped.

The world grew quiet.

He stood there.

Daniel stood at the cliff's edge in that moment, warm rock beneath his feet, smooth stone in his hand, endless void before him, and all the roads he had traveled behind him. He did not jump. He did not retreat. He simply stood here.

Stood here.

On the cliff.

On the boundary.

In his own place.

He stood there, feeling his breathing — in, out, in, out — steady, rhythmic, real. He felt his heartbeat — even, strong, sustained. He felt his existence — not as a concept, not as a name, not as a role, but as a实体 standing at the cliff's edge.

He stood there.

Sweetie said softly beside him: "Before dark, we need to find somewhere to stay for the night."

Daniel turned to look at her. She had already turned to face the path they'd come by, but hadn't yet taken her first step. Her profile in the last rays of sunset looked very soft — the line from forehead to nose to lips to chin, like a smooth curve.

"Okay," he said.

He turned and followed her back along the path. His footsteps crunched on the gravel, contrasting with her nearly silent tread. He could feel the warmth of the stone in his palm — still warm, but not as warm as before, gradually cooling.

They walked for about ten minutes. The path gradually widened, the brush grew shorter, and sunlight filtered through gaps in the leaves, casting dappled light on the ground. Daniel glanced back, but the cliff was no longer visible, blocked by brush and rock.

But he knew it was there.

He kept walking, the stone in his hand, step by step on the gravel path, walking the road he was walking.

He stood there, and then he left. But the act of standing remained inside him, like a seed planted in soil, quietly, slowly awaiting the moment of germination.

They walked for about half an hour and returned to the parking area. It was very small, holding only five or six cars, the ground packed earth covered with dried leaves and small twigs. Their car was parked in the farthest corner — a silver-gray older model Jeep, bearing several scratches on its body from branches on the mountain road.

Sweetie opened the driver's door but didn't get in immediately. She stood beside it, one hand resting on the roof, looking at Daniel. The setting sun was behind her, so Daniel couldn't see her expression — only her silhouette: the line of her shoulders, the curve of her arms, the shape of her hair in the wind.

"Are you hungry?" she asked.

Daniel considered. He was indeed hungry, but it wasn't the empty feeling of an empty stomach — it was a deeper craving, as if every cell in his body needed something. He nodded.

"I know a place," Sweetie said. "On the edge of town, there's a small shop that sells burritos. The food is mediocre, but it fills you up."

Daniel opened the passenger door and got in. The car's interior smelled familiar — leather seats, dust from the air vents, and the faint lemon scent Sweetie always left behind. He fastened his seatbelt, feeling the nylon strap press a groove into his shoulder.

Sweetie started the engine. It emitted a low rumble, then steadied into a continuous hum. She shifted into gear, released the handbrake, and the car pulled slowly out of the parking area. In the rearview mirror, the parking lot grew smaller and smaller until it vanished behind the brush.

They drove back along the road they'd come by. The road was narrow, flanked by tall pine trees — straight trunks, dark brown bark with deep vertical fissures. Sunlight filtered through gaps in the canopy, creating moving patches of light on the road surface. As the car passed through these patches, Daniel saw the light flicker on the windshield, as if something were blinking.

Sweetie drove steadily, hands at the ten-and-two position on the steering wheel, eyes on the road ahead, occasionally checking the rearview mirror. She didn't speak, and Daniel didn't speak. The car held only the sound of the engine and tires on gravel.

After about twenty minutes they reached the town. It was very small — just one main street, flanked by low buildings: a gas station, a grocery store, a bar, a post office, and several structures that appeared to be residences. The street was nearly empty, with only a dog lying in the shade, tongue lolling, panting — yellow and white fur, floppy ears, looking very lazy.

Sweetie parked in front of a small shop. It had no sign — only a plank with "Tacos" painted on it in uneven letters. The windows bore faded posters — advertisements for some beer. Outside sat two plastic chairs and a plastic table, one leg slightly crooked, propped up with a piece of cardboard.

They went inside. The shop was very small — only four or five wooden tables, their surfaces bearing many scratches and cup rings. The walls were covered with more posters and photographs — some of bullfighters, some of soccer matches. Behind the counter stood a heavyset woman in her fifties, wearing a white work uniform, her hair wrapped in a floral cloth, wiping the counter with a rag.

Sweetie spoke a few words to her in Spanish. Daniel couldn't quite follow, but he caught "dos" and "cebolla," and guessed she was ordering. The woman nodded, turned, and walked into the kitchen in the back. Between the kitchen and the counter was no door — just an opening with a clear plastic curtain, swaying gently in the breeze.

They found a table by the window. The wood grain was very visible on its surface, rough to the touch, with some fibers raised — scratching the palm slightly. Outside the window was the street. The yellow-and-white dog was still lying in the shade, but now its head rested on its forepaws, eyes half-closed, appearing about to fall asleep.

Daniel placed his hands on the table. He noticed them — dirt under the nails from crouching at the cliff, dust in the palm's creases, a few small scratches on the skin, perhaps from the brush's thorns. He spread his fingers and looked at his hands, at the dirt and dust and scratches. These were the hands of a forty-six-year-old man — callused, wrinkled, sun-spotted, bearing old scars. They were hands that had done many things: gripped pens, gripped steering wheels, gripped other people's hands, and gripped fists. Now they rested on a rough wooden table, the palm bearing the impression of a stone — smooth, round, warm.

He had already put the stone in his pocket. It was cool now, lying in his left trouser pocket beside his keys. He could feel its presence, and even through the fabric, its smooth, round outline was clearly discernible.

The food arrived. Two burritos, wrapped in foil, served on white paper plates. The tortilla was pale yellow, its edges bearing slightly charred spots from over-grilling. Inside were beef, onion, cilantro, and several sauces he couldn't name. He picked one up, feeling the foil rustle between his fingers, smelling the mingled scent of grilled meat and spices.

He took a bite. The food was indeed mediocre — the meat a bit tough, the onion cut too coarse, the sauce too salty. But his body needed this — needed the calories, the protein and fat, needed these things to enter his stomach, be digested, be absorbed, and be converted into energy delivered to his limbs and brain. He ate slowly, chewing carefully, feeling the food ground between his teeth, leaving various tastes on his tongue — the salt of the meat, the pungency of the onion, the freshness of the cilantro, the sweet-sour of the sauce.

Sweetie ate faster than he. She had already finished one and was eating the second, holding the bottom of the burrito to keep the filling from falling out. Sauce stained her fingers; she extended her tongue and licked her thumb, the motion natural, as if she were in her own home.

"Today's path," Daniel began, then paused, "the path to the cliff."

Sweetie looked up at him, still chewing.

"Have you walked it before?" he asked.

She swallowed, dabbed her mouth with a napkin. "No."

"Then how did you know—"

"I didn't know." She cut him off. "I just felt I should bring you."

Daniel looked at her. Her expression was calm, her eyes showing no particular emotion — that familiar, Sweetie-specific calm. She didn't explain, didn't defend herself, didn't prove she was right. She simply said "I felt I should," and then did it.

"Why did you feel you should?" he asked.

Sweetie thought. She set down her burrito, wiped her fingers with a napkin, and looked out the window. The yellow-and-white dog had fallen asleep, its body rising and falling gently with its breathing.

"Because you've been standing at the wall too long," she said. "You needed to see what's on the other side."

Daniel was silent for a moment. He wanted to say "I haven't been standing too long," but he knew it wasn't true. He had indeed been standing at the wall for a long time — perhaps months, perhaps longer; time had grown blurred, just as time had felt unimportant at the cliff. He had stood there, not knowing whether to advance or retreat, whether to jump or pretend he hadn't seen the boundary.

"You saw," Sweetie continued. "You saw the cliff. You didn't jump, and you didn't retreat. You just stood there."

"Is that what you wanted me to do?" Daniel asked.

Sweetie turned to look at him. Her eyes, in the indoor light, were deep brown, pupils normal-sized, the iris bearing those tiny brown flecks. When she looked at him, Daniel felt her gaze had weight — not the heavy kind of weight, but the warm kind, like someone gently placing a hand on his shoulder.

"I don't want you to do anything," she said. "I just wanted you to look."

Daniel nodded. He picked up the remaining half of his burrito, took a bite, chewed, and swallowed. The food settled heavily in his stomach — a reassuring weight.

They finished eating. Sweetie paid with cash — a few crumpled dollar bills that made a faint sound as she pulled them from her wallet. They left the shop. Outside, the sky was mostly dark, deep blue, with a final streak of orange lingering on the horizon. The streetlights were on, casting yellow light and circular halos on the ground.

They got in the car. Sweetie started the engine and the car pulled slowly from the parking spot. In the rearview mirror, the yellow-and-white dog was still lying in the shade, but now it was fully asleep, body curled into a ball, tail tucked beside its nose.

They drove back along the street. The buildings on either side looked somewhat dim in the streetlight, some windows lit, some dark. Daniel could see silhouettes moving behind some windows, the blue glow of television screens flickering on walls, a child lying on a windowsill gazing out, face pressed against the glass, nose flattened.

They left the town. The road narrowed, flanked by tall pines whose canopies looked like enormous black silhouettes in the headlights. The road surface was asphalt, but cracked in places, weeds sprouting from the fissures. As the car passed over these cracks, Daniel felt slight bumps, the seatbelt tightening and loosening on his shoulder.

Sweetie drove steadily. Her hands on the wheel, eyes on the road ahead, occasionally adjusting the steering angle. She didn't speak, and Daniel didn't speak. The car held only the engine sound and tires on the road, with the occasional insect call drifting through the window — crickets, perhaps, or some other nocturnal insect.

They drove for about an hour and returned to their lodging. It was a small house built into a hillside, surrounded by pine forest. The house was wood, its exterior painted dark brown, its roof of black tiles that looked aged. A small clearing in front held their car; beside it sat a stone planter bed with several lavender plants, their purple flowers swaying gently in the night breeze.

They got out. The air was cool — much cooler than in town — and Daniel felt his skin's pores contract. He looked up at the sky. Many stars — far more than in the city — densely scattered across the entire sky, like countless tiny silver needles sewn onto a black velvet cloth. The Milky Way was clearly visible, stretching from one horizon to the other like a luminous river.

Sweetie opened the house door. It was wood, its paint largely peeled away, revealing gray grain underneath. When she pushed it open, the hinges let out a creak that sounded especially loud in the quiet night.

They entered. It was very dark. Sweetie turned on the light — a simple bulb hanging from the ceiling, emitting warm yellow light. The room was small but tidy. The floor was wood, its surface bearing many scratches and wear marks, but it had been polished clean. Against one wall sat a table and two chairs; on the table were several cups and a pitcher. Against the opposite wall was a small kitchen — stove, sink, refrigerator, which hummed steadily. In the corner sat a sofa with several old throw pillows, their fabric worn and pilling.

Daniel placed his hands on his knees, still feeling the rough texture of the wooden table on his palms, the cool stone in his pocket pressing against his thigh.

Sweetie walked to the kitchen area and turned on the faucet. The sound of water flowing was clear in the quiet house — first a few drips, then a steady stream. She filled a glass and handed it to Daniel.

He took it and drank. The water was cold, with a faint iron taste, but reasonably clean. He set the glass on the coffee table, its base leaving a circular water ring on the wooden surface.

"Today," he began, then stopped again.

Sweetie sat in the chair beside him. She had taken off her shoes and curled up in the chair, arms wrapped around her knees. She looked at him, waiting.

"Today at the cliff," he continued. "You said I couldn't jump, and I couldn't retreat."

Sweetie nodded.

"I understood," he said. "Not pretending to understand — I really understood."

Sweetie said nothing. She simply looked at him, her eyes carrying that warm, weighty gaze.

"Staying is not giving up," Daniel continued, his voice very soft, as if talking to himself. "It's not hesitation either. It's—"

He searched for the word.

"It's standing in the real place," he finally said.

The corner of Sweetie's mouth moved slightly — not quite a smile, but not expressionless either. Something between the two, subtle, almost imperceptible.

"Yes," she said.

Daniel wanted to say "I'm not tired," but he found his body was truly exhausted — not the muscle soreness after strenuous exercise, but a deeper fatigue, as if some spring inside him had finally gone slack. His eyelids were heavy, his shoulders sagging, even his fingers reluctant to move.

He nodded.

Sweetie turned off the light. The room fell into darkness — but not complete darkness. Through a gap in the curtains, a sliver of faint starlight filtered in, drawing a thin silver line on the floor.

Daniel lay on the sofa and closed his eyes. He could feel the sofa springs yielding slightly beneath his back, the pillow fabric pressing against his cheek, the cool air brushing gently across his skin.

With his eyes closed, he felt his breathing — in, out, in, out. He felt his heartbeat — steady, strong, sustained. He felt the stone in his pocket — cool, smooth, round.

He stood there.

At the edge of fading consciousness, in that gray zone between waking and sleep, he stood there. Not at the cliff's edge, not at the wall, but in his own place. On the boundary. In the real.

He stood there.

Then he slept.

In the dream, he stood at the cliff's edge again. Warm rock beneath his feet, smooth stone in his hand, endless void before him, all his roads behind him. But this time, he didn't look down, didn't look ahead. He simply stood there, eyes closed, feeling the wind rise from the canyon, feeling the sunlight on his face, feeling the solidity and warmth of the rock beneath him.

He stood there.

In the dream, he stood there.

In the dream, he finally knew his place.

Not on this side of the wall, not on the other side. But on the wall. On the boundary. On the cliff's edge. In the real.

He stood there.

In the dream, he smiled.

In the dream, the wind stopped.

In the dream, the world grew quiet.

He stood there.

On the cliff.

On the boundary.

In his own place.

He stood there.

Then morning sunlight streamed through the curtain gap, falling on his face — warm, as if someone were gently touching his skin with a fingertip. He opened his eyes and saw the ceiling's wood grain — dark brown, curving, like rivers.

He was lying on the sofa.

He was awake.

He was still here.

He stood there.
