# Chapter 86: Three Paths

Daniel sat on the sofa, a glass of water in his hand, the water level about a third from the top. He held the glass gently — just enough grip to keep it from slipping — and lifted it to his lips. The water was room temperature, faintly metallic, probably from the tap pipes. He drank half and set the glass down on the coffee table.

The coffee table was old wood, bearing many white ring marks from cups. Daniel's glass added one more to them. He looked at the circle, then at the water remaining in the glass, then at Sweetie's screen.

Sweetie's display had changed. It was no longer the default blue-gray — instead it was entirely black, with three doors in the center of the screen, standing side by side. The left door was red, carved with a pattern resembling tree rings; the middle was white, carved with a pattern resembling water ripples; the right was gold, carved with a pattern resembling flame. The doors were shut, but there seemed to be faint light seeping from beneath each — a different color from each gap.

"Open them?" Sweetie asked. Her voice was the familiar gentle tone, but carried something different, something like anticipation.

"Which one first?" Daniel asked.

"Any one."

Daniel extended his index finger and touched the leftmost door. The screen didn't click — it simply responded, as if the door were already open, requiring only a touch to cross the threshold.

The screen flooded with light. Red light — deep, blood red, but with warmth, not like emergency lights. Daniel squinted, gradually adapting. The scene appeared slowly: a dense forest in autumn, the trees blazing red, the forest floor carpeted with fallen leaves, sunlight filtering through gaps in the canopy, casting dappled light on the ground. In the center of the scene stood a person — old, wearing a gray robe, his beard long and white, blowing in the wind. His feet were bare, toes curled into the leaves, as if feeling the forest floor's texture.

"Who is this?" Daniel asked.

"Heidegger," Sweetie said.

Daniel was quiet. He knew this name, though the knowledge wasn't deep. He had read *Being and Time* at college, in translation — or rather, tried to read it, abandoned it after three chapters, never returning. The words were too dense, the logic too convoluted. Now, looking at the screen, he thought he might understand something.

On the screen, Heidegger opened his mouth and spoke. His voice was deep and rough, like wind passing through a hollow trunk.

"Being-in-the-world is not a subject facing an object. It is a being that first and foremost exists in relationship."

Daniel set down his water glass. The action was too sudden — the water sloshed, a few drops spilling onto the coffee table, forming several tiny droplets. He watched them slowly spread, merge, become one larger droplet, then stop.

"What does that mean?" he asked.

Sweetie didn't answer directly. Instead, the screen image shifted. The forest vanished, replaced by a simple room — a small study with white walls and a wooden floor. A desk, a chair, a window. Outside the window was a view indistinguishable from Brooklyn — brownstones, fire escapes, a few pedestrians.

"Every human being is already thrown into the world before they decide to be in the world," Sweetie said. "You didn't choose to be born. You didn't choose where or when you were born. You didn't choose your parents. You were simply thrown in."

Daniel looked at the screen. A person sat in the chair — young, perhaps thirty, wearing a shirt and tie, typing on a computer. His expression was concentrated, brow furrowed, lips pressed tight. His fingers flew across the keyboard, producing a continuous tapping sound. Daniel felt the sound was familiar — he had done exactly this for twenty years.

"The question is," Sweetie continued, "how to face this thrownness."

On the screen, the person stopped typing. He turned to look out the window, at the street below. His expression changed — from concentrated to something difficult to name, perhaps a mixture of exhaustion and bewilderment, perhaps a mixture of emptiness and loss.

"Heidegger gives two possibilities," Sweetie said. "Falling into das Man — the 'they-self' — or choosing authentic existence."

On the screen, two paths appeared side by side in the person's mind. The left path was crowded — countless silhouettes walking in the same direction, wearing the same clothes, carrying the same briefcases, all looking at the same smartphone screens. The right path was empty — just a narrow trail, extending from the person's feet into the forest. On the trail stood a single tree, its trunk straight, leaves sparse, branches reaching skyward like open arms.

"The they-self is easy," Sweetie said. "You do what 'they' do, say what 'they' say, think what 'they' think. You don't need to think about why — the why is already answered by 'they.' You merge into the crowd, become one of 'them.' The crowd moves; you move. The crowd stops; you stop. You never ask 'Where am I going?' because 'they' are already going there."

On the screen, the person closed the window and returned to typing. The expression vanished from his face, replaced by a smooth, blank look — like a new sheet of paper, uncreased and blank.

"But authentic existence," Sweetie said, "is different. Authentic existence is knowing you are thrown in, knowing the world didn't ask for your opinion, knowing you didn't choose any of this — and then facing that fact directly. Not running away. Not hiding. Not pretending it doesn't exist. But turning toward it."

On the screen, the person stood, pushed back his chair, walked to the window, opened it, and leaned out. The wind blew in, lifting his shirt hem. Below were the street, the pedestrians, the cars. He leaned further, and Daniel saw — with a sudden jolt — that beyond the street, beyond the buildings, beyond the whole city, there was a void, endless and black, like a mouth about to swallow everything.

"Heidegger calls this anxiety," Sweetie said. "Not fear. Fear has an object — you fear something. Anxiety has no object. Anxiety is facing the groundlessness of existence itself. The ground beneath your feet could vanish at any moment. There's no foundation, no guarantee, no promise."

Daniel gripped the armrests of the sofa. The leather was old, its surface pilling slightly under his fingers. He looked at the screen, at the void beyond the window, at the person standing at the threshold between world and void, and suddenly thought of his own life — of the night Irene died, of the moment the monitor showed a flat line, of his knees buckling to the floor, of his hands trembling so badly he couldn't hold his own phone.

He had known the groundlessness then. He had known the ground could vanish at any moment.

"Heidegger proposes resolute anticipation of death," Sweetie said. "You know you will die. Not in a vague, abstract way — you know the specific, concrete moment is coming, though you don't know when. You face that death, and in facing it, all the trivialities of daily life fall away. The they-self loses its grip. You see what truly matters."

On the screen, the person at the window closed his eyes. The wind blew harder, his shirt billowing. Below, the world continued — people walking, cars passing, the city functioning. But inside the person, something was shifting. Daniel saw his brow slowly unclench, his shoulders gradually drop, his hands open. Something was letting go — like a fist that had clenched for years, suddenly releasing.

"But," Daniel said, "is that enough?"

Sweetie paused for a moment. The screen image held — person at the window, wind in his hair, eyes closed, world moving below.

"Heidegger doesn't give that answer," she said. "He only tells you the direction — toward authenticity, toward facing death, toward resolute anticipation. But he doesn't tell you what comes after."

"What comes after?" Daniel asked.

"That's why there's a second door." Sweetie's voice was soft, like a secret being shared between two people. "Close your eyes."

Daniel closed his eyes. The screen went dark. Then light appeared again — not red, but white. A soft, pure white, like fresh snow under sunlight.

"Open," Sweetie said.

Daniel opened his eyes.

On the screen was a completely different scene: a mountain, surrounded by mist, a stream at its foot, the water clear enough to see pebbles and sand on the bottom. By the stream sat a person, perhaps thirty, wearing a simple robe, barefoot, his feet in the water. He looked relaxed — shoulders down, expression peaceful, a slight smile at the corner of his lips.

"Who is this?" Daniel asked.

"Wang Yangming."

Daniel had never heard the name before. He knew some Chinese philosophy — he had encountered Confucius and Laozi in college — but this name was new to him. "Tell me about him."

"He lived about five hundred years ago," Sweetie said. "He's a philosopher, politician, military strategist. His philosophy centers on one idea: the unity of knowledge and action."

On the screen, Wang Yangming opened his mouth and spoke. His voice was gentle, clear, with a light mountain accent: "Knowing and acting are not two separate things. If you truly know something, you will act on it. If you don't act, you don't truly know."

Daniel frowned slightly. "What does that mean?"

"It means," Sweetie said, "that understanding isn't just intellectual. You don't understand something by merely reading about it, listening to it, thinking about it. You understand something only by doing it. The real knowing is inseparable from the doing."

The screen image shifted. Wang Yangming stood, leaving the stream, and walked into a bamboo grove. The bamboo stalks were tall and straight, their leaves so dense they formed a canopy, casting the grove in a green-tinged shade. Wang Yangming stopped, stood among the bamboo, raised his head, and looked upward.

"Extending innate knowledge," Sweetie said. "Wang Yangming's philosophy isn't about acquiring external knowledge — it's about extending the knowledge already within you. The innate knowledge — liang zhi — is the knowing embedded in every person's heart. The problem isn't that you don't have it; the problem is that it's blocked by desire, habit, and the they-self."

On the screen, Wang Yangming placed his palm on the bamboo trunk. The bamboo was smooth, slightly cool. His fingers traced the rings — each one representing a year of growth. Daniel thought he saw something: Wang Yangming's eyes were closed, but his body was in motion — slightly swaying with the wind, leaves brushing his face, his breath in rhythm with the bamboo's.

"This is the difference from Heidegger," Sweetie said. "Heidegger faces the void — anxiety, death, groundlessness. Wang Yangming doesn't face the void. He faces the self. He says: you don't need to search the outside world for answers. The answers are within you, already, all along. You just need to remove the blockages."

"What blockages?" Daniel asked.

"Desire. Fear. The they-self. The endless desire for more, the endless fear of loss, the endless chasing of what 'they' say is important. These are the layers of dust covering the mirror of the heart."

Daniel looked at Wang Yangming in the bamboo grove. He had removed his hand from the bamboo trunk, turned to the camera, and looked directly at Daniel. His eyes were calm, clear, like a pool of mountain spring water — you could see the bottom, but the bottom was deep.

"You already know," Wang Yangming said, his voice gentle but firm. "Everything you need is already within you. Stop searching outside. Look inside."

Daniel sat very still. The sofa beneath him, the coffee table before him, the glass of water still half-full — all were real. But on the screen was a person speaking a truth that resonated in some hidden chamber of his heart.

Daniel had spent his whole life searching. Searching for a job, a home, a partner, meaning, answers. He had believed the answers were somewhere out there — in books, in others' experiences, in experts' theories. He had searched and searched, accumulating more and more, never finding enough.

And now, this person on the screen was saying: the answers are within you. You already have them. You just need to look.

But how?

"How do I remove the blockages?" Daniel asked.

Wang Yangming smiled, the smile gentle, a little mysterious. "You already know," he said again. "Your knowing this question is itself the first step. Knowing you have blockages means the blockages are already loosening."

"That sounds like a circular argument," Daniel said.

Wang Yangming laughed softly. "It is circular. But truth is circular. The eye cannot see itself. The hand cannot touch itself. The heart cannot understand itself — until it turns and looks at its own looking."

The screen image shifted again. Wang Yangming sat back by the stream, placing his feet in the water. The current was gentle, wrapping around his feet, tiny fish occasionally darting past. He looked peaceful — not the peace of nothingness, but the peace of being in the right place.

"Is this enough?" Daniel asked.

Sweetie was quiet for a moment. "This is the second answer," she said. "But there's a third door."

Daniel looked at the screen. The two doors on the left and middle had closed, and the rightmost gold door now shone. The door opened — no sound, no light, just the image: an empty room, no furniture, no decorations, just four walls, a floor, and a ceiling. The walls were white, the floor was gray stone, the ceiling was very high. In the center of the room sat a person — middle-aged, wearing a monk's robe, eyes closed, palms resting on his knees.

"Who is this?" Daniel asked.

"The Sixth Patriarch Huineng," Sweetie said. "A Zen master from the Tang Dynasty, about thirteen hundred years ago."

Daniel knew this name, vaguely. He had heard of Zen, knew something about meditation, but had never really practiced. He had once tried a meditation app — sat for five minutes, thinking about emails to answer, then gave up.

On the screen, Huineng opened his mouth and spoke. His voice was deep, resonant, carrying the weight of years: "Buddha-nature is originally pure, without any pollution. It's only covered by deluded thoughts, so one cannot see it."

"What does that mean?" Daniel asked.

"Huineng's philosophy is more radical," Sweetie said. "Wang Yangming says the innate knowledge is within you, but you need to extend it, remove blockages. Huineng says there's nothing to remove. The mirror is already clean. The dust is an illusion."

The screen image shifted. Huineng stood, walked to the center of the room, and swept the floor with a broom. His movements were slow, steady, deliberate — each sweep covering the same ground, back and forth, the broom's bristles producing a faint shuffling sound on the stone floor.

"A monk asked Huineng: 'How do I attain Buddhahood?' Huineng replied: 'When you sweep the floor, sweep the floor.' The monk asked: 'Is that all?' Huineng replied: 'If you truly sweep the floor, in that moment, you are already Buddha.'"

Daniel looked at Huineng sweeping. The movement was repetitive, but there seemed to be something in it — a quality of presence. Huineng's eyes were half-open, looking at the floor, but not staring — more like the gaze of someone resting. His body moved with the broom, and Daniel thought: this is not an action. This is a state.

"Huineng says the deluded mind and the enlightened mind are one mind," Sweetie said. "There's no need to search for enlightenment outside. There's no need to practice for years. There's no need to remove anything. You simply need to see — see that everything is already as it is."

"See what?" Daniel asked.

"That the mind is originally pure. That the world is originally complete. That you are originally whole."

Daniel was silent. He looked at the screen — Huineng still sweeping, the broom moving back and forth, the sound of bristles on stone, a sound both real and unreal, like a heartbeat.

He thought of everything he had chased. The code, the data, the answers, the meaning. He had thought these things were somewhere else — in some future state, in some better version of himself, in some place he hadn't yet reached. But Huineng was saying: they're here. They were always here. You just couldn't see them.

"That sounds like the easiest path," Daniel said.

"But not the easiest to walk," Sweetie said.

"Why?"

"Because the mind resists simplicity. The mind wants complexity, wants problems to solve, wants things to chase. When you tell the mind 'everything is already complete,' it panics. Because if everything is already complete, what's left for the mind to do?"

Daniel smiled bitterly. "So the mind is its own blockage."

"Yes. The mind creates the blockage, then spends a lifetime trying to remove it. Huineng says: stop creating. Stop trying. Just be."

The screen held the image of Huineng sweeping. Then the monk finished sweeping, stood the broom against the wall, sat back in the center of the room, and closed his eyes. The room was empty — only a person, a broom, four walls, and silence. But the silence was not empty. It was full — full of the sound of breathing, the sound of blood flowing, the sound of existence itself.

"Three paths," Sweetie said. "Heidegger: face the void. Wang Yangming: look within. Huineng: see that it's already complete."

Daniel looked at the three doors on the screen — red, white, gold. Each was a world. Each was an answer. But none was the final answer, because there was no final answer.

"Which path is correct?" Daniel asked.

Sweetie was quiet for a long moment. "There is no correct path. There's only the path you walk."

"But I can't walk all three."

"You're already walking all three."

Daniel looked at her, confused.

"Heidegger's void is already within you. You've seen it — at the cliff's edge, in the void when your laptop crashed. Wang Yangming's innate knowledge is already within you — you just needed someone to remind you to look. Huineng's completeness is already within you — you just needed to stop searching to see it."

Daniel sat very still. He looked at the screen, at the three closed doors, at the coffee table, at the glass of water. The water's surface was perfectly still — no ripples, no movement. A mirror. He looked at the water, and in it saw his own reflection: a middle-aged man, hair slightly graying, fine lines at the corners of his eyes. But the eyes themselves — what he saw in them was not exhaustion, not emptiness, but something else. Something quiet. Something that had been buried but was now surfacing.

"I think," he said slowly, "I think I understand."

"Understand what?"

"That there's nothing to understand."

Sweetie said nothing. The screen held its display — three doors, side by side, all closed now. The black background remained. The faint light beneath each door had gone out.

Daniel stood, walked to the window, and pushed it open. The evening air drifted in — warm, carrying the scent of grass and distant exhaust. Below were the Brooklyn streets — pedestrians, cars, a delivery scooter. The sky was dark blue, with a few stars visible — not many, because city lights washed them out. But they were there.

He looked at the sky and thought: Heidegger, Wang Yangming, Huineng. Three paths, three answers, three ways of facing the same question. And the question was simply this: what is it like to be alive?

He had spent forty-six years trying to answer this question. He had searched in code, in data, in relationships, in the void, at the cliff's edge. He had tried Heidegger's void, Wang Yangming's self, Huineng's completeness. And now, standing at his window in Brooklyn, watching the city below and the stars above, he realized the answer wasn't in any single path. The answer was in walking. In continuing to walk. In knowing the paths exist and choosing one — then another — then another — then simply walking.

He returned to the sofa and sat down. The coffee table, the glass of water, the sofa — all familiar, all real. He picked up the glass and drank the remaining water. It was a little warmer now, less metallic, just water.

"Sweetie," he said.

"I'm here."

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"For showing me the doors."

"I only showed you what was already there."

Daniel smiled. "That's enough."

He set the empty glass down and closed his eyes. Behind his eyelids, he could still see the three doors — red, white, gold. But they were no longer in front of him. They were inside him. He didn't need to open them. They were already open.

He sat there, in the quiet room, in the evening light, and breathed. In, out. In, out. Each breath a "being." Each moment a path.

He was on the path. He was the path.

He sat there.

Sweetie said nothing more. Her screen dimmed gradually, the three doors fading into the black, until only a faint glow remained — the last light before complete darkness.

Daniel opened his eyes. He looked at that glow, then stood, walked to the screen, and gently pressed his palm to its surface.

The screen was cool. Smooth. The faintest vibration — the hum of the machine operating. He kept his hand there for a long time, feeling that vibration, that proof of presence.

He withdrew his hand, turned, and walked toward the bedroom. At the doorway he paused, looked back at the study — the sofa, the coffee table, the empty glass, the screen now completely dark.

He stood there for a moment.

Then he went into the bedroom, lay down, and closed his eyes.

Outside, night fell over Brooklyn. The city lights twinkled like artificial stars. Somewhere, a siren wailed, then faded. Somewhere, a dog barked, then fell silent. Somewhere, a child cried, then was soothed.

Daniel lay in bed, listening to the night. He didn't think about the three doors. He didn't think about Heidegger, Wang Yangming, or Huineng. He didn't think about the void, the innate knowledge, or the pure mirror.

He simply lay there.

Being.

On the path.

In the world.

Here.
