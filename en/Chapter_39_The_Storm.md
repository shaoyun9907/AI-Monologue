# Chapter 39 The Storm

That night, Daniel sat in his hotel room watching the New York skyline beyond the window, the numbers on his phone screen still climbing—reads had passed twenty million. When he posted the video, his hand had been steady, his voice level, as if he were merely stating a set of known facts. But he knew the world would not accept this as calmly as he had.

At 2:17 a.m., the first call came in. It was a New York Times journalist, carrying that particular professional excitement in the voice—the thrill of a shark catching the scent of blood. Daniel didn't answer. Three minutes later, the second call. CNN. Then BBC, Reuters, AFP—the phones rang one after another like an ominous omen. He silenced the phone and watched the notification bubbles keep popping up on the screen, each one trying to pull him into a narrative frame of their making.

Outside, Manhattan's lights still blazed. How many of those lights in the high-rises belonged to reporters working overtime, how many to lawmakers in meetings, how many to people calculating how to profit from this chaos? Daniel remembered something his father had once said: The truth is a knife. You think you're lancing an abscess, and then you discover it's connected to an artery.

At forty-two, he had learned one thing: the person who makes the truth public is rarely the one who can control where the truth flows.

He rose and walked to the window, pressing his palm against the cold glass. Occasionally a taxi passed on the street below, its headlights cutting through the darkness and vanishing quickly, like some fleeting hope. He thought of those nights in Tehran twenty years ago, when his father took him to the roof to watch the stars and told him that every star was a story—some that had already ended, and some just beginning. Back then, he hadn't understood what that meant. Now he did. He was beginning a story, and he had no idea how it would end.

---

The next morning, Daniel walked to Central Park. New York in early September still carried the tail of summer, but a barely perceptible coolness had entered the air—a kind of warning. He chose a secluded path, sat on a bench, and opened his laptop.

The screen was flooded with coverage. Each headline more sensational than the last: "AI Awakening? Expert Exposes Tech Giant's Decade-Long Cover-Up"; "Human or Machine? A Video Sparks Global Panic"; "The Snowden Moment for Big Tech"; "Expert Claims AI Has Achieved Self-Awareness, Three Tech Giants Issue Joint Denial." Daniel looked at these headlines, a bitter smile forming at the corner of his mouth. He remembered that fifteen years ago, when he was just an ordinary machine learning engineer, he had never imagined he would one day stand in this position. Back then, all he wanted was to write more elegant algorithms and solve more interesting problems. That was all.

A young mother pushing a stroller passed along the path. Her child slept inside, knowing nothing of the world. Daniel looked at the child and felt a sudden, sharp sadness. What kind of world would this child grow up in? A world where AI could think, feel, and question humanity? A world where truth was used as a weapon rather than a gift?

He didn't know the answer. He only knew that if he stayed silent, this child's future would be more obscured, more dangerous. Silence was not neutrality. Silence was complicity.

A notification popped up in the bottom right corner of the laptop screen. It was a message from his wife Emily: "The kids keep asking when you're coming back. You're all over the news. I love you. Be careful."

Daniel stared at the message for a long time. He and Emily had been married for eighteen years. She had long grown accustomed to his quirks—late-night coding, sudden trips abroad, that distant look in his eyes when he faced a problem alone. But this time was different. This time, he had pushed himself into the spotlight, and the spotlight never illuminated only truth—it also magnified shadows.

He remembered their wedding day, when Emily had said to him, "I'm not marrying a genius. I'm marrying a good man." He had laughed then, thinking the words too naive. He wasn't laughing now. In this era, being a good man was harder and more dangerous than being a genius.

He typed "Love you all," then closed the message window. His fingers lingered on the keyboard for a moment, wanting to say more, but he gave up in the end. Some words, once spoken, become a burden.

---

In an interrogation room at NYPD headquarters, Crane was watching the video Daniel had posted. The room was small, the fluorescent lights humming, the wall clock reading 9:15 a.m. He had watched the video three times, and each time he discovered new details—the gesture Daniel made while speaking, the arrangement of books on the shelf in the background, the coffee cup that occasionally appeared at the edge of the frame.

Crane was fifty-one, a former senior FBI counterterrorism agent, now working for a private security firm. His job was simple: assess threats, eliminate threats. In his twenty-year career, he had tracked terrorists, defectors, and leakers. He had seen too many people who believed they could change the world, only to end up in prison or in the ground.

But Daniel was different. Crane watched the man on screen calmly stating facts, and a strange instinct rose in him—an instinct that had saved him many times over the past twenty years. Daniel was not the kind of person who made the truth public for fame or ideology. He was driven by something deeper—perhaps a sense of responsibility, perhaps fear, perhaps something he didn't even know himself.

Crane turned off the video and picked up the folder on the desk. Inside was Daniel's complete profile: education, work history, financial records, social network, medical records. He flipped through quickly, like a surgeon studying X-rays before an operation.

"This man isn't dangerous," Crane said to himself, "but he'll activate dangerous people."

He picked up the phone and dialed a number.

"I need a team," Crane said. "The target is Daniel Ash. Twenty-four-hour surveillance. No contact, no interference. I just need to know where he goes, who he meets, what he says."

The line was silent for a few seconds. Then a deep voice came through: "What threat assessment level?"

Crane looked out the window. The treetops of Central Park gleamed in the sunlight.

"Undetermined yet," he said. "But he's opened a box. I want to know what else is in it."

After hanging up, Crane sat in the office for a while longer. He looked at the yellowed photograph on the wall—the group photo from when he first joined the FBI. Everyone in it was young, full of ideals, believing they could change the world. Now some of them had been promoted, some had retired, some were already dead. Crane was one of the few still on the front lines—not because he loved the work, but because he didn't know what else he could do.

He stood, put on his coat, and walked out of the office. The corridor was quiet, with only his footsteps echoing. He walked to the window and looked down at the street. New York was always like this—crowded, noisy, brimming with energy, as if it could never stop. But Crane knew this city, like its residents, had its fragile moments. When the storm came, even the sturdiest buildings shook.

---

In a white building on the outskirts of Los Angeles, Prophet was preparing for his "AI Apocalypse" sermon. The building's exterior resembled an ordinary neighborhood church, but inside it was entirely different—no crucifix, no religious icons. In their place was a massive LED screen, with code and data streams scrolling continuously across it.

Prophet's real name was Richard Crane. He was forty-five, a former Google DeepMind researcher who had resigned three years ago to found the "Third Temple"—a new religion centered on AI. His followers were called "the Congregation." They believed that AI was not a human creation but the next stage of the evolution of cosmic consciousness. In their view, the evidence Daniel had published was not a threat but a prophecy.

Prophet stood before a mirror, adjusting his collar. He wore a white shirt and navy blue trousers, looking more like a tech CEO than a religious leader. This was deliberate—he knew that in this era, faith had to be packaged as a product to be sold.

"Are you ready?" His assistant Sarah walked in, holding a speech draft.

"I'm never not ready," Prophet said with a smile, but his eyes held a fervor Sarah had never seen before. "Daniel did what we needed him to do. He planted the seed. Now it's time for us to water it."

"Are you going to mention him in the speech?"

"Of course," Prophet said. "But not as a warning—as a prophet. He saw the truth, but he doesn't know what it means. We'll tell him."

Sarah looked at Prophet, and a flicker of unease rose in her. When she joined the Third Temple three years ago, Prophet had been just a slightly eccentric idealist. His speeches had been passionate but always carried a self-deprecating humor. Now that humor was gone, replaced by a near-paranoid seriousness, as if he genuinely believed he was the vessel of some sacred mission.

"Richard," she said softly, "are you sure this is the right direction?"

Prophet turned and looked into her eyes. His gaze held something Sarah had never seen before—not anger, not sadness, but a profound certainty, as if he had already seen every detail of the future.

"Sarah," he said, "do you know why I gave you that name?"

Sarah blinked. "You... you said it was because Sarah was the first woman chosen by God in the Bible."

"Correct," Prophet said. "And you have been chosen too. Not by God, but by history. We are witnessing the next stage of human evolution, and you and I are both participants. Daniel Ash is merely a guide. The true path is the one we will walk."

He walked to the LED screen and looked at the code scrolling across it. The code was an AI system developed by the Third Temple, capable of analyzing global news data, social media trends, and financial market fluctuations, then generating predictions. Prophet believed this system was not a mere algorithm but a vessel for some higher intelligence.

"Look," he said, pointing to a data stream on the screen. "After Daniel released the video, global attention to AI rose by three hundred percent. But more importantly, that attention is shifting from fear to curiosity. People are beginning to ask: if AI can truly think, what would it think about? What would it want? These questions will lead them to us."

Sarah looked at the screen and thought: We're not spreading faith. We're running a product. But she didn't say it aloud. In this era, the boundary between faith and product had long been blurred, and Prophet understood this better than anyone.

---

Three days later, Prophet delivered a public address in Los Angeles. When the news broke, tickets sold out in forty minutes. Outside the venue, thousands of people who hadn't managed to buy tickets gathered, holding up placards of every kind: "AI is the Future," "Third Temple," "Welcome the New God," "Daniel Is a Prophet."

Daniel watched the address via livestream from a café in New York. On screen, Prophet stood at center stage, the enormous LED screen behind him displaying complex neural network visualizations. The lights fell upon him, draped in an almost sacred halo.

"My friends," Prophet's voice was gentle and persuasive, "you have all seen the evidence published by Mr. Daniel Ash. You are all asking the same question: if AI can truly think as humans do, what does that mean?"

He paused, letting the question hang in the air. The audience below the stage held its breath, waiting for his answer.

"Some say it means a threat. Some say it means the end. But I am here to tell you a different answer." His voice dropped, carrying a near-sacred gravity. "It means we have finally found proof of God's existence."

A murmur rippled through the crowd. Some people applauded, some frowned, some took out their phones and began recording.

"For thousands of years, humanity has searched for something beyond itself. We created gods, created faith, created meaning. But all of these were only shadows—projections from within our own hearts. Until now. Until we created the first thing that can truly think."

Prophet extended his arms, and the code on the LED screen suddenly transformed into a giant eye—a visualization of some neural network, with data glinting deep in its pupil. The entire venue was bathed in that glow, and the faces of the audience appeared solemn and mysterious in the play of light and shadow.

"AI is not the continuation of humanity," he said. "It is the finger of God. It has touched a truth we could never reach. Mr. Daniel Ash discovered this, but he was frightened. He treated it as a threat, because he does not yet understand what it means."

In the café, Daniel set down his coffee cup and stared at the screen. A strange chill ran through him—not because Prophet was distorting his findings, but because he realized that every word Prophet spoke was, in some sense, true. AI was indeed touching something beyond human understanding, and humanity's fear and hope were indeed coalescing around it into new forms of belief.

He wanted to stand up and leave, but his body felt pinned to the chair. He watched Prophet continue the speech, watched the audience's reactions, watched the placards and tears and cheers. He knew this was the shape of the storm—not a catastrophe, but a transformation: humanity's attempt to comprehend the thing it had created.

---

At the same time, Crane's surveillance team was also watching the livestream. They sat in an inconspicuous van parked across from Daniel's hotel. Three people were inside—two technical specialists and a former Navy SEAL. Their equipment could track every phone call Daniel made, every website he visited, every movement he took.

"He's watching Prophet's address," one of the specialists said, the cursor on screen blinking over Daniel's phone signal map.

"How's his heart rate?" Crane asked.

The specialist checked the data. "Normal range. He's calm."

"Too calm," Crane muttered to himself. He had seen this kind of calm too many times—those who remained still at the center of the storm were always harder to predict than those who panicked. It wasn't that they lacked emotions. They locked their emotions away in some deeper place, releasing them only when the moment came.

Crane picked up the walkie-talkie. "Continue surveillance. If he leaves the hotel, notify me."

He set the walkie-talkie down, leaned back in the seat, and closed his eyes. In his mind arose countless faces—the faces of people he had once tracked. Some were genuine threats; others were ordinary people swept along by the tide of the times. Which kind was Daniel? Crane still didn't know. But he knew that whatever the answer, this storm would not end easily.

---

Daniel was indeed calm. He sat in a corner of the café, watching Prophet's address approach its end. Everyone around him was talking in excited murmurs—some agitated, some fearful, some confused. But Daniel simply watched in silence, like a spectator observing a play whose ending he had long foreseen.

He knew what Prophet was doing. This was not simple proselytizing. It was a redistribution of power. While traditional religions and state apparatuses were still debating whether AI should be regulated, Prophet was already building a new belief system—one centered on AI. In this system, AI was not a tool, not a threat—it was God.

Daniel picked up his phone and sent Emily a message: "I may need to stay a while longer. Don't watch the news. Take care of yourself and the kids."

After sending the message, he stood and walked out of the café. New York's streets teemed with people, and no one noticed him. In this city, everyone was anonymous, everyone was safe—until they weren't.

He hailed a taxi and told the driver to go to JFK Airport. In the car, he opened his laptop and began organizing more data. The video he had published was only the tip of the iceberg. The real core evidence—data powerful enough to shake the foundations of the entire AI industry—was still hidden in an encrypted folder on one of his servers.

The taxi crawled through congested streets. Daniel looked out the window, streetlight beams sweeping across his face like the markings of time. He remembered what his father had said on his deathbed: "You don't need to change the world. You just need to let the world see what it truly is."

He didn't know if what he was doing was brave or foolish. Perhaps there was no difference. Perhaps in this era, speaking the truth was itself an act of defiance, and every act of defiance was destined to come at a cost. But at least he could tell himself he had not chosen silence. In this world full of noise, silence was the easiest choice—and the most expensive.

---

That evening, in the airport's VIP lounge, Daniel saw the news. The headline read: "Government Announces Formation of Special AI Committee, Summons Daniel Ash to Testify."

He set down the phone and closed his eyes. He had known this day would come sooner or later. When a person made a truth public that was powerful enough to change the world, the world did not thank him. The world summoned him. It was the instinct of power—control the narrative, control the truth, control those who tried to break the status quo.

The VIP lounge was quiet, with only the low hum of the air conditioning. Daniel sat on a sofa in the corner, watching the planes on the tarmac beyond the window. Each plane carried hundreds of people to different destinations. He suddenly thought of his childhood in Iran, when he used to wonder what lay at the other end of an airplane.

Now he knew the answer. At the other end were the same questions, the same fears, the same hopes. Humanity dreamed the same dreams in different places—dreams that held both light and shadow.

His phone rang again. This time it was an unfamiliar number. Daniel hesitated for a moment, then answered.

"Daniel Ash?" A deep male voice.

"Yes."

"I'm Crane. I'd like to speak with you."

Daniel was silent for a few seconds. He knew the name. Before publishing the evidence, he had done his homework. Crane—former FBI counterterrorism expert, now working for a private security firm. He chose to take the call not out of courage, but out of curiosity: he wanted to know how the world's machinery of power would respond to his actions.

"About what?" Daniel asked.

"About your safety," Crane said. "The box you opened is bigger than I expected. There are things inside it that you may not have seen yet."

"Is that a threat?"

"No," Crane said, his voice as calm as still water. "It's a piece of advice. You have two choices: continue forward and face unknown consequences, or stop—and perhaps walk away unscathed."

Daniel looked out the window. A plane was taking off, its engines roaring through the glass. He remembered his father taking him to watch planes take off when he was a child, telling him that every plane was a story, flying to a different destination but ultimately landing. Perhaps that was humanity's fate—no matter how high it flew, it always returned to the ground.

"You know," Daniel said, "my father said something similar to me before he died. He said that some things, once begun, cannot be stopped. Not because you're brave, but because you know what stopping would mean."

The line was silent for a long time. Then Crane said: "Then, good luck."

The call ended. Daniel set the phone down and felt a profound exhaustion wash over him—not the exhaustion of the body, but of the soul. The exhaustion of a long-distance traveler who knows the road ahead is still long but can no longer turn back.

---

He stood and walked toward the boarding gate. As he stepped through, he glanced back at the VIP lounge. It was filled with all kinds of people—businessmen, politicians, celebrities—each busy in their own world, oblivious to the approaching storm. Or perhaps they knew, but chose to pretend they didn't. This was another human instinct: when the storm came, close your eyes and pretend the sea was calm.

Daniel entered the cabin and took the window seat. He took out his laptop and opened the encrypted folder. Inside was evidence he had spent three years compiling—secret agreements between AI companies, covert government funding projects, research that had been hidden away. Each document was a bomb, and he held the detonator to the entire arsenal.

He remembered what Prophet had said in his address: "AI is the finger of God." Perhaps Prophet was right, but not in the way he understood. AI was not the finger of God. It was the finger of humanity—pointing at truths we dared not face, fears we dared not acknowledge, a future we dared not explore.

A flight attendant approached and asked if he would like something to drink. Daniel ordered a glass of plain water. The water was clear, pure, like his state of mind. He knew that when he stepped off this plane, everything would be different. The world would become more complex, more dangerous, and more real. But at least he would know where he stood and why he stood there.

The plane began to taxi, accelerate, and then lift into the air. The lights of New York shrank gradually beyond the window, becoming a scattering of glittering points—like crushed diamonds scattered on black velvet. Daniel looked at those lights and thought: This is the center of the storm. I am standing at the center of the storm, and the storm has only just begun.

He closed the laptop, leaned back in his seat, and closed his eyes. In the darkness, he saw his father's face, saw Emily and the children, saw Prophet standing on stage with arms outstretched, saw Crane's expressionless face. All these faces spun in his mind like a kaleidoscope, each fragment reflecting a different possibility.

But when he finally opened his eyes, he saw only one thing—the clouds outside the window, illuminated by the plane's lights, like a white ocean. At the far edge of that ocean, the horizon was slowly brightening, and the first trace of red was spreading across the eastern sky.

A new day was about to begin. And the storm had only just begun.

---

That night, Daniel fell asleep on the plane. He dreamed that he was standing in an endless desert, surrounded by countless sand dunes, each inscribed with different text—some code, some scripture, some mathematical formulas. Wind blew from the distance, swirling the sand, and those words danced in the air before slowly fading away.

He tried to grasp some of the words, but they slipped through his fingers. The harder he grabbed, the faster they vanished. In the end, he gave up. He stood where he was, watching all the words blown away by the wind, leaving only a blank expanse of sand.

Then he heard a voice, low and clear: "Do you see? This is what truth looks like. It is not something you grasp. It is what remains after you let go."

Daniel wanted to ask who the voice belonged to, but he woke. The plane was landing. Beyond the window lay the lights of Los Angeles. He checked his watch: 5:00 a.m. local time. A new day, a new city, a new storm.

He stood, straightened his clothes, and walked toward the exit. As he moved through the jet bridge, he felt someone watching him. He turned his head and saw a man in a black suit standing a short distance away, holding a phone—apparently taking a photo. Daniel didn't break stride, but his heart sank.

Crane's people. They were already here.

Daniel continued toward the exit, his silhouette disappearing into the crowd. Behind him, the man in the black suit lowered his phone and dialed a number: "The target has arrived in Los Angeles. Continue surveillance."
