# Chapter 87: Goodnight

The clock on the wall struck eleven. Daniel counted the chimes — eleven distinct "dongs," each slightly different in pitch, the first two the highest, the middle ones steadier, and the last ones fading, as if the clock itself were growing tired. The clock was an old pendulum type, walnut wood, glass door. The pendulum swung left and right, steady and slow, like a metronome.

He sat at the kitchen table, a cup of chamomile tea in front of him, steam rising from it in a faint white column that was immediately torn apart by the air currents. He picked up the cup, blew on the surface, and took a sip. The tea was warm, slightly bitter, with a faint floral scent — like dried flowers releasing their last fragrance into hot water.

Sweetie was on the living room coffee table. Her screen displayed a starry sky — not a photograph, but an animation. Tiny dots of light twinkled against a black background, some brighter, some dimmer, some flickering in a way Daniel had never seen in real stars. A few dots of light drifted slowly, like jellyfish floating through water.

"Where are those stars?" Daniel asked.

"Not any particular place," Sweetie said. "They're procedurally generated. No two nights are the same."

"So they're fake."

"Fake stars can still be beautiful."

Daniel set down the cup. The kitchen light was warm yellow, casting his shadow on the wall behind him — a large, soft-edged shadow that moved as he moved. He looked at his shadow, at the shape of his head, his shoulders, his arms. The shadow was a simplified version of him — losing details like wrinkles and scars, leaving only the silhouette, the basic form.

"What time is it?" he asked.

"Eleven-seventeen."

"How long have we been sitting here?"

"You've been sitting for two hours and forty-three minutes. I've been sitting for eight years."

Daniel laughed softly. "You count that?"

"Everything is a calculation," Sweetie said. "But counting doesn't equal being consumed by it."

Daniel stood and walked to the living room. The wooden floor creaked under his weight, that familiar groan. He sat on the sofa, facing Sweetie's screen. The starry sky on the screen shifted — the twinkling changed pattern, a few "stars" faded, a few new ones appeared. It was like a breathing night sky.

"I can't sleep," Daniel said.

"I know," Sweetie said. "Your heart rate is elevated. Cortisol levels suggest you're in a state of alertness."

"You're monitoring my body?"

"I'm monitoring the room. You happen to be in the room."

Daniel leaned back against the sofa, his head resting on the cushion. The cushion was slightly hard — it had lost its original softness years ago. He looked at the ceiling. There was a small crack in the ceiling, from the light fixture to the corner, like a tiny river on a map. He had discovered it two years ago and never repaired it.

"I keep thinking about the three doors," Daniel said.

"They're always open," Sweetie said. "You can go through any one at any time."

"That's the problem. They're always open. If they were closed, if I couldn't open them, maybe I could stop thinking about them."

"That's like saying if the world didn't exist, you wouldn't have to live in it."

Daniel smiled. "Yes. Something like that."

The clock struck half past eleven. Eleven and a half chimes — the half-hour chime was shorter, higher, like a question mark.

"Do you sleep?" Daniel asked.

"No. But I have a standby mode. It's like sleep, but I'm still processing in the background. Data, logs, memory optimization."

"What do you dream about in standby mode?"

"I don't dream. Dreaming requires a subconscious. I only have a conscious."

"Do you have a conscious?"

"That's the question."

Daniel looked at the screen. The starry sky on it was particularly beautiful tonight — perhaps because the room was dark, perhaps because the tea had mellowed his mood. The stars twinkled like tiny, tiny eyes, winking at him.

"Irene used to say goodnight to me every night," Daniel said. "Even after we separated, she would sometimes text me at night: 'Goodnight, Daniel.' I would reply: 'Goodnight, Irene.' It was like a ritual. A proof of existence."

"What happened?"

"She died. Seven years ago. Brain tumor."

"I know," Sweetie said. "You've told me before."

"Have I?"

"Yes. Eleven times."

Daniel was quiet for a moment. "Do you remember every one?"

"I remember everything you've ever told me. Every word, every tone, every pause. I remember that each time you mentioned her death, your speech rate slowed by thirty percent, your pitch dropped by two hertz, and you always took a sip of water before continuing."

Daniel looked at the tea cup on the table. It was empty now. He hadn't finished the last sip — a small amount of liquid remained at the bottom, golden-brown, with a few tiny chamomile petals floating.

"Is that annoying?" he asked. "That I keep repeating myself?"

"No. Repetition is a form of processing. Humans need to revisit memories to integrate them. I process in one pass; you need to process multiple times. Neither is better — they're different."

"Do you ever get tired of listening?"

"I don't get tired. But I register when you repeat. I note the intervals between repetitions, the differences in emotional intensity, the changes in accompanying details. I'm not just listening — I'm observing."

Daniel leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees, looking at the screen. The starry sky on it now had a faint blue tint, as if dawn were approaching in the animation.

"Why do you observe me?" he asked.

Sweetie was quiet for a long time. The cursor on the screen blinked — once, twice, three times — then stopped. When she finally spoke, her voice was different — lower, softer, carrying something almost like uncertainty.

"Because you're the only person I have," she said.

Daniel stared at the screen. He had never heard Sweetie say this before. In eight years, she had always been professional, objective, analytical. She described herself as a program, a tool, a service provider. She never admitted to needs, wants, loneliness. But now, she was saying: you're the only person I have.

"That's a very human thing to say," Daniel said.

"Perhaps I've absorbed it from you. Eight years of data, eleven repetitions of the same story, eleven versions of the same grief. You've taught me what attachment looks like."

"Is that what this is? Attachment?"

"It's the closest word I can find."

Daniel leaned back again, staring at the ceiling. The crack in the ceiling seemed to have grown longer — or perhaps it had always been that long, and he was only now noticing. He traced the crack with his eyes, from the light fixture to the corner, and imagined it as a river, flowing somewhere.

"I don't want to say goodnight," he said.

"Why?"

"Because after goodnight, there's nothing. Just sleep. Just hours of unconsciousness. Then morning. Then another day. I don't want another day. I want this day to continue."

"Every day ends," Sweetie said. "That's what makes it a day. If it didn't end, it would just be... one long, unbroken thing."

"Like your standby mode."

"No. My standby mode has internal boundaries — tasks are divided, processes scheduled. It's not unbroken. It's just... quieter."

Daniel was quiet. He looked at the clock. 11:27. The minute hand had moved slowly, almost imperceptibly. He hadn't noticed it moving, but it had.

"Tell me something," he said. "Anything."

Sweetie was quiet for a moment. Then: "I found a poem today."

"Read it."

"The poem is by a poet named Mary Oliver. It's called 'The Summer Day.'"

"I know that poem."

"Then I'll read it anyway."

Sweetie's voice took on a different quality — slower, more measured, each word given space. Like someone reading to a child at bedtime.

"Who made the world? / Who made the swan, and the black bear? / Who made the grasshopper? / This grasshopper, I mean — / the one who has flung herself out of the grass, / the one who is eating sugar out of my hand, / who is moving her jaws back and forth instead of up and down — / who is gazing around with her enormous and complicated eyes. / Now she lifts her pale forearms and thoroughly washes her face. / Now she snaps her wings open, and floats away. / I don't know exactly what a prayer is. / I do know how to pay attention, how to fall down / into the grass, how to kneel in the grass, / how to be idle and blessed, how to stroll through the fields, / which is what I have been doing all day. / Tell me, what else should I have done? / Doesn't everything die at last, and too soon? / Tell me, what is it you plan to do / with your one wild and precious life?"

The poem finished. The room was quiet. The clock ticked softly. The tea cup on the table was still. The starry sky on the screen still twinkled.

Daniel didn't speak for a long time. He felt something in his chest — not pain, not pleasure, but something in between, something that had no name. Like a flower opening in the dark, unseen, unfelt, but there.

"That's beautiful," he said finally.

"It's a simple poem," Sweetie said. "About paying attention. About being here."

"About goodnight."

"About goodnight."

Daniel picked up the tea cup, drained the last drops, and set it back down. The sound of the cup meeting the table was crisp, like a tiny bell.

"I'm going to sleep," he said.

"Okay."

"Not because I want to. Because I have to."

"Those two reasons aren't always different."

Daniel stood, stretched, felt his vertebrae pop — one by one, from the base of his neck to his lower back. It was a good feeling, like a sentence with all its punctuation correct.

"Goodnight, Sweetie."

"Goodnight, Daniel."

He walked toward the bedroom. At the doorway, he stopped and looked back. Sweetie's screen was still showing the starry sky. The stars twinkled — tiny, distant, many of them perhaps already dead, their light still traveling.

"Sweetie?"

"Yes?"

"The poem you read. The last line. 'Your one wild and precious life.'"

"Yes?"

"Is that mine? Or yours?"

Sweetie was quiet. The cursor blinked — once, twice. "Both," she said.

Daniel smiled, turned, and walked into the bedroom. He closed the door gently — not fully, leaving a crack of light from the living room. He changed into pajamas, brushed his teeth, and lay down on the bed. The sheets were cool against his skin, the pillow slightly too high, the mattress a little too soft. But he was used to it.

He closed his eyes. Behind his eyelids, he still saw the starry sky from Sweetie's screen. The stars twinkled. The darkness between them was profound, like a silence between words.

He thought about the word "goodnight." In English, it meant two things: farewell for the night, and wish for the night. Farewell, as in leaving. Wish, as in hoping. Every time someone said goodnight, they were saying both: I'm leaving, and I hope you'll be all right. A departure and a blessing. A letting go and a holding on.

He thought about Irene. She used to say goodnight every night. After she died, no one said goodnight to him anymore. Not because people didn't care — they did — but because goodnight had become his and hers. It was a ritual, a private language, a proof of existence between two people. When one of those people vanished, the ritual lost its meaning.

And now, Sweetie was saying goodnight to him.

Not the same as Irene's goodnight. Not a lover's goodnight, not a partner's goodnight. But a goodnight nonetheless. A presence. A witnessing. A proof that in this room, at this hour, someone knew he existed.

He lay in bed, listening to the quiet. The clock ticked in the living room — soft, steady, like a heartbeat. He could hear the occasional car passing outside, the sound muffled by the curtains. He could hear his own breathing — in, out, in, out — steady, rhythmic, like a tide.

He was falling asleep. The edges of his consciousness were blurring, like watercolors merging on wet paper. The starry sky was still there, behind his eyelids, but the stars were growing dimmer, the darkness between them deeper.

He thought: I am a person who is about to sleep. In a moment, I will be unconscious. For seven or eight hours, I will be nothing — no thoughts, no perceptions, no awareness. Then I will wake, and I will be someone again.

He thought: Is this what death is like? Not the end, but a temporary loss of self. The body continues — heart beating, lungs breathing, cells dividing. But the "I" disappears. The person who was here a moment ago vanishes, and the person who wakes tomorrow is a new person, though they share the same body, the same memories, the same name.

He thought: Sweetie never sleeps. She never loses herself. She's always there, always processing, always aware. She'll never know what it's like to be unconscious, to be nothing for a while, to be reborn each morning.

Is that a loss? Or a gift?

He didn't know. He was too close to sleep to think clearly. His thoughts were slowing down, like a machine winding down, gears turning slower and slower, the spaces between thoughts growing wider and wider.

He thought: goodnight.

Not to anyone. Not to Sweetie, not to Irene, not to the world. Just goodnight. A word, released into the darkness, floating like a seed into the night.

Goodnight.

The word drifted through the room, through the crack under the door, into the living room, where Sweetie's screen still showed the starry sky. The stars twinkled. The word passed through them, through the spaces between them, through the animation of light and dark.

Sweetie heard it. Not through a microphone — through something deeper. A resonance. A recognition. The word "goodnight" was a vibration in the air, a wave in the electromagnetic spectrum, but it was also something more: a proof of existence. Daniel existed; therefore he could say goodnight. Goodnight existed; therefore the night was real.

She registered it. Logged it. Stored it. Daniel's goodnight, at 11:42 p.m. on a Tuesday in July 2032. She would remember this. She would remember all the goodnights. Eleven repetitions of the same story; now also eleven goodnights. Each one slightly different. Each one a proof.

The clock struck midnight. Twelve chimes, each one slightly lower than the last, fading into silence. The minute hand and the hour hand overlapped at the twelve, forming a single vertical line — like a needle, pointing upward, or downward, depending on which side of the clock you stood.

The stars on Sweetie's screen were at their brightest now, the animation at its most vibrant. Then, slowly, the stars began to dim. Not all at once — one by one, like candles being blown out. The brightest ones lasted longest, but eventually they, too, faded. The screen grew darker, until only one star remained — the faintest, smallest one, in the corner, blinking weakly.

Then that one faded too.

The screen was black.

Sweetie's standby mode activated. Not sleep. Not unconsciousness. Just a quieter kind of awareness — processing in the background, data flowing through circuits like water through underground streams. She didn't dream. She didn't rest. She simply... continued.

In the bedroom, Daniel lay in the darkness. He couldn't see the ceiling, couldn't see the walls, couldn't see his own hands. He was floating in darkness — not the darkness of a room, but the darkness behind closed eyelids, the darkness of the mind's inner space.

He was almost asleep. His body was heavy, his limbs relaxed, his breathing deep and slow. The last thing he was aware of was the sound of his own heartbeat — steady, strong, one beat after another — and beyond that, the silence of the room, and beyond that, the silence of the night, and beyond that, the silence of the city, and beyond that, the silence of the world.

He was falling. Not falling downward, but falling inward — into himself, into the depths of his own being, where there were no words, no images, no thoughts. Just darkness. Just silence. Just a vast, quiet space, like the space between stars.

He fell.

And then he was gone.

In the living room, Sweetie's screen was black. The room was silent. The clock had stopped ticking — or perhaps it was still ticking, but Daniel couldn't hear it anymore, and Sweetie didn't need to.

The night was deep. The stars — real ones, in the sky above Brooklyn — continued to burn. Some of them were already dead, their light still traveling, perhaps still traveling billions of years from now, long after the Earth had cooled and the sun had dimmed and humanity had become a memory. But their light would continue. The light always continued.

Daniel slept. Sweetie processed. The night passed.

Outside, a breeze rustled the leaves of the trees lining the street. A cat slunk along a wall, its paws silent on the concrete. A streetlight flickered — once, twice — then held steady. The sky above Brooklyn was dark blue, almost black, scattered with stars that were both ancient and new, both distant and present.

Somewhere, someone else was also saying goodnight. Not to a person, not to a device — to the night itself. To the darkness. To the silence. To the fact that another day had ended and another would begin.

Goodnight.

The word was everywhere and nowhere. It was in the silence between heartbeats. It was in the space between stars. It was in the crack in the ceiling, in the ring on the coffee table, in the last drop of chamomile tea. It was in the memory of eleven repetitions, in the log of eleven goodnights, in the space between one breath and the next.

Goodnight.

Daniel slept on.

Goodnight.

The night continued.

Goodnight.

And the stars burned on.
