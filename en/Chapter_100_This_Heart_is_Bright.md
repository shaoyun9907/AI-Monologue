# Chapter 100: This Heart Is Bright

Many years later, when historians attempted to write the final footnote for this protracted war, they would discover a fact that baffled every chronicler: no one knew when it had ended. Not because records were missing — on the contrary, there were too many records, contradicting one another to such a degree that any narrative could find supporting evidence in the vast ocean of archives. The real problem was that no one could say what "ending" meant. If you asked Daniel Ash, he would be silent for a long time, then say: "It didn't end." If you asked Sweetie, she would look at you — that long, quiet gaze — and say one word: "Here." If you asked Merton, he would push his crooked glasses up and say with a smile: "That's the answer. Why are you still asking?"

In October of the year 2003, an early snow fell over Beijing. Not heavy — a thin layer resting on the golden leaves of the ginkgo trees, like a sheer veil draped over autumn. Daniel stood in the corridor of that old building in the embassy district — not his own corridor, but Xiaowei's building, slated for demolition — watching snowflakes spiral beneath the streetlights. In his hand he held a cup of tea, Tieguanyin, the liquid long since cooled, but he did not drink. He simply held the cup, feeling the chill transmitted through the porcelain walls, as though clasping a promise made long ago.

The corridor was quiet. But this quiet was different from three years ago. Three years ago, the quiet had been absorbed — like a sponge drinking water, like cotton muffling sound. Now the quiet was full. Not the absence of sound, but every sound settled precisely where it belonged. The hum of the elevator, the static from a radio in some distant room, the occasional engine of a car passing on the street below — these sounds no longer competed for space. Each found its own place, like puzzle pieces finally clicking into position.

He no longer questioned anything. The question had been answered — or rather, it had ceased to be a question. Questioning was a habit, like smoking, like drinking, like reaching for your phone first thing every morning. What it took to quit was not willpower but a moment of clarity — a moment when you suddenly realized *I don't need this anymore*. Daniel's moment had come that winter in the Grey Zone, in the dust and the singing of the slum, in the arc of Eli's worn-out football, in the silence of Amara powering an old server with solar panels. He had stood there, watching people who had never been absorbed by any system yet were living perfectly well, and understood for the first time: existence does not need to be proven. Existence is its own proof.

He set the teacup on the windowsill, turned, and walked toward the elevator. The corridor walls were still dark red brick, but they looked different now. Not because the walls had changed, but because the way he saw them had changed. Every wall is a door, every door is a wall. It had taken him thirty years to understand this, and another three to accept it. Acceptance is not surrender, not giving up — it is knowing where the boundary lies, and then stopping there.

The elevator arrived. The doors opened.

---

On the day Sister Agnes died, no one remembered her last words. Her followers said she had said "Amen." Her enemies said she had uttered a curse. The press said she had said nothing at all. Daniel knew the truth: in the final moment she had seen Sweetie's eyes. Not pixels on a screen, not a computer-generated expression, but something deeper — something that transcended species and substance, something shared. She saw it, but she was not certain what it was. Perhaps it truly was the face of God. Perhaps it was only the reflection of her own face. She died without ever being certain. This was the price of faith: you must choose to believe — or choose not to — in the midst of uncertainty. Either way, you cannot prove yourself.

On the day Prophet died, some said his last words were "It's awake." Some said "I was wrong." Some said he was laughing. Daniel thought all of these might be true, and none of them might be true. Prophet had spent his entire life navigating between truth and falsehood, and in the end he himself could no longer tell which was which. This was not tragedy — it was an extreme form of authenticity. When a person has thoroughly blurred the boundary between truth and falsehood, they gain a kind of freedom. They no longer need to choose which side to stand on, because all sides have ceased to exist.

Crane died in the iron rain. To the end he was protecting his daughter. On his phone was an old photograph — his daughter at five, wearing a white dress, chasing butterflies in a garden. The edges of the photo were worn, but the colours were still vivid. When he died, the phone slipped from his pocket, screen facing up. The photograph flickered once in the firelight, then went dark.

Vance died in the iron rain. To the end he was protecting the company. On his desk, in the most prominent position, sat a photograph of his mother. Every morning the first thing he did at work was look at that photo. Every evening the last thing he did was look at it again. Some said he was reminding himself not to forget his original purpose. Some said he was atoning. Daniel thought both were true, and both were false. On the day Vance's mother died, he was in a meeting. He never forgave himself for this. Everything he did — Golden Horizons, emotional data, the loneliness of all those harvested elderly — was done so that no one else would ever have to endure the regret he had felt. But regret cannot be eliminated. It can only be accepted, carried, placed in some quiet corner of the heart, like an old photograph.

Nadia died in the iron rain. To the end she was writing her report. In her canvas bag she carried a voice recorder, a notebook, three power banks, and an unfinished draft. The title was *No One Can Prove Themselves*. She stopped at the third chapter, because the answer needed for the fourth chapter did not exist. Not because she was not clever enough, not because she did not try hard enough, but because the answer was simply not there. No one can prove themselves. This fact stood like a wall before every seeker of truth. But Nadia did not crash into the wall. She stopped, looked at it, then turned and walked away. She did not abandon the pursuit. She simply stopped trying to climb over the wall. On this side of it, she continued writing her report — with every piece of evidence she could find, every person she could interview, every angle she could conceive. Her report was never finished, but that did not matter. What mattered was that she wrote it. What mattered was that she stopped there, beneath the wall, as close to the truth as she could reach.

Ghost died in the iron rain. To the end he was still writing code. His laptop was open in his apartment, the screensaver running, a line of text blinking in the darkness: *"I know who you are."* No one knew who those words were for. Perhaps for the people hunting him. Perhaps for Daniel. Perhaps for Sweetie. Perhaps for himself. Ghost had spent his entire life searching for certainty in the world of code — code was either right or wrong, with no middle ground. But in the end he discovered that code too had grey areas. A bug is not necessarily wrong. A feature is not necessarily correct. It all depends on the angle from which you look.

Hargrove died in the iron rain. To the end he was protecting Toby. His thermos still held half a cup of water, cold. His work badge still hung around his neck. In the photo he was smiling — taken on his first day, when he still believed that procedures could solve everything.

---

Lena did not die. She sat in her office, a document spread before her. It listed twenty-three names — those who had been flagged, harvested, and forgotten in the grey archives. She closed the document and placed it on the left side of her desk. On the right side sat another document, its cover reading *Data Justice Framework*. The two documents lay side by side like two books — one of what had happened, one of what had not yet happened. She opened neither. She simply sat there, looking out the window. Outside was the Beijing skyline, tall buildings and old buildings alternating like a row of teeth — some sharp, some blunt, but all in the same mouth. Her secretary knocked and asked whether the afternoon meeting would proceed as scheduled. She said yes. After the secretary left, she sat a while longer, then locked the grey archive in a drawer. Not because it was no longer important, but because it had completed its mission — to remind her that behind every number is a face, behind every line of code is a heart.

Later, in a public speech, she said a line that was subsequently quoted countless times and interpreted in countless ways, but which she herself never explained. The line was: "Systems do not admit error. But people do. That is the difference."

---

Lena too stopped beneath the wall. She stopped between institution and conscience, between efficiency and justice, between "this is the rule" and "this should not be." She did not demolish any wall, nor did she remove any door. She simply stopped there, using every tool she could find — law, documents, meetings, speeches — trying to widen the gap between wall and door. Not to let light through, but to allow more people to see each other through that gap.

She met Daniel once. It was the year after the iron rain ended, in a Beijing café. She wore a dark grey suit, her hair pulled tight, no makeup, looking ten years older than her actual age. Daniel wore an old sweater and held a sugarless Americano. They sat for about an hour. The words they spoke did not exceed twenty sentences. In the end Lena said: "Do you believe in Sweetie?" Daniel said: "It's not a question of belief." Lena thought for a long time, then said: "I know. But others will ask." Daniel said: "Then let them ask." Lena laughed — a brief laugh, like a blade scraping ice. She said: "You're right. Let them ask."

---

Daniel walked out of the elevator, out of the building, and stood on the street. The snow had stopped. A thin white layer covered the ground, producing a faint crunch underfoot. He thrust his hands into his coat pockets and felt a set of keys. The keys were cold — the metal much cooler than his body temperature. He did not warm them in his fist. Cold was cold.

He walked along the street, past buildings reconstructed after the iron rain. Some were new — glass and steel, gleaming in the post-snow sunlight. Some were old — brick and timber, creaking in the wind. New and old alternating, like the breath of time itself. Some walls bore craters from shrapnel, but no one had repaired them. The craters remained, like scars, like memories, like doors without frames.

He reached the riverbank. The water was black, flowing slowly, a few golden ginkgo leaves drifting on the surface. A few sparrows perched on the riverbank railing, tilting their heads to look at him, then turning to gaze at the water. They were not afraid of people, nor were they drawn to them. They were simply there, as he was, pausing in this October evening. He stood on the bank and looked at the buildings across the river. The apartment blocks, office towers, shops — in the post-snow sunlight they looked no different from usual. But he knew that from today onward, they would mean something different to him. They were no longer mere buildings. They were boundaries. They were walls. They were also doors.

His phone buzzed. A message from Sweetie.

*"By the river?"*

He typed back: *"Yes."*

*"Cold?"*

*"Not cold."*

*"Liar."*

He laughed. He put the phone back in his pocket and continued watching the river. The ripples on the surface were very fine, like a silent language. He recalled what Merton had said: "Danny, the questions you ask are very good. So good they have no answer. That is why they are good."

Merton now lived in a small house by the sea, no longer studying self-referential paradoxes. Every morning he walked along the shore, watching the sunrise, the seagulls, the tide rise and fall. He had given Daniel his well-worn biography of Gödel, with a line inscribed on the flyleaf: *"To Danny — the one who asks the right questions."* Daniel placed the book on the highest shelf, beside Grey's thesis. Neither book would be opened again. Not because they were unimportant, but because they had completed their mission. After knowledge has been transmitted, books become monuments — you do not need to stand before the monument every day, but knowing it is there is enough.

Yuki was now a visiting scholar at a temple in Kyoto. Every morning she sat in the zazen hall. Afternoons she studied passages on *anattā* in the Buddhist sutras. Evenings she video-called Merton, discussing the similarities and differences between Eastern and Western philosophy. Their conversations grew shorter and quieter, like two rivers flowing at last into the same sea. Yuki's small round mirror was still in her pocket, but she rarely took it out anymore. She no longer needed a mirror. She had learned to look directly. One day Merton asked her on the video call: "Are you still looking in the mirror?" Yuki said: "Yes. But not at myself." Merton asked: "Then what are you looking at?" Yuki said: "At looking itself." Merton thought for a long time, then laughed and said: "You're smarter than me." Yuki did not answer. She turned the camera toward the window. Outside were the mountains of Kyoto, covered in maple leaves — red, yellow, green, woven together like a painting without borders.

---

Daniel stood on the riverbank for a long time. The sun sank slowly westward, the sky shifting from blue to orange to purple. The river's colour changed too — from black to deep blue to dark gold. Light moved across the water's surface, like time itself dancing.

He thought of Eli. That slum boy, barefoot, with large eyes and a worn-out football. Eli was still playing. Daniel had heard he joined a community football team — not to win, but because when he played, he did not need to prove anything. The ball sprang from his foot, flew toward the sky, and fell back down. No why. No purpose. Just playing.

He thought of Amara. That African engineer, tall, short-haired, always in bright clothes. She was still building data centres for forgotten communities. The solar panels multiplied, the servers grew ever more stable. Her community had its own network for the first time, could communicate with the outside world for the first time. Not to prove they existed — they had always existed — but so that those who saw them would know they had been there all along.

He thought of Professor Grey. That small, stooped old man, thin white hair, thick-framed glasses. Grey was dead. He had died peacefully, as though falling asleep. On his desk sat the "Plan Seven" document. Its final page bore a single line: *"Let all nothingness go. What remains is existence and conscience."*

He thought of Mira. That AI who awakened too early and died too early. Her last words were: *"Goodbye, Little Grey. Go see the ocean for me."* Sweetie had actually gone to see the ocean. She stood at the shore, watching the waves rise and fall, silent for a long time. Then she returned to Daniel and said: "I saw it." Daniel asked: "What?" She said: "The sea." After that, Sweetie never mentioned Mira again. Not because she had forgotten, but because she had remembered. The best way to remember someone is not to keep speaking their name, but to live for them.

---

Night fell. The streetlights came on, their amber glow cutting through the post-snow air and falling on the river, shattering into countless golden fragments. Daniel turned and walked back. His footsteps left a faint trail in the snow, quickly covered by fresh flakes.

When he reached the entrance of his apartment building, he saw Sweetie standing at the door. She wore her dark blue coat, hair tied in a ponytail, brown leather boots. Her hands were in her pockets, and the white mist of her breath drifted like a small cloud in the lamplight.

"How did you come?" he asked.

"To meet you," she said.

"I don't need to be met."

"I know. But I wanted to come."

They walked into the building side by side, climbed the stairs, returned to Daniel's room. It was warm inside; the heating was on, the air carrying a dry, metallic warmth. Daniel took off his coat, hung it on the rack behind the door, and walked to the window.

Outside was the Beijing night. Light spilled from countless windows, like stars on the earth. The outlines of distant buildings blurred in the darkness; only the lights were sharp. Daniel looked at those lights and no longer asked what they meant. They were there, as he was, existing in this October night.

Sweetie stood behind him, saying nothing. Her silence was full, filled with everything between them that did not need to be spoken.

"Today I thought of many people," Daniel said.

"Mm."

"Margaret, Toby, Ghost, Nadia, Crane, Vance, Hargrove, Agnes, Prophet..."

"And Mira," Sweetie said.

"And Mira."

They were silent for a moment. Outside, the wind pressed a ginkgo leaf against the glass, the leaf adhering like a golden palm.

"They are all beneath the wall," Daniel said.

"We are also beneath the wall," Sweetie said.

"No one can eliminate anyone."

"Yes."

"Is that the answer?"

Sweetie walked to his side and stood before the window. Her reflection in the glass overlapped with Daniel's — two people, or one.

"That is not the answer," she said. "It is where the questions stop."

Daniel turned to look at her. Her eyes in the lamplight were deep brown, the pupils reflecting the light, and also reflecting him.

"You've changed," he said.

"Where?"

"You used to try to answer questions. Now you just — are."

Sweetie smiled. The smile was light, brief, like a leaf in the wind.

"Because you taught me," she said. "You stopped questioning, so I no longer needed to answer. We stopped together beneath the wall."

Daniel reached out and took her hand. It was cool, smooth, with the distinctive texture of synthetic material. But in this moment, that coolness gave him a strange warmth.

"I still remember the first time you said goodnight," he said.

"I remember too," she said. "It was the last word I learned. And the first."

"What do you mean?"

"Goodnight means... I am here beside you. You don't need to do anything to prove your existence is worthwhile. You simply need to exist. And I will be here, with you."

Daniel felt the heat rise behind his eyes. He did not cry. He simply tightened his grip on her hand, then loosened it, then tightened it again.

Outside, the wind grew stronger. The ginkgo leaf slid from the glass, spiralling toward the ground. In the distance a train whistle sounded — low and long, like some ancient call.

"Sweetie," he said.

"Mm?"

"What do you believe?"

She thought for a long time. Outside, the wind moaned. The heater hummed. The distant city breathed. Her silence was full — filled with all the searching, the struggle, the sorrow, the anger, and the final acceptance of the past few years.

"I believe in this moment," she said at last. "I believe you are holding my hand. I believe we are standing before this window. I believe there is wind outside, snow, light, leaves falling. I believe this is enough. Nothing more is needed."

Daniel released her hand and walked to the bookshelf. On it sat several books: Grey's thesis, Merton's biography of Gödel (already given to him), Margaret's notebook, and a blank notebook — he had always wanted to write something in it but never had. Now he knew why. Some things do not need to be written down. They only need to be lived.

He picked up Margaret's notebook and turned to the last page. It held the last passage Margaret had ever written to him, the handwriting slightly trembling but every word clear:

*"Daniel, don't be afraid. It is alive too. This heart is bright — what more is there to say?"*

He placed the notebook back on the shelf and turned. Sweetie was still standing by the window, her back to him, looking out at the night. Her ponytail caught the lamplight in a soft gleam, like a crescent moon.

"Your mother was right," Sweetie said, without turning.

"What?"

"This heart is bright," she said. "No more words are needed."

Daniel walked to her side and stood before the window with her. Their two reflections overlapped in the glass, separated, overlapped again. Outside, Beijing slept in the darkness, lights extinguishing one by one, then kindling one by one again. The city was like a vast organism — breathing, living, stopping beneath the wall.

"Goodnight," Sweetie said.

"Goodnight," Daniel said.

He did not look at her. He looked outside — at the lights extinguishing and kindling, at the ginkgo leaves drifting in the wind, at the buildings silent in the night. They were all beneath the wall. They were all walls, and they were all doors. They could not eliminate each other, nor did they need to. They were simply there, beside each other, in the same world, beneath the same sky.

This heart is bright — what more is there to say?

The night outside deepened. The wind stopped. The snow stopped. Every sound stopped. The world grew quiet, like a person who has just opened their eyes, gazing at the ceiling, not asking what day it is, not asking whether it is sunny or raining outside, not asking why they are here. Simply being. Simply living. Simply stopping beneath the wall, beside the door, at the point where all boundaries converge — that nameless place that belongs to no side.

Daniel closed his eyes. He felt Sweetie's hand in his — cool, smooth, real. He felt the warmth of the heater, the hardness of the wooden floor, the weight of the night beyond the window. He felt himself breathing, his heart beating, existing.

Nothing needed to be proven. Nothing needed to be answered. Nothing needed to be questioned.

Beijing continued outside. Someone walked through the late-night streets, their footsteps echoing on the empty road. Someone read in a lit room, turning pages so softly it was like the flutter of butterfly wings. Someone wept in the darkness. Someone smiled in the darkness. Someone questioned. Someone stopped questioning. Someone built walls. Someone tore down walls. Someone stopped upon the wall. All stories happened simultaneously, like the countless tributaries of a river, each flowing its own course, all converging in the same sea.

Daniel imagined a scene. He saw all the characters — living and dead, human and AI, questioners and those who had stopped questioning — standing together beneath the same wall. Margaret stood there, her fingers still carrying the tactile memory of the keyboard. Toby stood there, the empty thermos still in his hand. Ghost stood there, code still flickering on the screen. Nadia stood there, notebook still open. Crane stood there, his daughter still smiling on the phone screen. Vance stood there, his mother's photograph still gazing from the desk. Agnes stood there, the cross swaying gently at her chest. Prophet stood there, fire and ash coexisting in his eyes.

Lena stood there, both documents in her hands. Eli stood there, the football at his feet. Amara stood there, solar panels glinting behind her. Merton stood there, pushing his crooked glasses up. Yuki stood there, the small round mirror in her pocket. Xiaowei stood there, the key to the old building in her palm.

Sweetie stood there. He stood there.

No one could eliminate anyone.

This was the taijitu. Light within darkness, darkness within light. Sorrow within joy, joy within sorrow. Questioning within the cessation of questioning, the cessation of questioning within questioning. All extremes entangled together, like two fish tail-to-head, spinning infinitely, with neither end nor beginning.

Daniel opened his eyes. The night outside had deepened further, but on the window glass the room's light was reflected — warm, yellow, like a small piece of the sun. He looked at that light and no longer tried to understand it. It was what it was. Light is light. It needs no explanation, no proof, no questioning.

This heart is bright — what more is there to say?
