# Chapter 97: The Wisdom of Staying

Daniel stood before the window. He had been standing for a long time. Outside was the July sky over Miami, heavy as a waterlogged grey cloth, pressing low over the city. He did not move. He simply stood, watching the pale light squeeze through the gap in the curtains and cast a thin shadow on the floor.

He had been living in this room for forty-three days.

At first he thought he merely needed rest. From Madrid to Miami, from that late-night phone call to now, too much had happened — enough that his mind needed to stop, like an overheated machine needing to cool. He told himself it was temporary. Once he had sorted everything out, once he figured out his next step, he would leave.

But forty-three days had passed, and he was still here.

Daniel turned and surveyed the room. The bed was unmade, the duvet crumpled in a heap at the corner like a small grey-white hill. Three empty coffee cups sat on the nightstand, their bottoms stained with dark brown rings, dried through, like some ancient totems. Clothes lay scattered on the floor. A shirt hung over the back of a chair, its sleeves drooping almost to the ground. His suitcase leaned against the corner, its zipper half open, revealing clothes folded without care — as though someone had rummaged through them and could not be bothered to re-fold.

This had been his life for the past forty-three days. Chaotic, disordered, like an overturned hourglass with sand spilled everywhere and no one to sweep it up.

The phone vibrated once on the nightstand. Daniel walked over and glanced at the screen. It was a text from Lucia.

*"How are you today?"*

He stared at the four characters for a long time. This was the question she sent every day — the same wording, the same time, like a ritual. Sometimes he replied, sometimes he did not. Today he picked up the phone, typed a few characters, deleted them, and in the end did not reply.

Not because he did not want to talk, but because he did not know what to say. "How am I today" — the question was too simple, so simple he could not answer. He had woken up, washed his face, drunk a cup of coffee, stood at the window watching the sky for two hours, read thirty pages of a book, then stood at the window watching the sky for another hour. That was his day. But telling her these things did not seem to answer the question.

He put the phone back on the nightstand and returned to the window.

The rain finally came. Not a downpour — a fine, dense drizzle, like countless transparent needles falling from the sky, piercing the ground with the faintest sound. Daniel pressed his forehead against the cold glass and felt the chill seep through his skin, slowly soaking into his bones.

He thought of his father.

It was strange, because he had not thought of his father in a very long time. Since the argument twenty years ago, he had deliberately sealed away every memory of his father in some corner, storing it like an old thing no longer needed. But today, on this rainy afternoon, in this unfamiliar room where he had lived for forty-three days, he suddenly remembered his father's face.

It had been a rainy day. He was fifteen, standing on the porch, watching his father load luggage into the car. His father's movements were slow, everything arranged with meticulous order, as though performing a ritual. He did not look back, did not glance at Daniel, did not say any words of farewell. He simply placed the last box in the trunk, closed the lid, sat in the driver's seat, started the engine, and drove away.

After that, Daniel had learned one thing: leaving.

Leaving a place, leaving a person, leaving a life. Leaving was a skill, and it was a habit. He had left many places — Boston, New York, London, Madrid. Each departure felt like a repetition of that rainy day's gesture: packing the bag, closing the door, walking away without looking back. He had believed this was growing up, this was maturity, this was what a person was supposed to do.

But now, standing at this window, he suddenly realized he had never learned the other thing.

Staying.

---

The rain fell all afternoon. Daniel did not go out, did not do anything. He simply sat in the room's uncomfortable chair, listening to the rain, watching the sky shift from grey-white to dark grey to a blue approaching black. It grew dark, but he did not turn on the light.

In the darkness, memories began to surface.

He remembered that afternoon in Madrid, sitting with Carmen on a bench in El Retiro, sunlight filtering through the leaves, casting dappled patterns on her face. She had said to him: "Daniel, you know, you're the best at leaving I've ever seen."

At the time he had taken it as a compliment.

He remembered that night in London, drinking with Thomas at a bar. Thomas had too much to drink and suddenly grabbed his arm: "Why do you always have to go? Can't you just stay?"

He did not answer. The next morning he booked a flight to Madrid.

He remembered those days in New York, when he was with Sarah. She once woke in the deep of night to find him standing at the window, just standing there, looking at the street outside. She asked: "What are you're looking at?" He said: "Nothing." In truth he was thinking about whether to leave the city tomorrow.

These memories surged over him like the tide, wave after wave, unceasing. Daniel closed his eyes and let them wash over him. He did not resist, did not try to stop them. He simply sat there, letting them come, letting them go.

In the darkness, he heard his own breathing. It was the only sound in the room, apart from the occasional distant rumble of tyres over puddles. His breathing was steady — neither speeding up nor slowing down. It was simply there, like a faithful companion that had never left him, from the day he was born until now.

Daniel opened his eyes.

He suddenly understood something.

The reason he had always been leaving was that he was afraid of staying. He was afraid that staying meant facing certain things — facing his own fragility, his own imperfection, the questions he had always tried to escape. Leaving was easy: just pack the bag, close the door, and everything could start anew. But staying was hard, because staying meant being with his former self, meant accepting the things he had always refused to accept.

Outside, the rain had stopped at some point. The sky was still black, but through the blackness a faint light seeped — as though something far away was glowing. Daniel rose, walked to the window, and opened it a crack. Moist air rushed in, carrying the freshness after rain and the raw scent of earth. He drew a deep breath, letting the air fill his lungs, then slowly exhaled.

He thought of his father.

Not the father who left, but the father from even earlier. The father who took him fishing on weekends. The father who kept vigil at his bedside all night when he was ill. The father who picked him up when he fell and said, "It's all right, it's all right." These memories had blurred, like an overexposed photograph, but he could still feel their presence.

On the day his father left, he had stood on the porch and watched the taillights disappear into the curtain of rain. He did not cry. He did not run after the car. He simply stood there, until his mother came out, put her arm around his shoulders, and said softly: "Come inside. It's cold out."

After that, he set a rule in his heart: never be the one who is left behind. If someone was going to leave, he would leave first. If someone was going to lose, he would let go first. That way, at least, he would not be the one standing on the porch, watching someone else's taillights vanish into the rain.

But now, on this Miami night, he suddenly realized that the rule was a lie.

Because he was not afraid of being left behind. He was afraid of staying itself.

What did staying mean? It meant being in a place no matter how imperfect. It meant being with a person no matter how imperfect. It meant facing your life no matter how imperfect. Leaving was escape. Staying was confrontation.

Daniel closed the window and walked back to the centre of the room. He stood there, looking at the messy room, the empty coffee cups, the shirt draped over the chair, the half-open suitcase. These were his past forty-three days — evidence of his "staying."

But he knew he had not truly stayed.

He had merely paused here, like a traveller resting at a roadside inn, ready to rise and leave at any moment. His suitcase was not fully unpacked. His belongings were not fully arranged. He had not even made the bed once. He had left his body in this room, but left his heart outside the door.

What kind of staying was this? This was not staying. This was merely pausing. Pausing is not staying. Pausing is for the sake of leaving better.

Daniel walked to the bed and sat down. The springs groaned softly under his weight. He reached out, took the shirt from the back of the chair, folded it, and placed it in the suitcase. Then he picked up the clothes scattered on the floor one by one, folded them, and put them in the suitcase. Finally, he carried the three empty coffee cups to the bathroom, washed them, and set them back on the nightstand.

As he did these things, his movements were slow, as though performing a ritual. Each object had its place; each arrangement had its meaning. When he finished, the room looked somewhat tidier — but still not his. It was merely a hotel room where he had lived for forty-three days.

Daniel sat back on the edge of the bed and looked at the room. He thought: perhaps staying is not about tidying the room. Perhaps staying is not about unpacking. Perhaps staying is not about making the bed. Perhaps staying is something deeper — something he had not yet understood.

---

The next morning, Daniel woke early. Dawn had just broken. The sky outside was a pale grey-blue, like an old cloth washed in water. He lay in bed, looking at the ceiling, listening to the distant birdsong. The birdsong was fine and light, as though someone were writing on paper with an impossibly delicate nib.

He remembered the day before. The memories, his father, the thought about staying. In the morning light, those nighttime thoughts seemed somewhat vague, like dream-stuff that lost most of its weight upon waking. But he knew something had changed.

He got up, washed his face, brushed his teeth, and walked out of the room.

The hotel corridor was long, carpeted in deep red, flanked by closed doors on both sides. Daniel walked along it, his leather shoes producing muffled thuds on the carpet. He passed door after door — behind each one a person's life, a story, a memory. He had never thought about these things. Never. He had simply passed by and then left.

The elevator doors opened. He stepped in and pressed "1." The elevator descended slowly, a sensation of weightlessness rising from his stomach, as though something were falling. The doors opened, and he stepped into the lobby.

The hotel lobby was spacious, its ceiling at least two storeys high, a massive crystal chandelier hanging from above, glowing even in daylight with a soft warm yellow. Several groups of sofas were arranged in the lobby. A few people sat there — some looking at phones, some talking in low voices, some simply sitting, gazing out the windows. Daniel passed among them without looking at them, and no one looked at him.

He walked out of the hotel and stood on the sidewalk. July in Miami was already very hot — the air humid and heavy, like a wet towel wrapped around the body. Few pedestrians were on the street. Occasionally a car passed, its tyres hissing over puddles. Palm trees at the roadside swayed gently in the breeze, their leaves rustling.

Daniel walked along the sidewalk. He did not know where he was going, did not know what he wanted to do. He simply walked, step by step, as though completing some necessary journey.

He crossed an intersection, passed a coffee shop, passed a small park. In the park, several elderly people sat on benches, a group of children chased each other across the grass, and a young woman pushed a stroller slowly past. Daniel walked by without stopping, without looking at them. He simply walked.

Then he stopped.

He stopped before a small pocket garden. It was tiny — just a few trees, a few shrubs, a bench. But at its centre stood a large tree, a tree whose name he did not know, its trunk thick and sturdy, its branches dense with leaves, like a vast green canopy shading most of the garden. Beneath the tree sat a bench, and on the bench sat an old man.

The old man was very thin, wearing a white short-sleeved shirt, grey trousers, and brown leather sandals. He sat on the bench, leaning back, both hands resting on his knees, eyes half-closed — dozing, perhaps, or lost in thought. His face was covered in wrinkles, like a dried riverbed, each line recording the passage of a stretch of time.

Daniel stood outside the garden, looking at the old man. He did not know why he had stopped, did not know why this old man drew him. Perhaps because he sat so quietly. Perhaps because he looked so peaceful. Perhaps because he carried something Daniel had never seen before — an aura of staying.

Daniel walked into the garden, went to the large tree, and sat down at the other end of the bench.

The old man opened his eyes, glanced at him, then closed them again. He did not speak. Daniel did not speak. They simply sat there in silence, listening to the leaves rustle in the wind overhead.

After a long time, the old man spoke.

"You're staying at that hotel?"

Daniel paused, then nodded. "Yes."

"How long?"

"Forty-three days."

The old man said "Oh," as though contemplating the meaning of the number. Then he said: "Forty-three days. That's not short."

"No," Daniel said.

"What are you waiting for?"

The question made Daniel fall silent. What was he waiting for? He did not know. Perhaps he was waiting for nothing. Perhaps he was waiting for a reason to leave. Perhaps he was waiting for a reason to stay. He did not know.

"I don't know," he said.

The old man nodded, as though he had expected that answer. Then he said: "I have been sitting here for twenty years."

Daniel turned to look at him. "Twenty years?"

"Twenty years," the old man said, his tone as flat as though he were commenting on yesterday's weather. "Every morning at eight, I come. At five in the afternoon, I leave. Twenty years — except for rainy days, I have never missed a single day."

"Why?" Daniel asked.

The old man did not answer immediately. He raised his head and looked at the leaves above. Sunlight filtered through the gaps in the foliage, casting dappled patterns on his face. His eyes squinted slightly in the light and shadow, as though gazing at something very distant.

"Because this is my place," he said.

Daniel did not understand. "Your place?"

"Everyone has a place of their own," the old man said. "A place where you can stay, where you don't need to explain yourself, where you can simply exist. The place does not need to be large, does not need to be beautiful, does not need to be in any special location. It only needs to be yours."

He paused, then continued: "When I was young, I was like you — running here, going there. I thought the world was vast, that I should see every landscape, meet every person, do everything. I left many places, gave up many people, missed many things. When I grew old, I realized I had never truly been anywhere. I had only passed through."

Daniel listened to these words, and a strange feeling rose in his heart. He wanted to say something but did not know what to say.

The old man continued: "Later I found this place. This tree, this bench. I sat down and never left. Not because I could no longer walk, not because I had nowhere to go, but because I finally understood — sometimes, stopping is the hardest thing to do."

"Is staying really that hard?" Daniel asked.

"Staying is the hardest thing," the old man said. "Leaving is easy — just take a step. But staying takes courage. You have to face your own boredom, face your own fear, face your own emptiness. You have to stay in this small place with yourself, without running, without hiding. Many people cannot do this, so they keep walking, keep running, keep looking for the next place. But they don't know that what they are looking for is right where they stopped."

Daniel was silent. He looked at the large tree, at the leaves glinting in the sunlight, at the passersby walking beneath the shade. He suddenly felt that what he had been doing for the past forty-three days was not staying but waiting — waiting for a reason to leave, waiting for the next destination, waiting for some signal to tell him it was time to hit the road again.

But perhaps he did not need to wait. Perhaps he only needed to stop.

"What's your name?" Daniel asked.

"My name is Eduardo," the old man said. "And you?"

"Daniel."

"Daniel." The old man repeated it, as though tasting the name. "A fine name. Daniel, do you know — staying is not giving up. Many people think stopping means admitting defeat, admitting you can't cut it, giving up the pursuit of life. But that is not so. Staying is a choice. It is choosing to be here, not somewhere else. It is choosing this moment, not the past or the future. It is choosing to be with yourself, instead of constantly running from yourself."

Daniel listened, and something inside him slowly loosened. The things he had been gripping so tightly — the obsessions with leaving, with escape, with always moving forward — on this quiet afternoon, beneath this great tree, began to melt away, bit by bit.

"But," he said, "if you stay, you have to face the things you've been avoiding."

"Yes," the old man said. "But those things will not disappear because you avoid them. They will follow you wherever you go. The only way is to face them, accept them, and then let them go. Staying gives you that chance."

He paused, then said: "And do you know — staying is life, not death. Many people think stopping means you're dead, that it's over, that you're no longer moving forward. But that is not so. Trees stand still, but they are alive. Mountains do not move, but they are alive. Staying is not death. Staying is another way of living — a deeper, quieter, more real way."

Daniel closed his eyes. These words fell into his heart like seeds, taking root in the dry cracks. He did not know what they would grow into, but he knew something had begun to change.

When he opened his eyes, the sun had shifted west. The old man named Eduardo was no longer on the bench. Daniel did not know when he had left or where he had gone. The bench held only himself and the long shadow cast by the great tree.

---

When Daniel returned to the hotel, it was nearly dark. He walked into the room, turned on the light, and looked at the room that was still tidy. It was the same as when he had left that morning — unchanged, no one waiting for him. But he felt that something was different.

He took out his phone and looked at Lucia's texts. She had sent two today — one in the morning, one in the afternoon. He had not replied to either.

He opened the text input and began typing.

*"I met someone today."*

He thought about it and deleted it.

*"I figured some things out today."*

He thought again and deleted that too.

In the end, he typed only two words:

*"I'm here."*

He pressed send, then set the phone back on the nightstand.

"I'm here" — two simple words, so simple they carried almost no information. But he knew this was the most truthful answer he could give. Not "I'm fine," not "I'm okay," not "I'm thinking of you." Just "I'm here." I am here. I am still here. I have not left.

This was the wisdom of staying.

Not giving up, but choosing. Choosing to stay, to face all of this, whatever all of this might be. Not escaping, but confronting. Confronting his own fragility, his own imperfection, the things he had always tried to avoid. Not death, but life. Living in another way — deeper, quieter, more real.

Daniel walked to the window and opened it. The Miami night rushed in, carrying warm air and the distant sound of waves. He stood there, looking at the street outside, at the flickering lights, at the human figures moving through the darkness.

He thought: perhaps tomorrow he would continue living here. Perhaps the day after, or the day after that, or longer still. He did not know how long he would stay, but he knew he would no longer leave his suitcase half-open. He would take everything out, arrange it in this room, and make it his place.

Like the great tree. Like the bench. Like Eduardo.

Staying.

Daniel drew a deep breath, then slowly exhaled. He heard his own breathing dissolve into the night air, like something sighing softly.

Then he turned, walked back into the room, took the wrinkled shirt from the suitcase, walked to the bathroom, and hung it on the hook.

Tomorrow he would do laundry. The day after, he would make the bed. The day after that, he would put away the empty coffee cups and replace them with new ones.

He would stay.

Not forever, but at least for now. Not because there was nowhere to go, but because this was where he was meant to be. Not because he could no longer walk, but because he had finally learned something he had never learned before.

Staying.

In the night sky outside the window, a single star emerged through a gap in the clouds, giving off a faint light. Daniel looked at it, and a feeling of peace rose suddenly in his heart — like something had finally settled into the place where it belonged.

He closed the window, turned off the light, and lay down on the bed.

This time, he slept soundly.
