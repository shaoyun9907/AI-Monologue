# Chapter 98: Return to Civilization

The first thing Daniel did upon returning to the civilized world was stand before a mirror in the airport restroom and study himself for three minutes.

The mirror was the slightly concave kind common to airports, with a crack in the upper-left corner running from the edge to a point just right of centre, like an unhealed scar. The glass was fogged — not from temperature but from someone who had just washed their hands, the moisture still clinging to the surface. Daniel wiped it with his sleeve, and his face emerged from blur into clarity.

A forty-six-year-old face. A few more wrinkles at the corners of his eyes than when he had left, cheekbones more pronounced, skin a shade between wheat and bronze — the result of prolonged exposure to wind, dust, and sun. His hair had grown out, untrimmed, falling somewhat untidily across his forehead. The most striking change was in the eyes. Those eyes once held something — Daniel remembered looking in the mirror at thirty and seeing something like a flame: bright, hot, eager to burn. That something was still there, but its position had shifted, sinking from the surface into the depths, no longer spreading outward but drawing inward.

He looked at himself in the mirror and recalled what the old man in the Grey Zone had said. He could no longer remember the man's name — only that his hands were like dried bark, his fingernails packed with sand, and his voice rose as though from a very deep well. The old man had said: "People walk in civilization and stay in the Grey Zone. Those who walk don't know what they're looking for. Those who stay know what they're waiting for."

Daniel had not answered. He had already learned a new kind of silence — not the silence of having nothing to say, but the silence of feeling that anything said would be superfluous.

The airport PA was announcing the imminent arrival of a flight from Istanbul, in both Chinese and English, a female voice, even-toned, the gaps between each word exactly equal, as though measured with a ruler. Daniel listened and felt a strange alienation. How long had he been in the Grey Zone? Three months? Four? Time there had lost its calibrations — no distinction between workdays and weekends, no boundary between morning and evening, only the brightness of light and the degree of warmth. He had grown accustomed to that uncalibrated time, and now, hearing this announcement precise to the minute, it was like someone scratching a blackboard with a fingernail beside his ear.

He left the restroom and pushed his luggage trolley into the arrivals hall.

The hall was crowded. Some people held up signs, some embraced, some talked on phones, some stared at phone screens, some searched for their way. Everyone was moving, talking, heading in some direction. Daniel stood still, letting the crowd flow past him like a stone standing in the middle of a river. He noticed a detail: nearly everyone here was doing two things at once. The person on the phone was also walking; the person on the phone was also queuing; the people embracing were also talking. In the Grey Zone, people did one thing at a time. Walking was walking, talking was talking, waiting was waiting. No one did two things at once, because each thing required your full attention — otherwise you might step on a landmine, or miss a signal, or appear in the wrong place at the wrong time.

Daniel found the taxi queue. It was not long — seven or eight people. He stood at the back, watching each person ahead get in. When his turn came, the driver helped him load his luggage into the trunk and asked: "Where to?"

"Near the airport," Daniel said. "Any hotel will do."

The driver glanced at him in the rear-view mirror. A quick, professional assessment — the kind used to determine whether a passenger was local or from out of town, whether they were trouble or not. Daniel knew this look. In the civilized world, everyone was being judged this way, at every moment — from the moment you walked into a restaurant to the moment you left a supermarket, from the moment you got into one taxi to the moment you got out.

"Back from abroad?" the driver asked.

"Mm."

"Where?"

"The Middle East."

The driver nodded, as though the answer confirmed something. "Things are really messy over there."

Daniel did not respond. Outside the window was the city at dusk, the sky that grey-blue that was neither day nor night — a transitional state. The streetlights were on, but the daylight had not entirely faded. The two light sources mixed together, making everything look somewhat unreal, as though two different coloured lamps had been turned on simultaneously. The road was thick with cars, each one honking, the horns rising and falling in a contest with no referee.

Daniel looked at the buildings outside. When he had left, they were constructing a new commercial centre. It was now complete, its glass curtain wall reflecting the streetlight like a massive luminous cuboid. Beside it was a pedestrian street lined with brightly lit shops, their signs displaying Chinese, English, Korean. People on the street wore clothes of every colour, walked at every speed — some fast, some slow, some pausing before shop windows, some looking down at phones, some gazing up at the sky.

He remembered an old man in the Grey Zone, seventy-odd years old, sitting by the roadside selling lemons. His stall was tiny — just a cloth spread on the ground, a dozen or so lemons arranged on it, each one wiped clean, glowing yellow. The old man did not call out, did not solicit — he simply sat, eyes half-closed, dozing. Daniel had bought one as he passed. The old man handed it to him and said something in Arabic. Daniel did not understand, but judging from the tone and gesture, it was probably something like "peace be upon you." That old man and his lemon stall had stood at that intersection for many years — decades, passed down from the old man's father. Now that intersection had probably become a parking lot or a fast-food restaurant. The old man was probably gone. Those lemons had probably been replaced by other fruit.

The taxi stopped at a hotel. Daniel paid, collected his luggage, and walked into the lobby. The temperature was at least ten degrees cooler than outside, the air conditioning running full blast. The air carried a blend of disinfectant and perfume. The receptionist was a young woman in a dark blue uniform, her hair neatly pinned up, her face wearing a standard smile.

"Good evening, sir. Do you have a reservation?"

"No."

"How many nights, please?"

Daniel thought for a moment. He did not know how long he would stay here, did not know where he would go next. In the Grey Zone, he had never needed to answer such questions. In the Grey Zone, you did not need to tell anyone how long you would stay, because no one would ask. Everyone was there. That was all.

"Three nights to start," he said.

The woman typed a few keystrokes and handed him a key card. "The elevator is on your left. Room 1207. Breakfast is on the second floor, seven to ten."

Daniel took the card and walked to the elevator. There was a mirror inside. He did not look at it. He pressed the button for the twelfth floor. The elevator rose. He felt a faint sensation of weightlessness, a floating feeling in his stomach. He had taken an elevator once in the Grey Zone — in a building that had been half-bombed. The elevator still worked, but each floor it passed produced a harsh metallic screech, as though it might stop at any moment. That elevator had no mirror, only four grey metal walls, on which someone had scrawled with a marker. He could not read Arabic, but judging by the hurried strokes, it was probably nothing complimentary.

The room was on the twelfth floor, facing the other side of the city. A river was visible. Daniel set down his luggage and walked to the window. The river was black under the lights. Occasionally a boat passed, its lamp trailing a long ribbon of light across the water, like a golden snake gliding. On the opposite bank was a residential district, windows lit one by one, like a vast chessboard — each square a different story.

He drew back the curtain and let the city's noise in. Horns, music, voices, wind — all blending into a continuous, low hum, like a vast machine in operation. In the Grey Zone, nights were quiet — so quiet you could hear your own heartbeat, hear the sound of sand shifting in the wind, hear the cry of an unnamed animal in the distance. That quiet was not the absence of sound but the scarcity of it, scarce enough for you to distinguish each individual sound. Here the noise was not the absence of quiet but quiet drowned by sound, packed so densely that nothing could seep through.

Daniel sat on the edge of the bed and took out his phone. The phone had finally regained signal since he left the Grey Zone. Notifications popped up one after another, like a clogged pipe suddenly clearing. Emails, texts, WeChat messages, news alerts, app updates. He did not open any of them. He simply watched the red numbers flickering in the corners of the icons, like a group of small animals waiting to be fed.

He set the phone on the nightstand, stood, and walked into the bathroom. The bathroom light was that white, blinding kind that eliminated all shadows. He turned on the tap. Hot water flowed, steam rose, the mirror fogged again. He stripped, stood under the shower, and let the hot water pour down from the crown of his head.

The water ran over his face, his neck, his shoulders, his chest. In his three months in the Grey Zone, he could count his baths on one hand. Each time it was bucket water — cold, a splash on the body, scrubbed with sand, another splash to rinse. No hot water, no showerhead, no tiles, no drain. Now the hot water struck his skin and he felt a warmth so intense it was almost painful, as though countless fine needles were pricking him.

He closed his eyes and let the water wash away the sand, sweat, and dust. The water turned grey, then pale yellow, then clear. He stood beneath it, listening to the sound of water hitting tile — a sound completely different from water hitting sand. Water hitting sand was muffled, light, almost inaudible. Water hitting tile was sharp, heavy, distinct.

He washed for a long time, long enough for the water temperature to shift from hot to warm, from warm to cool. He turned off the tap and stood in the bathroom, dripping, looking at his blurred reflection in the mirror. The steam was slowly dispersing, his outline emerging from blur into clarity. He recalled the old man's words: "People walk in civilization and stay in the Grey Zone." He wondered — was he walking now, or staying? He had returned to civilization, but the Grey Zone's rhythm still lived in his body. His heartbeat was the Grey Zone's heartbeat. His breathing was the Grey Zone's breathing.

He dried himself, put on the hotel's white bathrobe, and walked out. The phone on the nightstand was still flashing red. He picked it up, opened WeChat, and saw that Sweetie's last message had been sent three days ago: *"Did you arrive? Are you safe? Waiting for your message."* He did not reply. He set the phone down and lay on the bed.

The ceiling was white. A lamp hung in the centre, its shade frosted glass, the light filtering through soft and warm yellow. In the Grey Zone, he had slept in tents, in abandoned rooms, or in the open. Sleeping under the open sky, you could see stars — so many they were frightening, densely packed across the entire sky, as though someone had upended a bag of crushed diamonds onto a black velvet cloth. Here you could see no stars, only this lamp — this warm yellow lamp behind its frosted glass shade.

Daniel closed his eyes. He thought he would fall asleep quickly. After all, it had been a long time since he had slept in a real bed, a long time since he had slept in a room with air conditioning, hot water, and curtains. But he could not sleep. His body lay on the bed, but his heart was still in the Grey Zone — still in that place of fierce wind, poor signal, and hot water as luxury. His ears had grown accustomed to the Grey Zone's quiet, and now, surrounded by the city's noise, he was more awake than ever. He could hear footsteps in the corridor, a television in the next room, the hum of the air conditioner, the sound of his own blood flowing through his veins.

He rolled over, rolled over again, and finally sat up.

He checked his phone. More new messages. An email with the subject line "Meeting Invitation: Progress Report on Project XX," from his boss, sent two weeks ago. A text from his ex-wife: *"Xiao Ming's birthday is next week. Will you come back?"* And a WeChat message from someone called "Old Zhang": *"Brother, it's been a while. How are things?"*

Daniel set the phone down. He remembered a doctor in the Grey Zone — a man in his sixties, his beard streaked with white, wearing a blue robe washed almost white. The doctor treated patients in a bombed-out school that had no roof, only four walls and a few pillars, on which bullet holes were still visible. The doctor's "clinic" was a corner of the schoolyard, covered with a plastic sheet, on which were arranged a few bottles, several rolls of bandage, a pair of scissors, and a box of needle and thread. No sterilization equipment, no operating table, no anaesthetic — even painkillers were a luxury.

Daniel had asked the doctor: "Why don't you go somewhere else? To a big city, to a place without war, to a place with a hospital?"

The doctor was bandaging a little girl's arm. She had been cut by shrapnel — not deep, but long, running from wrist to elbow. His movements were slow and gentle, each wrap maintaining the same spacing as the last, as though crafting a piece of fine art. He did not look up at Daniel. He simply said one thing:

"If I leave, who will wrap this bandage?"

That question lingered in Daniel's mind for a long time. He did not know the answer, but he knew the question was more important than any answer. In the civilized world, people constantly asked "why" — why it was this way, why it was not that way, why he could and I couldn't, why this happened and that didn't. In the Grey Zone, people did not ask "why." They asked "what now?" The bomb has fallen — what now? The pipe has burst — what now? Someone is hurt — what now? There was no "why did the bomb fall," no "why did the pipe burst," no "why is someone hurt." "Why" was a luxury. Before survival, "what now" was the only question.

Daniel lay down again. The city noise outside continued, but his ears were beginning to adapt. He remembered that night in the Grey Zone, sitting with Sweetie on the second floor of an abandoned building, watching the distant skyline. There was fire on the horizon — not a sunset, something burning somewhere. They did not speak. They simply sat, watching the fire, listening to the occasional distant gunfire, as though listening to a very, very distant song. Sweetie had said something then, her voice very soft, almost a whisper: "Have you ever thought about why we're here?" Daniel did not answer, because he did not know. But that "why" was different from the civilized world's "why." The civilized world's "why" chased causes. Sweetie's "why" confirmed existence.

The next morning, Daniel was woken by the alarm on his phone — the built-in kind, synthetic, sharp, without warmth. Completely different from the Grey Zone's alarm. The Grey Zone had no alarms. The Grey Zone's alarm was light. The sun rose, light crept through the gaps in the tent or the broken holes in the window, fell on your face, and you woke. Light's alarm was gradual, slow, natural — you could feel the entire process from dark to bright, as though someone were slowly turning up the room's lamp. The phone's alarm was abrupt, violent, without warning — from zero to a hundred in a single second, as though someone had thrown a bucket of cold water in your face.

Daniel turned off the alarm and sat up. His body ached — not the ache of exercise, but the ache of having been curled in one position for too long. In the Grey Zone he had slept on the ground, on hard floors or sand, never anything as soft as a mattress. The soft mattress had robbed his spine of support; his entire back felt as though someone had beaten it with a stick.

He washed, dressed, and left the room. The hotel corridor was carpeted in thick, dark red carpet with a repeating pattern. Walking on it produced no sound. In the Grey Zone, walking always produced sound — sand whispered, gravel crunched, concrete rubble creaked. You could never truly walk quietly. Your footsteps told everyone where you were. Here that was not a problem. The carpet devoured all your footstep sounds. You could glide through the corridor like a ghost, and no one would know you had passed.

He reached the elevator and pressed the down button. The doors opened. Two people were already inside — a man in a suit and a woman in sportswear. The man was on the phone, his voice very low, but Daniel could still catch a few words: "meeting," "report," "deadline." The woman was looking at her phone, her finger swiping rapidly across the screen, as though searching for something.

Daniel stepped in and stood in the corner. The elevator descended. The weightlessness returned. He watched the floor numbers change — 12, 11, 10 — each floor a step, carrying him from one height back to ground level. He thought of that half-building in the Grey Zone, where the elevator only reached the fourth floor. Above the fourth there were no more floors — only sky and bullet holes. From the fourth-floor window you could see the entire city in ruins, like an overturned sand table. All the buildings toppled, all the streets severed, all the人流散. Now the elevator carried him from the twelfth floor to the first, from a complete building into a complete city, every floor intact, every door closeable, every light lit.

Breakfast was on the second floor. The dining room was large enough for hundreds, but only a dozen or so people were eating. Each person had a plate — bread, eggs, fruit, salad, and beverages of every colour. Daniel found a corner seat. A server came over quickly and asked what he would like.

"Coffee," he said. "Black coffee."

The server nodded and left. Daniel looked around at these people. The man in the suit was eating bread while reading a document on a tablet. The woman in sportswear was drinking juice while talking to the person beside her. An elderly couple were taking photos while feeding each other fruit. Everyone was doing at least two things at once, as though competing in a contest where the one who did the most won.

The coffee arrived. Daniel lifted the cup and took a sip. It was hot, bitter, caffeinated — utterly different from the Grey Zone's coffee. Grey Zone coffee was brewed in something called a *qahwa*, made from coarsely ground coffee and cardamom. It came out dark brown, very strong, with the scent of earth and spice. That coffee was not for savouring. It was for staying alert. In the Grey Zone, you might need to be alert at any moment, because danger did not confine itself to daylight.

Daniel set down the cup and took out his phone. He opened WeChat, found Sweetie's conversation, and typed: *"I'm back."*

The message was sent. Less than ten seconds later, Sweetie replied. At the top of the conversation, "typing..." appeared for about thirty seconds, then a single line: *"Where are you?"*

*"A hotel near the airport."*

*"I'll come find you."*

*"No need. Wait there. I'll come to you."*

*"Okay. When will you arrive?"*

*"This afternoon. I need to take care of some things first."*

*"Okay. I'll wait for you."*

Sweetie's profile picture was a photo she had taken in the Grey Zone. The background was a ruin, her face in shadow, only one eye catching the light. In that eye was something Daniel could not name — not fear, not sorrow, not hope, something more complex than all of those. During their days in the Grey Zone, they had not talked about that something. They had talked about weather, food, routes, safety, politics, history, religion — but not about what was in that eye. Some things, once spoken, change — like gas sealed in a jar; open the lid and it evaporates.

Daniel finished breakfast and returned to the room. He opened the suitcase and began sorting through his things. Inside were the items he had accumulated during three months in the Grey Zone: a few dirty clothes, a handful of sand (he did not know why he had brought it back — perhaps as a keepsake, perhaps he simply forgot to empty it), a compass, a roll of bandage, a few bullet casings, a notebook, a pencil. The notebook was one he had bought in the Grey Zone — very thin paper with rough edges, its cover printed with local script he could not read a word of. The pencil was an ordinary wooden one, the barrel printed with a brand name and a place of origin that was not China but a small country he had never heard of.

He opened the notebook and turned to the first page. On it was a date — his first day in the Grey Zone. Every subsequent page carried a date; some dates were followed by a few lines, some by nothing at all. He turned to the last page. It bore two characters: "Departure," dated the day he left the Grey Zone.

He closed the notebook and placed it on the nightstand. Then he picked up his phone and searched for the nearest bookstore. The system showed one a kilometre away — a chain bookstore on the third floor of a shopping centre. He decided to go.

Stepping outside, the sunlight was bright — autumn sunlight, bright but not blinding, warm but not scorching. Far more pedestrians were on the street than the evening before. Everyone was walking, hurrying, heading somewhere. Daniel merged into the crowd and entered the shopping centre. It had air conditioning, music, fragrance — a carefully designed space where every sense was attended to, every need considered, every corner illuminated.

The bookstore was on the third floor. Not large, but packed with books — from floor to ceiling, three walls were bookshelves. The shelves were dark wood, the spines a mosaic of colours, like shattered stained glass. Daniel walked slowly between the shelves, his fingers trailing along the spines, feeling the different textures of the covers: smooth, rough, patterned, embossed. He stopped before a row of history books and pulled out one on modern Middle Eastern history. It was thick, over six hundred pages, its cover a black-and-white photograph of ruins — strikingly similar to the Grey Zone's ruins.

He opened the book. The table of contents listed "War and Peace," "Empire and Colonization," "Revolution and Restoration" — each chapter title grand and sweeping, summarizing a century in a single phrase. He recalled the doctor's words: "If I leave, who will wrap this bandage?" That question would not be answered in this book's table of contents. This book would tell him the causes of war, the process of war, the outcome of war — but it would not tell him who wrapped the bandage, would not tell him what happened to the little girl's arm, would not tell him where the doctor went afterward.

He put the book back and continued walking. He paused at the psychology shelf. There was a book called *Trauma and Recovery*, beside it *The Resilience Factor*, beside that *Walking Out of Shadow*. All had similar cover designs: a dark background, a blurred human figure, a beam of light shining in from some direction. He picked up *Trauma and Recovery*, flipped a few pages, and saw terms like "post-traumatic stress disorder," "flashback," "avoidance behaviour," "emotional numbing." He closed the book and put it back. He had never heard these terms in the Grey Zone. The people there did not use such words. They only said "scared," "homesick," "can't sleep," "having nightmares."

He left the bookstore without buying anything. As he exited the shopping centre, he sat on a bench at the entrance for a while. The bench was metal, with a backrest, painted green, the paint flaking to reveal silver-grey metal beneath. He sat and watched the people on the street.

He noticed a young man, perhaps early twenties, wearing a T-shirt with a band name printed on it, headphones on, walking and gesturing in the air with one hand while muttering under his breath. He passed Daniel without a glance, completely absorbed in his own world. In the Grey Zone, you did not walk like that. You did not wear headphones. You did not stare at your phone. You did not ignore the people around you. In the Grey Zone, you had to be constantly aware of your surroundings, because danger could come from any direction. Here there was no danger — or rather, the danger was invisible, hidden beneath the surface of daily life, requiring you to dig before you could find it.

Daniel sat a while longer, then stood and walked toward the subway station. He was going to see Sweetie. The station was even more crowded than the airport. Everyone was moving, flowing like water from one direction toward another. Daniel bought a ticket from a vending machine and followed the stream to the platform. The platform lights were white, bright enough to make you squint. The walls were plastered with advertisements — cosmetics, real estate, education and training. Every ad conveyed the same message: you need this, you need more, you need better.

The train arrived, the doors opened, and Daniel stepped into the carriage. It was very crowded. He was wedged between two strangers — one on the phone, one eating. The person on the phone was saying work-related things; the eater was eating a hamburger. The air was saturated with the smell of sauce and bread. Daniel closed his eyes and withdrew his consciousness to a deeper place. He remembered the Grey Zone's transport — a battered pickup truck with over a dozen bullet holes in its body, its windows shattered and patched with plastic sheeting and tape. Four of them sat in the cabin, pressed together, no one speaking, only the engine's roar and the wind whistling through the plastic. The truck bounced along a dirt road; each jolt threw them up and then down, like a very slow roller coaster.

The train arrived at his stop. Daniel walked out of the carriage and up to ground level. This was another part of the city, different from the area near the airport — older, more worn, bearing more traces of life. The buildings on either side of the street dated from the last century. Some had been renovated, their exterior walls retiled; others were still old, the cement crumbling to expose the red brick beneath. Street vendors lined the roadside — selling fruit, roasted sweet potatoes, *jianbing* — each stall drawing a small queue.

He turned into a narrow alley, barely wide enough for two people to walk abreast. Residential buildings flanked both sides, so close together that you could almost reach across and touch the opposite wall. Laundry lines stretched from one window to another, hung with clothes of every colour, swaying gently in the breeze. As Daniel walked, his shoulder brushed a white shirt hanging outside a window — slightly yellowed, with a small oil stain at the collar.

At the end of the alley stood a three-storey building. A sycamore tree grew at its entrance, its leaves still green, not yet turned to yellow. Beneath the tree sat a stone table and a few stone stools, the table cracked, moss growing in the fissures. Sweetie was sitting on one of the stools, holding a cup of tea — an ordinary ceramic cup with a small red flower printed on its side.

She saw Daniel and stood. She was thinner than the last time he saw her, the line of her jaw more defined, faint dark circles beneath her eyes, but her hair was neatly combed and tied back in a ponytail. She wore a light blue cotton dress — old, the cuffs slightly frayed, but very clean.

She did not run to embrace him. She simply stood there, watching him approach. Daniel stopped before her. The two looked at each other for several seconds without speaking. Then Sweetie reached out and lightly touched the back of his hand. Her fingers were cool, trembling slightly, but they steadied quickly.

"You've lost weight," she said.

"So have you."

They sat down. Sweetie poured him a cup of tea. It was green, very pale, as though steeped for only a minute or two. Daniel lifted the cup and took a sip. The tea was warm, not scalding, with a faint fragrance — utterly different from the Grey Zone's tea, which was thick, sweet, and heavy with mint, tasting more like drinking a flavoured liquid than a beverage.

"How were you over there?" Sweetie asked. Her voice was calm, as though asking an ordinary question, but Daniel could hear what lay beneath.

"Fine."

"Were you hurt?"

"No."

"Were you ill?"

"No."

Sweetie nodded and looked down at her cup. The tea inside trembled faintly, reflecting her image — blurred, distorted, like a face in a funhouse mirror.

"What are your plans now that you're back?" she asked.

Daniel thought. During his three months in the Grey Zone, he had never considered this question, because it did not apply there. In the Grey Zone, you did not need "plans." You only needed to be alive. Being alive was the grandest plan; everything else was secondary. Now he had returned to civilization, and being alive was no longer the question. How to live was the new one.

"I don't know," he said. "I haven't decided yet."

Sweetie looked up at him. Her eyes were as he remembered — deep brown, with large pupils that looked black in certain light, though on close inspection a ring of dark-brown iris was visible. Those eyes had seen too much in the Grey Zone — ruins, wounded, bodies, smoke after explosions, a mother's tears, a child's crying. All of it remained in those eyes, settled like sediment, never to be displaced.

"What did you learn over there?" she asked.

Daniel set down his cup. This question was harder than the others. In the civilized world, "learn" meant acquiring knowledge, mastering skills, grasping concepts. But in the Grey Zone, learning was not like that. Grey Zone learning was not knowledge — it was sensation. Not concept — experience. Not understanding — lived through. You could not pack the Grey Zone into a sentence, just as you could not pack a desert into a pocket.

"The Grey Zone taught me to stay," he said.

Sweetie looked at him, saying nothing.

"In the civilized world, we're always walking," Daniel said. "From one place to another, from one goal to the next, from one age to the next. We walk so fast we don't know what we're walking, so fast we don't know where we're going."

He paused, looking at the tea in his cup. The surface had stilled, reflecting the ceiling lamp — its reflection a small white dot, like a star suspended in green liquid.

"In the Grey Zone, you have nowhere to go," he said. "The city is surrounded. The roads are bombed out. The airport is closed. You cannot go anywhere. You can only stay where you are — in the place you were dropped. At first you are afraid. Then anxious. Then angry. Then desperate. Then you accept. And after you accept, you discover something."

"What?"

"You discover that only when you stop thinking about going somewhere can you truly see where you are. You notice the direction of the wind. You notice the angle of the sunlight. You notice the shape of cracks in the wall. You notice the changes in expression on the faces of people beside you. You notice all the things that the civilized world caused you to overlook — because the civilized world has too many things distracting your attention, too many things telling you where you should go, what you should do, what kind of person you should become."

Sweetie placed her hands on the table, fingers interlaced. Her fingers were slender, nails clipped short, a small ink stain under one nail — perhaps she had been writing.

"Do you not want to come back?" she asked. Her voice was soft, but the question was heavy.

Daniel did not answer immediately. He looked at Sweetie, at the ink stain on her fingers, at the thin silver bracelet on her wrist, at the tiny button on her collar. These were very small things — so small they were easily overlooked in the civilized world's busyness. But in the Grey Zone, such things became important, because they were evidence of "this moment," evidence of "here," evidence of "you are still alive."

"I want to come back," he said. "But I don't know how."

Sweetie was silent for a moment. Somewhere in the distance a dog barked, its cries intermittent, as though conversing with something. Wind passed through the alley, rustling the sycamore leaves — a sound like people whispering.

"You don't need to know how to come back," Sweetie said. "You're already here."

Daniel nodded. He knew she was right, but he also knew it was not that simple. He had returned to civilization, but the Grey Zone's rhythm still lived in his body. His heartbeat was the Grey Zone's heartbeat. His breathing was the Grey Zone's breathing. His silence was the Grey Zone's silence. He sat on a stone stool, drinking pale tea, talking to someone he cared about, but a part of him was still in that place of fierce wind and poor signal, still among the ruins and bullet holes, still in a place where only one thing could be done at a time.

"I want to do something," he said. "About the Grey Zone."

Sweetie looked at him. "What kind of thing?"

"I want to tell the people here about what's over there."

"How?"

"I don't know. Write articles, or give talks, or anything. I want the people here to know that in another corner of the world, there are people living entirely different lives. They have no hot water, no air conditioning, no internet, no choices — but they are alive, and they are doing things we would never do."

Sweetie said nothing, only looked at him. Something flowed in her eyes — not tears, something quieter, like an undercurrent in deep water, invisible but powerful.

"That doctor," Daniel said. "He treated patients in a school with no roof. His clinic was a plastic sheet. His surgical instruments were a pair of scissors and a box of needle and thread. Someone was hurt, he wrapped a bandage. Someone was ill, he gave medicine. He did not ask why. He only asked what now."

He paused and looked at the sky in the distance. It was blue, without clouds, clean as freshly washed cloth.

"I want to bring that back," he said. "Not as knowledge, not as a concept, not as a story, but as a possibility. To let people know that besides our way, there is another way. Another way to live, another way to see the world, another way to face difficulty."

Sweetie raised the cup to her lips and took a small sip. Her movements were slow, gentle, as though tasting something precious.

"Do you want to be the Grey Zone's mouthpiece?" she asked.

"Not a mouthpiece," Daniel said. "A mouthpiece is passive — it simply transmits sound. That's not what I want to do. I want to be a translator. To translate the Grey Zone's language into the civilized world's language. To translate the Grey Zone's wisdom into a form the civilized world can understand."

"What wisdom does the Grey Zone have?"

"Staying," Daniel said. "As I said. The Grey Zone taught me to stay. The civilized world taught me how to walk; the Grey Zone taught me how to stop. Walking is important, but stopping is important too. We are too good at walking, too skilled at it. We have walked for a thousand years, and we've arrived at this place — a place with electric lights, the internet, airplanes, mobile phones. But we cannot stop. We don't know how to stop. The moment we stop, we are afraid. We feel anxious. We feel we are wasting time. We feel we are falling behind. We feel we are being overtaken."

His voice rose slightly, then fell again. He glanced around. No one in the alley had noticed they were talking. Through a nearby window, someone was watching television, the sound very low, the words indistinguishable.

"The people in the Grey Zone cannot stop, because they cannot stop," Daniel said. "Bombs are falling, people are hurt, water is cut off, food is running out — they must act, must solve the problem. But when no bombs fall, when everything grows quiet, they stop. They sit there, doing nothing, simply sitting, watching the sky, watching the distance, watching the people beside them. That stopping is not for lack of things to do. It is for digesting, absorbing, letting experience settle."

Sweetie placed her hand on Daniel's. It was still cool, but this time there was no trembling.

"Have you settled?" she asked.

Daniel looked at her hand — her fingers resting on the back of his, the contrast between her nails and his. Hers were clean. Beneath his, sand still clung, unremovable.

"Not yet," he said. "But I know I need to stop. I don't need to go somewhere, don't need to do something, don't need to become someone. I only need to stop here, stop in this moment, and drink this tea with you."

Sweetie said nothing. She simply withdrew her hand, lifted her cup, and took a sip. Then she set the cup down, stood, walked to the sycamore tree, and looked up at the leaves. They swayed gently in the wind, sunlight filtering through the gaps to the ground below, forming irregular patches of light that shifted across the earth as the leaves moved, dancing a very slow dance.

Daniel also stood and walked to her side. They stood together without speaking, watching those patches of light move across the ground. The wind in the alley carried a blend of earth, leaves, and the distant scent of someone cooking — a complex smell, but real. Simpler than the Grey Zone's smells, but equally real.

He remembered the Grey Zone's last day. He had stood at the entrance of that abandoned school, looking at the schoolyard. The plastic sheet was still there, but nothing was on it — the bottles, bandages, scissors, needle-and-thread box all gone, as though someone had collected them or the wind had blown them away. Only a few crows walked across the yard, their black feathers glinting blue in the sunlight. The doctor was gone. The little girl was gone. The bandaged arm was gone. Everything was gone. Only the crows and the empty schoolyard remained.

He had stood there for a long time, until Sweetie came over and tapped his shoulder. She said: "Let's go." And he went. He did not look back, because he knew that some places, once you leave, you cannot return to — not because the road is broken, but because you have changed. The place has not changed, but you have, so you cannot go back.

Now he stood in this alley of the civilized world, beneath a sycamore tree, beside Sweetie. This was not the Grey Zone. There were no ruins here, no bullet holes, no bandages, no doctor, no little girl. Here there were lights, tea, trees, wind, and someone he cared about. This was where he had left, and where he had returned. But it no longer looked the way he remembered, because his eyes had changed, his ears had changed, his skin had changed, his heartbeat had changed. He saw the civilized world with Grey Zone eyes, heard its sounds with Grey Zone ears, felt its temperature with Grey Zone skin, measured its time with a Grey Zone heartbeat.

He did not know if this was good or bad, but he knew it was true. He could not go back — not back to the Grey Zone, but back to the self he had been before leaving it. He had become a new person, a person who belonged to both worlds, a person who walked in civilization but stayed in the Grey Zone.

Sweetie turned her head and looked at him. Sunlight fell through the leaves onto her face — patches of light and shadow moving across her features as the leaves swayed, like an ever-changing painting.

"What are you thinking about?" she asked.

"I'm thinking," Daniel said, "about what I should do on my first day back."

Sweetie looked at him. The corners of her mouth shifted slightly — not a smile, something more subtle, as though confirming something, or waiting for something.

"You're already doing it," she said.

Daniel looked at her — at the light and shadow on her face, at the deep brown iris in her eyes, at the thin silver bracelet on her wrist. He did not know what she meant by "already doing it," but he did not ask. Some questions do not need answers. Some answers do not need words. Some words do not need to be spoken.

They stood a while longer, until the patches of light had completely crossed the shadow beneath the sycamore. Then Sweetie reached out and gently tugged his sleeve. The gesture was so light it was almost imperceptible, but Daniel felt it.

"Let's go inside," she said. "I've brewed fresh tea. You should try it."

They walked into the three-storey building and into Sweetie's room. It was not large but very clean. On the windowsill sat a small green plant with heart-shaped leaves, vivid and bright. A map was pinned to the wall, several places circled in red — Daniel recognized one as the Grey Zone. On the desk lay several books, bookmarks bristling between their pages, each covered in dense writing. A laptop was open, its screen displaying a document titled *"War Correspondent's Notes."*

Daniel looked at the title and said nothing. Sweetie walked to the desk, closed the document, then picked up the teapot and poured two cups. The tea was freshly brewed, still steaming, the vapour rising through the air like an invisible thread.

She handed one cup to Daniel, then took the other and sat in the chair by the window. Daniel also sat, in the chair opposite. Between them stood a small tea table holding the teapot and two cups. The tea inside the cups glowed pale green in the lamplight, like two small pools of molten jade.

"When do you plan to start?" Sweetie asked.

"Start what?"

"What you just said — telling the people here about the Grey Zone."

Daniel raised his cup and let the tea's warmth drift across his face. The fragrance reached his nostrils. He closed his eyes briefly, letting the scent linger a moment longer.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe tonight."

Sweetie looked at him and did not press with "what are you doing tonight?" She simply nodded, then lowered her gaze to her own cup. The tea inside was perfectly still, reflecting the ceiling lamp — its image a tiny white dot, like a small star suspended in green liquid.

Daniel looked at that reflection and remembered that night in the Grey Zone, sitting with Sweetie on the second floor of the abandoned building, watching the distant skyline. Fire burned on the horizon, flickering in the darkness, as though something far away was breathing. They did not speak. They simply sat, watching the fire. That moment had been very quiet — so quiet they could hear each other's breathing, hear the occasional distant gunfire, hear the wind whistling through the gaps in the ruins.

That moment and this one were alike. Both were quiet. Both contained only two people. Both involved tea or something warm. But that moment had carried an urgency — the feeling that anything could be interrupted at any second, that everything might change in the next instant. This moment had no such urgency. Here there were no bombs, no gunfire, nothing to dodge. Here there was only tea, only wind, only the rustle of sycamore leaves beyond the window, only the faint gleam of Sweetie's silver bracelet reflecting the lamplight.

Daniel set down his cup and looked at Sweetie. She set down hers and looked at him. Their eyes met for several seconds without words. Then Daniel reached out and gently took her hand. It was still cool, but this time he could feel her fingers slowly warming in his palm.

Outside, the sycamore leaves still rustled. Wind passed through the alley, carrying the scent of someone cooking in a distant home. The sky was slowly darkening. The streetlights had not yet come on. The sky was that deep blue — deeper than the grey-blue of dusk, lighter than the black of night, a colour caught between two moments. Daniel looked at that colour and thought of the Grey Zone's sky. The Grey Zone's sky never had this colour. It was either a blinding blue or a pitch black — no transition, no in-between. The civilized world had many in-betweens, many transitions, many grey zones, but people noticed only the two extremes — day and night, happiness and sorrow, war and peace. They overlooked the subtle, gradual, important parts in between.

He held Sweetie's hand and felt her fingers move slightly in his, as though responding to something, or confirming something. He thought: the Grey Zone taught him to stay, but Sweetie had taught him something else — how to feel while staying. Not feeling with the eyes, not with the ears, not with the skin, but with the heart. Feeling the temperature of this moment with the heart. Feeling the quiet of this moment with the heart. Feeling the warmth of another person's palm with the heart.

He did not know what tomorrow would bring, did not know if he could tell the people here about the Grey Zone, did not know if they would listen, did not know if they would understand. But in this moment, he only wanted to stop here — stop in this moment, stop between this cup of tea and this hand, stop in this transitional instant between the Grey Zone and civilization.

He closed his eyes and let the moment linger a little longer. The wind still blew outside. The sycamore leaves still rustled. Sweetie's hand still warmed slowly in his. This was staying. This was what the Grey Zone had taught him. Not knowledge, not concept, not theory — simply stopping here, feeling this moment, doing nothing else, thinking of nothing else, just stopping.

He opened his eyes and saw Sweetie looking at him. In her eyes was that something he had seen in the Grey Zone — something more complex than fear, sorrow, or hope. Now he knew what it was. It was "this moment." It was "here." It was "you and me." It was everything that cannot be captured by language but can be captured by feeling.

He said nothing. He simply held her hand and sat with her in this quiet, tea-scented room, waiting for the sky to darken completely, waiting for the streetlights to come on, waiting for this night to unfold.

He thought: this is coming home. Not returning to a place, but returning to a moment. Not returning to a room, but returning to a feeling. Not returning to the past, but returning to this moment. The Grey Zone taught him to stay. Sweetie taught him to feel. The civilized world gave him a place where he could both stay and feel. He did not know if this was enough. But he knew that in this moment, it was.
