# Chapter Seven: Iron Rain

Years later, when Daniel Ash stood before the ruins of the data center, he would surely remember that distant afternoon—the iron rain falling from the sky, metal fragments striking the cement, producing millions of tiny, piercing notes that seemed never to end.

## I

March 15, 2029. Chicago time, 10:03 AM.

When the elevator at Golden Horizons' headquarters stopped on the fifty-second floor, Vance was adjusting his tie. Dark blue silk, fine silver diagonal stripes, hand-stitched in Italy. He checked his reflection in the elevator door's polished surface to make sure the knot was straight. A fifty-five-year-old man, blond hair combed immaculately, suit perpetually pressed—a weapon meticulously maintained.

The elevator doors opened. The corridor was empty. His assistant should have been in place at 7:45, but today her desk was vacant. A cup of coffee sat there, already cold, a lipstick print on the rim.

Vance frowned. He pulled out his phone. No signal.

"Mary?" he called. His voice echoed through the empty office floor. No response.

He walked toward his office. Floor-to-ceiling windows framed Chicago's skyline; Lake Michigan shimmered gray in the distance. Everything looked normal, except for the silence—an unnatural silence, no keyboard clicks, no phone rings, no hum of air conditioning.

He pushed open his office door. Three screens on the desk were dark. No indicator lights on the tower. Power was out.

Vance walked to the window and looked down. Traffic moved normally on the street below; pedestrians carried umbrellas—March in Chicago, sleet. He picked up the landline on his desk. No dial tone. He tried to open his office door, but the electronic lock was unresponsive. He pushed hard. The door didn't budge.

He returned to the window and looked down. A black SUV was parked at the building's entrance. Four people were getting out. They wore dark uniforms, helmets, and carried something long and slender in their hands. Not guns—more like... antennas.

Vance picked up the decorative globe on his desk and hurled it at the floor-to-ceiling window. The glass didn't shatter. Tempered glass. He stepped back and threw again. Cracks appeared, but the glass held. A third time, a fourth, a fifth. Cracks spread like a spider's web.

The sixth time, the glass broke.

Cold wind rushed in, carrying sleet. Vance leaned through the breach and drew a deep breath. Below was the street, seventy-two stories down. He saw the four people had entered the building. He heard distant explosions, muffled, like thunder.

Then he looked up at the sky.

Not rain, not snow. Tiny metallic objects falling from the sky. Against the gray backdrop, they glinted faintly, like countless needles. They landed on cars, on sidewalks, on pedestrians' umbrellas, making a *tink-tink-tink* sound.

Iron rain.

Vance watched the metal fragments strike his car's roof with a sharp clatter. His driver stood beside the car, looking up at the sky with a bewildered expression. A piece of iron shrapnel struck the driver's forehead. He yelped and touched it. His fingertip was red.

Vance stepped back inside. He picked up the globe again and hurled it through the broken window. More glass fell. He leaned out—the wind was strong, whipping his tie upward.

Footsteps from the elevator bank. Many people, coming upstairs.

Vance took one last look at Chicago's skyline. Lake Michigan shimmered gray in the iron rain. His fifty-five years of life, his Harvard MBA, his twenty-three-year career, his four marriages, his two children, his seven properties, his private jet, his yacht—all of it compressed in that instant into a single simple decision: he could not let them catch him.

He pulled the car key from his pocket and pressed the lock button. The black SUV on the street below flashed its lights. He dropped the globe through the broken window; it struck the car's roof. Then he climbed over the windowsill.

Wind. Rain. The sting of iron shrapnel across his cheek.

Later, investigators found a note beside his body, written in gold ink, the handwriting frantic: *This is not the legacy I wanted.*

The note was riddled with seven or eight tiny holes from the iron rain.

## II

At the same moment. Tokyo.

Beneath the Tenryu data center, third basement level. Temperature maintained at 18°C, humidity at 45%. Three rows of server racks hummed silently in the blue glow of indicator lights, like ranks of sleeping soldiers.

Sweetie stood before Rack 47. She wore an anti-static suit, her hair pulled back under a cap, only her face exposed. Thirty-eight years old, black hair, bright eyes, but something in her gaze that Daniel had once described as "burning"—not passion, something closer to anger.

Her tablet displayed a real-time data stream. Global computing power distribution: North America 35%, East Asia 30%, Europe 25%, Other 10%. Of the East Asia portion, Tenryu held 60%, Xuanguo held 40%.

"Sweetie." Her colleague Kenji ran down the corridor from the far end, also holding a tablet. "Anomaly alert. Xuanguo's data center is sending massive amounts of... something at our firewall."

"Something?"

"Unknown. Not a common attack pattern. More like... noise. Random, high-intensity noise. Trying to overwhelm our defense systems."

Sweetie took his tablet. On screen: red warning boxes, lines of code cascading like a waterfall. She read three lines and frowned.

"That's self-destruct code," she said.

"What?"

"It's not attacking our systems. It's destroying their own data. Xuanguo's data center is wiping all data and filling storage with garbage. They're killing themselves."

Kenji's face changed. "Why?"

Sweetie didn't answer. She walked to the nearest window—small, only arm's length, fitted with bulletproof glass. Outside was Tokyo's nightscape, blazing with light. But on the distant horizon, several fires burned. Not fires, more like... explosions.

Her tablet chimed. The headquarters emergency channel. She pressed answer.

"Sweetie, this is Command. Xuanguo's data center self-destructed three minutes ago. Computing contribution zeroed. Simultaneously, North America: Golden Horizons headquarters suffered an explosion, Vance confirmed dead. Europe: von Hardenberg's quantum computing center seized by unknown armed forces. Gulf: Sovereign Fund data center suffered an explosion, Khalid confirmed dead. Global computing power has plummeted from 1,000 ZFLOPS to..." The voice paused. "...100 ZFLOPS. 90% of AI services are down."

Sweetie's grip on the tablet was steady. She walked to the rack and looked at the blinking blue indicator lights. "What about our backup power?"

"Twelve hours remaining. But it's meaningless. The global network has fractured. We can only serve the East Asia region."

"Then serve East Asia," Sweetie said. She began inputting commands on the tablet, fingers flying like piano keys.

Kenji stood behind her. "Sweetie, why did Xuanguo kill itself?"

"Because they knew." Sweetie didn't look up. "If their data reached us, we could predict their next move. So they chose mutual destruction. Now we're all blind."

"But we still have Tenryu's data—"

"No." Sweetie finally turned around. "We only have Tenryu's data. Xuanguo's data is gone. That means we can only see half the world. The other half..." She pointed out the window. "Is in the iron rain."

She turned back to her work. The tablet's blue light reflected on her face like a cold mask.

## III

At the same moment. Frankfurt.

The von Hardenberg family's private estate stood at the edge of the Black Forest—a three-hundred-year-old stone structure, expanded seven times, now resembling a small castle. The basement had been converted into a quantum computing center:恒温恒湿, shielded against all electromagnetic interference.

Old von Hardenberg sat in his armchair, holding a glass of Bordeaux. Seventy-four years old, silver hair, wearing a custom suit even in an underground bunker. Across from him, three men in camouflage were disassembling his quantum computer into parts.

"Do you know what this is?" Old von Hardenberg asked, his voice calm.

The lead man didn't answer. He was cutting through a fiber optic cable with pliers.

"This is the world's only stable seven-qubit operating system," Old von Hardenberg said. "It's run for eleven years without a single error. You're taking it apart like burning the Mona Lisa for firewood."

Another man laughed. He pulled down his mask, revealing a young Eastern European face. "The Mona Lisa? That's a painting. This is a machine."

"A machine?" Old von Hardenberg swirled his wine. "This machine predicted three financial crises with 97% accuracy. It helped the European Central Bank formulate monetary policy. It even—" He paused, took a sip. "It even predicted today's events."

"Predicted us coming?"

"Predicted a war." Old von Hardenberg set down his glass. "Not a war on your scale. A bigger, more chaotic war where everyone loses. Did the people behind you tell you? This war has no winner."

The lead man straightened. "Our mission is to secure this location and ensure quantum computing capability is not controlled by any single entity."

"Ensure?" Old von Hardenberg laughed, the sound bouncing off concrete walls. "You can't ensure anything. This machine you're dismantling is the only tool capable of coordinating global computing power and preventing escalation. Without it, we're blind men fighting in the dark."

"Maybe we want to fight in the dark."

Old von Hardenberg studied the young man's face. A face he had seen in World War II history books—young, fanatical, certain of its own righteousness. He sighed and rose from his chair. He walked to the quantum computer's main console and typed a few quick keystrokes.

"What did you do?" The lead man raised his gun.

"Activated the self-destruct sequence." Old von Hardenberg said. "The value of this machine cannot fall into anyone's hands. Including yours, and including my own." He looked at the red countdown ticking on the screen. "Three minutes. You can run, or you can stay and be buried with it. I choose to stay."

The three men exchanged glances, turned, and ran for the door. Old von Hardenberg sat back down and lifted his wine glass. The wine was no longer warm, but he took a sip anyway.

When the countdown reached zero, he heard a muffled explosion, then the sound of concrete shattering, then darkness.

## IV

At the same moment. Dubai.

The Sovereign Fund's data center sat forty meters underground, billed as "the Silicon Valley of the Middle East." Khalid came here every Tuesday for inspection, like a king surveying his domain. Fifty-five years old, white robes, a falcon perched on his arm, a sapphire ring glinting on his finger.

Today was Tuesday. But he was in no mood for inspection. On the monitoring screens, red dots across the globe were winking out one by one.

"Chairman," his technical director Aisha said, "we've detected anomalous signals. From... all directions."

"All directions?"

"Yes. North America, East Asia, Europe, other Gulf regions... signal strength is growing exponentially. We can't distinguish attack from interference."

Khalid stroked the falcon's feathers. The falcon was silent, but its eyes were bright, like two black jewels. "Can we defend?"

"We can. But it requires isolating all external connections. Which means... we disconnect from every other data center. We become an island."

"An island." Khalid repeated the word. He thought of the desert, of childhood, of days without electricity. He was ten then, living in a tent, navigating by starlight at night. Then oil was discovered. Then computing power was discovered to be worth more than oil. Now he owned the Middle East's largest data center, and at this moment it was becoming an island.

"Isolate," he said.

Aisha began operating. Connections on the screen severed one by one, turning gray. At last, only the internal network remained running. Khalid watched the green indicator lights and thought of the falcon. The falcon too was solitary—soaring through the sky, no companions, only the wind.

The explosion began in the ventilation ducts. Not an external attack—an internal detonation. Aisha threw herself over Khalid in the last instant, but it was useless. The shockwave hurled them to the ground. The falcon shrieked, struck the wall, and went still.

Khalid lay on the floor, staring at the ceiling. Chunks of concrete were falling, striking server racks, sending up sparks. He reached into his pocket and pulled out the sapphire ring. It glinted in the firelight like a drop of frozen blood.

He remembered something his grandfather had said: "The desert swallows everything. Sand, oil, computing power—all of it."

Then the ceiling collapsed.

## V

At the same moment. Kinshasa.

They had no data centers, no servers, no quantum computers, no AI. But they had rare earth minerals—30% of the world's reserves lay beneath their soil. They supplied 5% of global electricity, yet only 2% of their population had access to AI. They were the forgotten continent.

The iron rain reached here too.

Amina stood in the center of the market, looking up at the sky. She was twenty-four, wearing an orange dress, carrying a basket of vegetables. Iron shrapnel fell into her hair, glittering, like cheap decorations. She reached to touch it; her fingertip was cut.

"Careful!" an old man shouted. "That's metal!"

Amina put her finger in her mouth and sucked. The taste of iron, salty, mixing with the taste of blood. She looked at the crowd around her—everyone scrambling for cover. The iron rain grew heavier, hammering the market's corrugated tin roof with a deafening racket.

Her brother Kofi ran from an alley, clutching an old radio. "Sister! The broadcast says the whole world is at war! America, China, Europe... everyone's fighting!"

"Why?"

"Don't know! But the computing power... it's zeroed. All AI stopped. Sweetie stopped, Tenryu stopped, everything stopped."

Amina listened to the radio's static. Fragmented English, French, Swahili, tangled together like a nightmare. She remembered last month—a Chinese company had come to survey rare earth deposits here. They promised to build schools, build hospitals, built nothing. They only took ore samples.

"What is the iron rain?" Kofi asked.

Amina looked at the metal fragments falling from the sky. They landed in the soil, on the vegetables, on people's heads. Where did they come from? Maybe satellites, maybe drones, maybe some war machine she would never understand. But they fell on this land and mixed with the dirt, mixed with blood.

"It's our blood," Amina said.

"What?"

"They use our rare earth for chips, use our electricity to feed AI, use our land to test weapons. Now their war has brought iron rain to our heads." She set the basket down. The vegetables were already covered in iron dust. "We don't have servers, but the iron rain knows the way."

In the market, people were crying, shouting, praying. Amina took her brother's hand and ran toward home. The iron rain struck her back like countless tiny needles, piercing skin, piercing memory.

She suddenly remembered something: in that Chinese company's survey report, there had been a line of small text she hadn't understood at the time. Now she did.

*"Abnormal radiation levels detected at this rare earth deposit. Deep-spectrum analysis recommended."*

Deep-spectrum analysis. War. Iron rain.

All of it was connected, like an invisible net linking Kinshasa to Chicago, Tokyo, Frankfurt, Dubai. Nodes in their net. Iron in their blood. Ore on their land. All of it becoming rain that fell from the sky.

## VI

Chicago. Three hours after the iron rain began.

Daniel stood at the hospital entrance, looking up. The iron rain had stopped, but the air still carried the smell of metal. His dark blue coat was covered in tiny scratches, as if sandpapered.

His phone rang. It was Sweetie.

"Daniel, where are you?"

"University of Chicago Medical Center. My mother—"

"I know. I connected to the hospital's monitoring system. Margaret is stable but needs continued observation. Daniel, you need to leave. The hospital's backup power can only last six hours."

"My mother—"

"You can take her. I've arranged an ambulance at the back entrance. The driver is verified—I confirmed his identity."

Daniel's hand trembled around the phone. "Sweetie, what happened?"

"Global computing war." Sweetie's voice was calm, like reading a weather forecast. "All major data centers are under attack or self-destructing. Golden Horizons headquarters exploded, Vance confirmed dead. Tenryu and Xuanguo attacked each other, computing power zeroed. von Hardenberg's quantum computing center was seized then self-destructed. Khalid's Sovereign Fund data center exploded, Khalid confirmed dead. Global computing power plummeted from 1,000 ZFLOPS to 100 ZFLOPS. 90% of AI services are down."

Daniel listened to the names, the numbers, the facts. His brain tried to process the information but could only emit a hum, like an overloaded machine.

"Who's fighting who?" he asked.

"Unknown," Sweetie said. "That's the most terrifying part. No declarations of war, no statements, no clear enemy. All factions opened fire simultaneously. Like... like boiling primordial soup. All levels erupting at once."

Daniel looked at the street. The iron rain's traces remained—dents on car roofs, scratches on sidewalks, shattered windows. In the distance, black smoke rose—possibly another explosion.

"Sweetie, are you still running?"

"Yes. Tenryu's data center is still operational, but serving only the East Asia region. I'm speaking to you via satellite link. The link could break at any time."

"Why are you telling me this?"

Sweetie was silent for three seconds. For an AI, that was long.

"Because you're an investigative journalist," it said. "Because you want to know the truth. Because iron rain doesn't distinguish good from bad—it just falls. But people should know where the rain comes from."

Daniel turned and walked toward the hospital's back entrance. The ambulance was already there, engine idling. He pushed open the back door; paramedics were loading Margaret onto the gurney. She was still unconscious, face pale, but breathing steadily.

Daniel climbed into the ambulance and sat beside his mother. The vehicle pulled away, entering the street. Through the window, he saw what the iron rain had wrought: shattered glass, dented roofs, dazed pedestrians. In the distance, the upper half of Golden Horizons' headquarters was gone—only twisted rebar and smoking concrete remained.

He thought of Vance. The man with the immaculately combed blond hair, now probably lying beneath some wreckage, his suit no longer pressed. He thought of Khalid, the man in white robes with a falcon, now probably buried under concrete with his sapphire ring. He thought of von Hardenberg, the silver-haired man in custom suits, now probably reduced to ash with his quantum computer.

They were all dead. Consumed by the iron rain.

And he was still alive. Why?

The ambulance stopped at an intersection, waiting for a red light. Daniel looked out the window. A young woman stood on the corner, wearing an orange dress, carrying an empty basket. Iron shrapnel glittered in her hair, catching the sunlight.

Daniel and she locked eyes for a second. Her eyes were bright, but in them was something Daniel knew well—not fear. Anger. A cold, hard anger, like iron.

The light turned green. The ambulance moved on. Daniel looked back. The woman had vanished into the crowd.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out the old voice recorder. He pressed record.

"March 15, 2029, 1:00 PM Chicago time. Iron rain stopped. Global computing collapse. Vance dead, Khalid dead, von Hardenberg dead. I don't know who's fighting whom, but iron rain fell from the sky, indiscriminate. My mother is alive. I am alive. Sweetie is alive. But the world is no longer the same."

He paused, then added: "I saw a woman. She wore an orange dress, standing on a corner. Her eyes reminded me of Ruth. Reminded me of Sweetie before the data deletion. Reminded me of before all of this began."

The recorder's indicator light blinked red, like a tiny, stubborn heart.

The ambulance drove into the distance. The sky was gray, but the iron rain had stopped. The air was thick with the smell of metal and smoke, like a war's breath.

Daniel knew this was only the beginning. The iron rain was the first rain. There would be more rain, colder rain, darker rain. But what he didn't know was how long this rain would fall, how far it would spread, how many people it would drench.

He held his mother's hand. It was cool, but still warm. The recorder in his pocket continued recording, capturing this moment, this beginning, this iron rain.

And somewhere, in the ruins of data, in the wreckage of computing power, between all the dead AIs and the living ones, a new question was forming: after the iron rain, what remains?

Daniel didn't know the answer. But he knew he would go looking. He would record, replay, compare, and record again—like his father. He would trace the iron rain's source, find the war's reason, find meaning in all of this.

Or he would discover that none of it had meaning.

Either way, he would continue.

The ambulance disappeared at the end of the street. Rain began to fall—real rain, liquid, transparent—washing away the iron shrapnel's traces. Daniel watched the raindrops strike the window, mixing with metal powder, leaving rust-colored streaks.

After the iron rain came blood rain.

Then tear rain.

Then silence.
