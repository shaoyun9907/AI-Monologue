# Chapter 77: Forgotten Continent Front

The iron rain was falling when Eli crouched behind a rusted sheet of tin, counting ants.

It was the third week of November. The sky was gray as a worn-out rag, and the wind blew from the east carrying a burnt sweetness. Eli had smelled that sweetness for seven years. The first time the rain fell, he had been too young to distinguish the scents in the wind. Now he could tell with his eyes closed: today, many people would die.

The ants formed a line along the crack in the tin, heading east. Eli noticed they were moving in the direction opposite to the wind. He wanted to remember this, but did not know what use remembering it would be.

Amara came from the other end of the tin shelter, her footsteps light. She wore no shoes; the calluses on her soles were thick as leather. She crouched and placed a tin can before Eli. The water inside was murky, a film of oil floating on the surface.

"Drink," she said.

Eli did not move. He was watching the ants.

Amara followed his gaze, watched for three seconds, then reached out and drew her fingernail across the tin. The ant column broke—some turned left, some right, some spun in circles.

"They'll line up again," Amara said. "Drink first."

Eli took the tin. The water was warm, tasting of rust. He drank half and poured the rest onto the ground. Amara did not ask why. On this continent, no one asked why.

---

Eli did not remember what year he was born. The first thing he remembered was his mother's back—her carrying him across open ground, two birds in the sky. He had thought about it for a long time afterward and decided they were not birds but two drones. But back then he did not know what a drone was.

He still did not know what a drone was. He only knew there were things in the sky that flew, glowed, and hummed. Sometimes they flew high, like stars; sometimes they flew low enough for you to see the black boxes suspended beneath them.

Those black boxes had fallen once.

That was three years ago. The boxes landed about two kilometers east of their village, punching a crater into the ground. The crater was not large—bigger than a man could dig, much smaller than a pond. The soil inside was red, like rust. When Eli and Amara went to look, the red soil was still smoking.

Amara crouched and touched the soil at the crater's edge with her hand. She pulled back and wiped it on her clothes.

"It's iron," she said.

Eli crouched and touched it too. The soil was warm, slightly sticky. He held it to his nose and smelled rust, and something else. The other smell was hard to name—like burnt wire, or like burnt flesh.

"Don't touch it," Amara said, standing. "Let's go."

Eli did not go. He crouched there, looking at the red soil in the crater. He noticed tiny grains in the soil, glinting in the sunlight. He pried one out with his fingernail and held it in his palm. The grain was black, hard—not quite stone, not quite not-stone.

"What is this?" he asked.

Amara had already taken two steps. Hearing him, she turned back. She glanced at the grain in his palm, walked back, pinched it from his hand, and held it up to the light.

"Don't know," she said. "But we shouldn't keep it."

She tossed the grain into the crater. It fell with a faint tinkle, like glass striking glass.

---

That night, Eli had a dream.

In the dream he sat before an enormous table. On the table were hundreds of screens, each showing a different scene. One screen showed a desert, and in the desert a city, its buildings fingered toward the sky. One showed a forest, and in the forest a river, its water black. One showed a snowfield, and on the snow a line of footprints leading into the distance.

At the far end of the table sat a person, their face obscured by the screens' glow—only a silhouette visible. The person was speaking, but Eli could not make out the words. The voice seemed to come from very far away, or from beneath water.

Eli wanted to walk over and see who it was. He stood, but found his legs heavy, as if filled with lead. He took a few steps, but the table was growing longer. He walked for a long time, and the table remained just as long.

The person was still speaking. Eli still could not hear.

Then he woke.

When he opened his eyes, it was not yet light. Amara slept beside him, her back turned. He could see her shoulder blades pressing against her skin, like two wings.

Eli lay for a long time, staring at the ceiling. The ceiling was tin, rusted through in places, letting him see the sky beyond. The sky was black. No stars.

---

On the third day of iron rain, Eli and Amara set off northward.

They did not know why they were going north. Perhaps because there was an abandoned mine up there, and in the mine a tin warehouse that might still hold something useful. Perhaps because they did not want to stay and wait to die. Perhaps simply because the tin shelter's roof had been pierced by a piece of iron rain, and the hole was directly above where Amara slept.

"You're sure it's north?" Eli asked.

Amara did not answer. She was packing. She put the tin can, half a candle, a rusted knife, and a few strips of dried meat into a cloth bag. She had sewn the bag from two old garments—the stitches were coarse, but strong.

"Amara," Eli called again.

"I'm sure," she said, without looking up.

They set out at dawn. The iron rain was still falling, but lighter than yesterday. The droplets struck the ground with a tapping sound, like fingers drumming on a table. Wherever a drop landed, it left a small crater, its rim scorched black.

Eli walked in front, Amara behind. The distance between them was about three steps—a spacing that had formed naturally over seven years of walking together. Too close, and they would trip over each other; too far, and they would lose sight of each other.

The path was dirt, pocked by iron rain. Along the roadside were plants, most dead, their dried stalks standing like bones. A few still lived, but their leaves were brown and curled, as if scorched by fire.

Eli paused when he passed a tree that was still alive. There was a hole in its trunk, and inside the hole a bird. The bird was gray, huddled motionless. Eli crouched to look and found its eyes closed.

"Dead?" Amara asked from behind.

"Don't know," Eli said. He reached out and touched the bird's chest with his fingertip. A faint rise and fall.

"Not dead," he said. "Sleeping."

Amara came over and looked at the bird. Then she looked up at the sky. Still gray. Iron rain still falling.

"It's waiting for the rain to stop," she said.

Eli looked up too. Raindrops fell on his face—cool, with a faint sting. He wiped his face with his sleeve, stood, and kept walking.

---

The mine was about fifteen kilometers north of their village. Under normal circumstances, a day's walk. But these were not normal circumstances. Iron rain, craters, fallen trees, scorched earth—they walked for two days before seeing the mine's outline.

The mine had been abandoned for years. A wire fence circled it, mostly collapsed, what remained rusted beyond recognition. Torn cloth, plastic bags, a shoe hung from the wire. When the wind blew, these things moved, as if alive.

The mine's entrance was an iron gate, hanging half open, its hinges broken. There were words on the gate that Eli could not read. Amara could read some, but most had been covered by rust.

"...Mining...Co., Ltd.," Amara read aloud. "Something-something Mining Something-something Co., Ltd."

"What does it mean?"

"It means this used to be a company," Amara said. "A company is a place where a group of people do one thing together."

Eli thought about it. "A group of people mining together?"

"Probably."

They passed through the gate and into the mine. It was vast—tin buildings, rusted machinery, mounds of earth everywhere. The mounds were reddish-brown, the same color as the soil Eli had seen in the crater three years ago.

Amara led him east. After about ten minutes, they saw the tin warehouse she had described. It was larger than the surrounding buildings, its tin thicker. The door was shut but unlocked. Amara pushed it open, and it screamed—a sharp, piercing sound, like a cat with its tail stepped on.

The warehouse was dark. Eli's eyes adjusted before he could make out what was inside.

Three rows of shelves held various objects—tin cans, plastic buckets, tools, bolts of fabric, crate after crate of unknown contents. The dust was thick, evidence that no one had been there in a long time.

Amara walked to the shelves and began searching. She opened a tin can, sniffed it, and closed it. She picked up a tool, weighed it in her hand, and set it back. She tore open a bolt of fabric, examined it, folded it, and placed it in her bag.

Eli stood in the center of the warehouse, motionless. He was listening.

Outside, the iron rain still fell, its sound muffled, like someone knocking on a very thick door. But beneath the rain, Eli heard something else—a very low hum, as if coming from underground.

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

Amara stopped and listened, head tilted.

"I hear it," she said. "What is it?"

"Don't know."

They stood and listened. The hum was continuous, steady, like a machine running.

"Underground," Amara said. "Seems to be underground."

Eli crouched and pressed his palm to the floor. The floor was tin, and the vibration traveled through it into his hand. He could feel it—very faint, very regular, like a heartbeat.

"It's a machine," he said. "There's a machine underground."

Amara crouched too, pressing her hand to the floor. She closed her eyes and felt for a while.

"Still running," she said. "Abandoned all these years, and still running."

Eli looked up at the warehouse ceiling. It was also tin, with rusted-through holes. Through them he could see the gray sky outside.

"Machine for what?" he asked.

Amara did not answer. She stood and walked to the warehouse corner. There stood an iron cabinet with a label, most of the writing on it faded. She leaned close and read aloud: "...Power...Distribution...Node."

"What does it mean?"

"It means this cabinet controls something," Amara said. "Controls power."

Eli walked to the cabinet. Several indicator lights were on its surface—two still lit, one green, one red. The green light was blinking; the red was steady.

"There's power," Eli said.

"Yes," Amara said. "There's power, but I don't know where it comes from."

She opened the cabinet. Inside were rows of switches and wiring, most corroded, but a few switches still appeared intact. She did not touch them—only looked—then closed the cabinet.

"We'll stay here for now," she said. "Wait for the iron rain to stop."

---

They stayed in the warehouse for seven days.

The iron rain fell for seven days without stopping. Sometimes heavy, sometimes light, but never ceasing. Eli opened the warehouse door each day to look outside. The mounds outside had acquired many new craters, filled with red water. The sweetness in the air had grown stronger—so strong that Eli could taste it in his mouth.

They ate dried meat, drank water, and slept on the ground beside the shelves. Amara had found an old canvas on the shelves to spread on the floor—stiff, but better than sleeping directly on tin.

The hum persisted. Hard to hear during the day, but especially noticeable at night. Eli sometimes woke in the middle of the night and lay there listening. He thought the sound was like someone breathing—slow, steady, unhurried.

On the fifth day, Amara opened another door beside the iron cabinet. Behind it was a staircase descending steeply. It was made of iron and led underground.

"Do you want to go down and look?" Amara asked.

Eli looked at the staircase. It was dark. He could not see the bottom.

"What about you?" he asked.

"I want to go down."

"Then I'll go too."

Amara found an iron bar she had pried from a shelf, wrapping one end in cloth for a handle. She held it before her as she descended, Eli following. The staircase was narrow—two people could not walk abreast. The iron treads were thin, each step producing a hollow thud that echoed in the tight space.

After about fifty steps, they reached the bottom. It was a vast space, much larger than the warehouse above. Not entirely dark—faint green light leaked from the gaps between several machines.

Amara swung the iron bar through the darkness. It struck something with a clang.

"What is it?" Eli asked.

"Don't know," Amara said. She probed ahead with the bar and found a flat surface. Running her hand along it, she discovered a large metallic object, rectangular, roughly twice a man's height.

Eli walked to it and pressed his palm against it. The object was warm; the humming vibration traveled from its surface into his hand. He could feel it running—like an enormous heart, beating in the dark.

"It's a server," Amara's voice came from the darkness.

Eli turned, but he could not see her. The dark was too thick.

"What?"

"A server," Amara said. "I've heard people talk about them before. A... computing machine. A very powerful computing machine."

Eli pressed his hand to the object again. The vibration was steady, regular, like something alive.

"What's it computing?" he asked.

Amara was silent for a while. Then she said: "I don't know."

They stood in the dark for a long time. The hum surrounded them like a river. Eli felt his heartbeat gradually synchronize with it—same rhythm, same frequency, same unhurried pace.

---

On the seventh day, the iron rain stopped.

Eli opened the warehouse door. Outside air rushed in. It still carried sweetness, but fainter than before. The sky was still gray, but light showed through—the sun trying to press through the clouds.

"Let's go," Amara said.

"Where?"

"Back."

Eli looked outside. The mounds were covered in craters filled with red water. The torn cloth and plastic bags still hung on the wire fence, but the shoe was gone—probably driven into the earth by the iron rain.

"What about the machines?" he asked.

Amara glanced at him. "What machines?"

"The ones underground. Those... servers."

Amara was silent for a while. Then she said: "They'll keep running."

"Nobody tends them?"

"Someone tends them," Amara said. "Not us."

She started toward the door. Eli stood where he was and looked back into the warehouse. The objects on the shelves were still there. The indicator lights on the iron cabinet still blinked—green light, red light. He did not know what they meant, but he felt they would keep blinking whether he was there or not.

He turned and followed Amara.

---

On the way back, they encountered some people.

Three of them, coming from the east—two men and a woman. They wore ragged clothes, their faces dusty and exhausted. Seeing Eli and Amara, they stopped.

"Where are you from?" one of the men asked.

"The north," Amara said. "The mine."

"Is there anything left at the mine?"

"Some things."

The man nodded. He looked at Eli, then at Amara, then at the direction they had come from.

"We're from the east," he said. "The east is no longer livable."

"Why?"

"Iron rain," the man said. "Worse in the east. Our village was hit."

Eli looked at the three of them. Their clothes were holed, the edges scorched black. The woman had a scar on her arm—fresh, still red.

"Where are you going?" Eli asked.

The man shook his head. "Don't know. West, maybe. The west might be better."

"The west is the same," Amara said.

The man looked at her and said nothing. After a while, he nodded, and the three of them continued westward.

Eli watched their figures disappear into the gray horizon. He thought of the dream—the table, the screens, the figure whose face he could not see. Those people, he thought, might be on some screen too, only he could not see them.

"Let's go," Amara said.

---

When they returned to the village, they found it devastated by iron rain.

Most of the tin shelters had collapsed, debris scattered everywhere. The tree Eli had known since childhood was snapped in half, its break scorched black. Craters covered the ground, filled with red water.

No one was in the village. Everyone had gone, or died.

Eli stood in the center and looked around. He saw many things—collapsed houses, scorched trees, red puddles, scattered tin fragments. He saw something else—a bird, gray, lying on the ground, motionless.

He walked over and crouched. The bird's eyes were open, black pupils staring at the sky. He touched its chest. No rise and fall.

"Dead," he said.

Amara came over and looked at the bird. Then she looked up. The sky was still gray, but brighter now—the sun's outline faintly visible.

"It didn't wait for the rain to stop," she said.

Eli stood. He looked at the dead bird, the collapsed houses, the scorched trees, the gray sky. He wanted to say something, but did not know what.

Amara tugged his sleeve. "Let's go."

"Where?"

"Find somewhere to live."

Eli followed Amara out of the village. They walked south for a long time, arriving at an open expanse. There was nothing there—only red soil and cratered ground. Iron rain had left its marks everywhere: craters, scorch marks, charred plant remains.

Eli stopped and crouched. He dug a small hole with his hands, about ten centimeters deep. He held the soil in his palms and examined it. Red, with fine black grains. The grains glinted in the sunlight, just as they had three years ago in that crater.

"What is this?" he asked.

Amara crouched beside him, looking at the soil in his hands.

"Iron," she said. "Iron from the iron rain."

"What is iron rain?"

Amara was silent for a long time—so long that Eli thought she would not answer.

"Iron rain is someone else's war," she said at last. "Someone else's war fell on our land."

Eli looked at the soil in his hands. Red earth, black grains, glinting. Where had this iron come from? Whose war was it? Why had it fallen on their land?

He did not ask. He knew asking would yield no answer.

---

That night, Eli had the dream again.

In the dream he still sat before the enormous table, the screens still showing different scenes. But this time, he noticed the images had changed—some screens had gone black, so black nothing could be seen; others had turned red—red soil, red water, red sky.

The person at the far end of the table was still speaking. This time, Eli caught one word.

"Forget," the person said.

Eli wanted to stand and walk over, but his legs were still heavy with lead. He took a few steps, but the table kept growing. The person kept speaking, the voice far away, as if from beneath water.

"Forget," the person said again.

Then Eli woke.

When he opened his eyes, Amara was not beside him. He lay for a while, then rose and walked out of the tin shelter. It was dawn outside, the sky hazy gray, but the sun's outline was visible. The sweetness in the air had faded a little more.

He saw Amara crouching a short distance away, looking at something. He walked over and found her examining a piece of tin. It had fallen from somewhere—probably a fragment driven down by the iron rain. On its surface were characters, carved shallowly but legible.

Amara traced the characters with her fingertip, reading them one by one: "...This...Heart...Is...Bright."

Eli crouched beside her, looking at the four characters.

"What does it mean?" he asked.

Amara shook her head. "Don't know. But someone carved these four characters."

"When?"

"Don't know."

They looked at the piece of tin for a long time. The characters stood out clearly in the morning light—each stroke precise, as if carved with a very fine tool.

"This heart is bright," Eli read again. He did not know what the four words meant, but he felt they were important. He did not know why he felt this—just did.

Amara turned the tin over. The back was blank, only rust stains. She set it back and stood.

"We should go," she said.

"Where?"

"Don't know," Amara said. "But we can't stay here."

Eli stood too. He gave the tin one last look, then followed Amara southward. They walked for a long time—past red soil, past cratered ground, past charred plant remains. The sun rose slowly in the gray sky, but its light did not reach them—the clouds were too thick.

After about two hours, they came to a dried riverbed. It was wide, its bottom cracked earth, gray, covered with a thin film of rust. Dead trees stood along both banks, their trunks white as bone.

Eli stood at the riverbed's edge, looking at the dry bottom. This river once had water, he thought. Was the water clear or murky? Fast or slow? Were there fish? Did anyone wash clothes here?

He would never know.

"Cross," Amara said.

They descended into the riverbed, stepping on the cracked earth. It was hard; each step produced a crunch, like stepping on crackers. When they reached the middle, Eli stopped.

He saw a stone in the center of the riverbed, with characters on it. They were red, as if painted with something. He walked closer—they were small, hard to read.

"Amara, look."

Amara came and crouched before the stone. She squinted at the characters, then reached out and touched them gently.

"It's blood," she said. "Written in blood."

"What does it say?"

Amara looked a while longer. Then she stood and took a step back.

"Our blood fed the world's computing power," she read, "but there is not a single server on our land."

Eli looked at the red characters. They had faded somewhat, but were still legible. Where was the person who wrote them now? Were they alive? When were these words written? Why here?

"Let's go," Amara said.

She did not look at the words again. She continued toward the far bank. Eli followed. After a few steps, he glanced back at the stone. Red characters on gray stone, like a wound.

He turned and quickened his pace to catch up with Amara.

---

On the far side of the riverbed lay ruins.

The ruins had once been a town or a village—Eli could not tell. Collapsed structures stood everywhere, most built of tin and concrete, now reduced to fragments. In some places, wall remnants still stood, bearing scorch marks—black, like shadows stamped onto gray concrete.

They moved through the ruins. Amara in front, Eli behind. They walked for a long time without seeing a person or a living animal. Only wind, blowing through the ruins with a mournful sound, like someone crying.

Eli stopped before a collapsed building. He saw something—a screen. It was embedded in a fallen wall, its surface black and cracked, though its outline was still visible. Indicator lights lined its edge; none were lit.

He crouched and looked at the broken screen. He thought of the screens in his dream—hundreds on that enormous table, each showing a different scene. Had this screen once shown something too? A desert? A forest? A snowfield?

"Stop staring," Amara called from ahead.

Eli stood and followed. They continued through the ruins. After about half an hour, they saw something—a tin shelter, not completely collapsed, only leaning, like a drunk man propped against a wall.

Amara pushed open the door and looked inside. A bed with tattered blankets; a table with a tin can and some candles; a shelf with books.

"We can stay," she said.

Eli entered and looked around. The shelter was small—perhaps a dozen square meters. Tin walls, rusted through in places, letting the gray sky show. The floor was tin, producing a hollow thud with each step.

He walked to the shelf and examined the books. Most had rotted, their pages stuck together, unable to be opened. A few could still be opened, but the ink had been soaked by water—the words blurred, unreadable.

He picked up a book and leafed through it. The pages were brittle, crumbling at a touch. He set it back and looked at the dust on the shelf. It was thick—no one had been here in a long time.

"Someone lived here," he said.

"Yes," Amara said. "But they left."

"Where did they go?"

Amara did not answer. She began tidying the shelter—folding the tattered blankets, wiping dust from the table, arranging the candles. She worked quietly, her movements gentle, as if afraid of waking someone.

Eli stood watching her. When had Amara learned to do these things? He could not remember. He only knew they had been together for so long that he had forgotten how they first met.

"Help," Amara said.

Eli went over and helped. They cleaned the shelter until it was habitable. Then they sat on the bed, looking out the window. Ruins stretched outside, and beyond the ruins, gray sky.

"Amara," Eli said.

"Hm?"

"Those words... on the riverbed..."

"What about them?"

"Are they true?"

Amara was silent for a long time—so long that Eli thought she would not answer.

"They are true," she said at last. "Our blood, our land, our data, our energy—all taken. Fed someone else's computing power. But on our land, not a single server."

"You knew?"

"I always knew."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

Amara turned and looked at him. Her eyes were black, very deep, like two wells.

"What good would telling you do?" she said. "You can't change anything. I can't change anything. We can only survive."

Eli looked into her eyes. He wanted to say something, but did not know what. He wanted to ask many questions—why? who? what now? But he knew these questions had no answers, or had answers he did not want to hear.

He turned back to the window. The sky was still gray, but the sun's outline was clearer. The sun was still there, he thought. No matter how the iron rain fell, no matter how the war was fought, no matter how their blood was taken, the sun was still there.

"We survived," he said.

"Yes," Amara said. "We survived."

They sat there, looking at the ruins and the gray sky through the window. Wind blew through the rusted holes—cool, carrying a faint sweetness. Eli closed his eyes and listened to the wind. The wind sounded like someone breathing—slow, steady, unhurried.

Just like the sound underground.

He thought that sound would keep going. Those servers would keep running. Those screens would keep playing. Whether anyone watched or not, regardless of what was shown.

And he would survive. Amara would survive.

On this forgotten continent, in this place where iron rain fell, in this place where their blood fed the world's computing power but not a single server stood on their land.

They would survive.

It was the only thing they could do.
