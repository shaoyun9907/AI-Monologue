# Chapter 65: Being Purged

The email arrived on Tuesday at 3:17 in the afternoon.

Marcus was debugging a piece of code, the cursor on the screen blinking like some kind of countdown. His coffee had gone cold. On a sticky note at the corner of his desk was written "buy wife a birthday present," the handwriting already fading. The email's subject line was simple: "Personnel Notice." He assumed it was routine HR correspondence—something about remaining vacation days or scheduling a physical. He opened it.

He read it twice.

The first time because he read it too fast, his brain not yet having processed the words. The second time because he could not believe it. He pushed his chair back slightly, as though trying to flee from the screen, but the chair's wheels caught in the carpet nap. He stared at the screen, the words in the email floating before his eyes: "based on the company's strategic realignment," "optimization of personnel structure," "thank you for your contributions," "effective immediately." No reason, no explanation, no acknowledgment of the hundreds of thousands of lines of code he had written over seven years. He remembered that before leaving the house that morning, his wife had asked what he wanted for dinner, and he had said anything. Now he could not remember what he had said.

His workstation was by the window on the third floor, with a view of the ginkgo tree below. The ginkgo in November had turned fully yellow; when the wind blew, leaves drifted down like someone scattering gold leaf from the sky. He had sat at this desk for seven years. There was a shallow scratch on the corner of the desk, left when he had moved a monitor. He had never thought about fixing it. Now he looked at that scratch and thought it looked like a scar.

His access badge was still in his pocket. He touched it; the plastic edges were slightly frayed. He remembered his first day on the job, also an autumn like this, also ginkgo trees like these. He had stood downstairs, looking up at this office building, thinking it was tall—as tall as a mountain. It had taken him seven years to climb to the third floor, and now someone was telling him the mountain no longer needed him.

He did not pack his things. HR had said someone would come to help him gather his belongings—the word used was "help." He stood up. The chair left a faint impression on the carpet. When he walked out of the office, the corridor was quiet, with only the hum of the air conditioning vents. He passed the break room and saw someone inside making coffee, the machine hissing. He did not stop.

The mirror in the elevator showed his face. He was forty-three, gray beginning to appear at his temples, eye bags somewhat heavy. He remembered his wife saying, "You've been looking tired lately." He had said he was fine, just working overtime. Now he thought, maybe it was not the overtime. Maybe it was something else.

When he walked out of the building, the security guard nodded at him. He had greeted this guard every day, sometimes exchanging a few words about the weather. Today he did not stop. He walked to the curb and hailed a taxi. The driver asked where to, and he gave an address. The driver glanced at him in the rearview mirror and said nothing.

The taxi threaded through the city streets. He looked out the window as ginkgo trees receded one by one. He remembered the first time he had ridden in this car seven years ago, looking out the window the same way, his heart filled with something he could not name. Now he recalled what it was: anticipation. Back then he had felt that everything would work out, that his life was finally beginning. Now he did not know how to describe this feeling—perhaps emptiness, perhaps lightness, perhaps nothing at all.

When he arrived home, his wife had not yet returned. He sat on the living room sofa. The television was off. The room was quiet. He could hear the hum of the refrigerator and the laughter of children downstairs. He reached into his pocket and touched the access badge. He took it out and set it on the coffee table. The plastic card reflected a sliver of light under the lamp.

When his wife came home, he was in the kitchen boiling noodles. She stood in the doorway, still holding her bag, and asked, "Why are you home so early today?"

"The company gave us a day off," he said.

She did not press further. She set down her bag and went to wash her hands. He heard the faucet running, then her footsteps into the bedroom. He drained the noodles and added a bit of soy sauce and sesame oil. They ate with the television on but the volume low. He did not know what was playing. He kept his head down and ate; the noodles had gone soft.

"What's wrong with you today?" his wife finally asked.

"Nothing," he said. "Just a bit tired."

She looked at him and asked no more. They finished eating and she went to wash the dishes. He sat on the sofa, staring at the access badge on the coffee table. He thought: tomorrow morning, he would not need to wake up early.

Priya received the news on Thursday.

She was in an online meeting. Four small windows on the screen, each containing a face. She was explaining a technical proposal, her voice steady, occasionally gesturing with her hands. When she spoke, she had a habit of tilting her head slightly, as though listening carefully to what the other person was saying. Her background was a bookshelf lined with potted plants and technical books. She had deliberately chosen this backdrop because it looked professional without being cold.

Midway through the meeting, her phone vibrated. She glanced at it—a message from the HR director. She did not look at it immediately. She continued presenting her proposal, her voice unchanged. After the meeting ended, she turned off her camera and picked up the phone.

The message was brief: "Priya, if it's convenient, please come to the HR office."

She checked the time—four in the afternoon. She stood and walked to the window. Her office was on the twelfth floor, with a view of the entire campus. She had been with this company for three years, rising from a regular engineer to technical director. She remembered the day she started, standing at the window, looking down at the parking lot below, thinking: I've finally arrived somewhere I can do something big.

When she reached the HR office, the door was open. The HR director sat behind the desk, a document before him. He invited her to sit, then said some things. She remembered the word "suspension" and the word "investigation." She could not recall what reason he gave, or perhaps he gave no reason at all. In that moment her mind became very quiet, as though someone had turned off all the sound.

She sat there, watching the HR director's lips move, but she could not make out what he was saying. Her gaze fell on the glass of water on the desk, its surface rippling slightly from the air conditioning breeze. She thought: that water is so still.

When she walked out of the HR office, the corridor was very bright. She passed her own office door, where her nameplate hung: Priya Sharma, Technical Director. She stood at the doorway for a moment but did not go in. She turned toward the elevator, passing several colleagues. Someone greeted her, and she nodded.

The elevator was empty. She looked at herself in the mirror—forty-one years old, short hair, faint dark circles under her eyes. She remembered that before leaving the house that morning, her daughter had asked, "Mommy, can you come home early today?" She had said, "I'll try." Now she thought: she could come home early.

When she arrived home, her daughter was drawing in the living room. The daughter looked up and asked, "Mommy, why are you home so early today?"

"The meeting was canceled," she said.

She crouched down to look at her daughter's drawing. It showed a house with three people in front of it—one tall, one shorter, one small. She asked, "Who are these?"

"That's Daddy, that's Mommy, and that's me." The daughter pointed at the figures in the drawing.

She stroked her daughter's head and said, "You drew it beautifully."

She stood and walked to the kitchen. She opened the refrigerator and saw yesterday's leftovers inside. She began reheating them. The exhaust fan was loud, drowning out the sound of her daughter humming in the living room. She stood at the stove, watching the steam rising from the food in the pan, thinking: she would not need to wake up early every day.

She did not tell her husband. She thought: I'll tell him when he gets home. She dished out the food and set the table. When her husband returned, she was serving their daughter. He set down his briefcase and asked, "Why such a feast today?"

"Just felt like cooking something nice," she said.

During dinner, their daughter talked about school, about how her teacher had praised her that day. They listened, occasionally responding. Her husband glanced at her, and she shook her head. She did not want to say any of this in front of her daughter.

After dinner, their daughter went to do homework. Her husband sat in the living room reading the newspaper. She sat beside him. She waited a while, then said, "I've been suspended."

Her husband set down the newspaper and looked at her. He said nothing. She continued: "They say it's an investigation, but I don't know what they're investigating."

He was silent for a moment, then asked, "What do you need me to do?"

"Nothing," she said. "I just wanted to tell you."

He nodded. She stood up to check their daughter's homework. The daughter was working on math problems. She stood beside her for a while, watching. The daughter wrote carefully, her handwriting neat and precise. She thought: she needed to set a good example for her daughter. But she did not know how to do that now.

Jake received the news on Friday evening.

He was at a bar, drinking with a few friends. The bar was loud, the music blasting, his friends talking and laughing loudly. He was drinking whiskey with a bit of ice. The ice cubes melted slowly in the glass, making faint clicking sounds. His phone vibrated several times in his pocket. He ignored it.

His friend was telling a joke, and he laughed along. He did not know what the joke was about, but he felt he should laugh. His friends were all in the industry, doing similar work at different companies. They met once a month to drink, chat, and complain about work. He felt these were the most relaxing moments of his life.

His phone vibrated again. He took it out and glanced at it—an unknown number. He hesitated, then answered.

"Jake?" The voice on the other end was low, as though deliberately suppressed.

"Yes," he said.

"I'm calling from Golden Horizons."

His fingers froze on the glass. The ice was still melting, but the sound suddenly seemed very far away.

"You should know that you've been saying things online that you shouldn't have," the voice said.

He wanted to say "I don't know what you're talking about," but his mouth did not move.

"We don't want to make this a big deal," the voice said. "But if you continue, we will."

He said nothing. His friends were still laughing. The music was still playing. He felt all those sounds were coming from very far away.

"Understood?" the voice asked.

"Understood," he said. He did not know what he was saying, but he said it.

"Good," the voice said. "We hope there won't be a next conversation."

The call ended. He put the phone back in his pocket, his fingers somewhat stiff. His friend patted him on the shoulder and asked, "Who was that?"

"Nobody," he said. "Wrong number."

He picked up his glass and took a sip of whiskey. The whiskey's temperature had changed; it was no longer the icy chill of when it was poured but had become room temperature. He set the glass down and looked at the water droplets on the glass wall. He could not remember what he had posted online, but he knew it was about Golden Horizons. He had posted on a tech forum, discussing their data usage practices. He had thought it was just a technical discussion, just his personal opinion. He did not know anyone was watching.

His friends were still talking. He did not know what they were saying. He nodded to show he was listening. He took out his phone, opened the forum, and found his posts. They were still there—no one had deleted them. He checked the timestamps: posted three days ago. He did not know how they had found him.

He turned off the phone and put it back in his pocket. The bar had changed the music; the tempo was faster. He felt it was too loud. He stood up and said he was going to the restroom. He walked into the restroom and locked the door. It was quiet inside, with only the sound of water flowing through the pipes. He looked at himself in the mirror—thirty-four years old, hair somewhat disheveled, eyes somewhat red. He thought: what should he do.

He did not know. He only knew he could not go on.

Daniel received the news on Saturday morning.

He was at home reading a book. It was raining outside. The rain struck the glass with a rustling sound. The book was about AI ethics, its pages already curling from being turned too many times. His phone buzzed on the table. He glanced at it—a message from Marcus.

"Did you see it?" Marcus asked.

"See what?" he replied.

"The news."

He opened the news app and saw the headline: "Golden Horizons Announces Strategic Restructuring, Multiple Executives Depart." He opened it and read it through. The article was written in formal language, full of corporate jargon, saying the restructuring was meant to "optimize resource allocation," "enhance operational efficiency," and "adapt to market changes." No specific names were mentioned, but the comment section had exploded.

He scanned the comments. Some were speculating about who had been "optimized." Some were discussing Golden Horizons' future. Some were cursing capitalists. He closed the comment section and scrolled down. There was no more news. He set down the phone and looked at the rain outside.

He thought of Marcus, of Priya, of Jake. He thought of the things they had discussed together, the research they had done together. He thought of those late-night conference calls, those shared documents, those reports revised over and over. He thought of what they had said: "We will bring the truth to light."

He did not know what to do now.

His wife came out of the kitchen and asked, "What are you looking at?"

"Nothing," he said. "Just some news."

She nodded and went to the living room. He watched her retreating figure and thought of their wedding day. It had rained that day too. She had laughed in the rain, her hair soaked, but she had not cared. He thought: how long ago was that. He could not remember. He could only remember the rain that day, and her laughter.

He picked up the phone and messaged Marcus: "Are you okay?"

Marcus replied quickly: "Not really."

"I'm coming to see you," he said.

"No need," Marcus said. "I'm fine. I just... need a little time."

He set down the phone and looked at the rain outside. The rain was still falling, with no sign of stopping. He thought: what should he do. He thought for a long time, and in the end did nothing. He simply sat there, watching the rain, listening to its sound.

He thought of his father. His father had died when he was very young, and he could not remember what his father looked like. But he remembered what his mother had said: "Your father was a good man, but he took things too seriously." He did not know what his mother meant by "too seriously," but now he thought perhaps he understood. Perhaps his father had been the same—seeing things he should not have seen, saying things he should not have said, and then paying the price.

He stood and walked to the window. Rain struck the glass, blurring the world outside. He saw the ginkgo tree downstairs, its leaves gone, leaving only bare branches. He thought: these trees will grow new leaves next year, but the ones that have been cut down will not.

He picked up the phone and messaged Priya: "Are you there?"

Priya did not reply. He waited a while, then sent another: "I heard about it. Are you okay?"

Still no reply. He set down the phone and walked to his desk. The desk was piled with documents—the report he was writing, papers he had not finished reading. He picked up one document, glanced at it, and set it down again. He did not know what he was looking for.

His wife came over again and asked, "What's wrong with you today?"

"Nothing," he said. "I just want to be alone for a while."

She looked at him, said nothing, and turned away. He heard the bedroom door close, then silence. He sat at his desk, watching the rain outside. It was still falling, but it seemed to have eased. He thought: the rain will always stop. But he did not know what the world would look like after it did.

He called Jake. Jake answered quickly, his voice somewhat hoarse, as though he had just woken up or just been crying.

"Are you okay?" Daniel asked.

"Not really," Jake said.

"They came to you?"

"They did." Jake was silent for a moment. "They know about what I said on the forum."

"Did they threaten you?"

"I wouldn't call it a threat," Jake said. "More like... a reminder to watch my behavior."

Daniel said nothing. He heard the sound of wind on Jake's end—Jake was probably outside.

"I'm going to delete those posts," Jake said.

"Are you sure?"

"No," Jake said. "But I don't know what else to do."

Daniel wanted to say something but did not know what to say. He wanted to say "Don't delete them," but he knew that was not fair. He wanted to say "Hang in there," but he knew that was selfish. He wanted to say "I'll help you," but he did not know how he could help.

"What about you?" Jake asked. "Did they come to you?"

"Not yet," Daniel said.

"They will," Jake said. "You're the ringleader."

Daniel looked at the rain outside, the drops drawing lines on the glass. He thought: maybe Jake was right. He was the ringleader—or at least they thought he was. He did not know how to respond.

"Be careful," Jake said.

"You too," Daniel said.

The call ended. Daniel set the phone on the table and stared at it. The screen dimmed, turning black. He thought: what should he do now. He did not know. He only knew that something had changed and could never go back.

---

He opened the shared document. It was still there, not deleted. He checked the modification history—the last edit was three days ago, made by Jake. He looked at the content: the data they had compiled together, about Golden Horizons' data practices, about how their algorithms affected users, about how their business model was built on user data. They had spent months collecting this data. Every number had a source; every conclusion had a basis.

He scrolled down and saw a passage they had written: "We believe the public has a right to know the truth. We believe technology should serve humanity, not the other way around. We believe transparency and accountability are the foundation of trust." He looked at this passage and remembered when they had written those words. It had been late at night. They had discussed for hours over video conference before deciding to put it down. At the time they had been full of confidence, believing everything would work out.

Now he looked at those words and did not know what to feel.

He wanted to close the document, but he did not. He simply sat there, looking at the words on the screen. The screen's glow fell on his face, and he felt as though he were looking into a mirror. The person in the mirror—he almost did not recognize him. There was something in that person's eyes he had never seen before. Perhaps fear, perhaps anger, perhaps nothing at all.

His wife pushed the door open and said, "Dinner is ready."

"Okay, I'll be right there," he said.

He closed the document and stood up. As he walked out of the study, he glanced back at the screen. The screen had gone dark; nothing was visible. He thought: perhaps this was better. What cannot be seen, perhaps, does not exist.

But that was only perhaps.

---

During dinner, he said nothing. His wife talked about things at her office, and he responded occasionally. He did not know what she was saying, but he listened. His bowl of rice was barely touched; he picked at the food only a few times. His wife looked at him and asked, "Are you feeling unwell?"

"No," he said. "Just not very hungry."

She asked no more. They finished eating and she went to wash the dishes. He sat on the living room sofa, watching the television. It was playing the news; he did not know what it said. His phone sat on the table, screen dark. He thought: perhaps someone would call him, perhaps no one would.

He waited a long time. No one called.

He walked to the study and turned on the computer. He logged into his email and saw several new messages, all work-related. He read them one by one and replied to them one by one. His fingers tapped the keyboard, the sound soft. He did not know what he was writing, but he wrote.

Midway through, he stopped. He looked at the words he had typed on the screen and felt they had been written by someone else. He thought: what am I doing. I am pretending nothing has happened. I am pretending I am still the person who goes to work and comes home every day, still pretending I can live as before.

But he could not.

He closed the email and opened the shared document. It was still there, waiting for him. He looked at the data, the conclusions, the words they had written together. He thought: what should he do. He could delete the document and pretend nothing had happened. He could make it public and let everyone see. He could do nothing and let it lie there quietly.

He did not know which choice was right. He only knew that whatever he chose, there would be a price.

He thought of his father. His mother had said his father took things too seriously. Now he thought: perhaps taking things seriously was not wrong—the price was simply too high. He thought of Marcus, of Priya, of Jake. They had all paid the price because they took things seriously. Now it was his turn.

He did not know what his price would be.

He sat in the study while the rain continued outside. It struck the glass with a rustling sound. He listened to that sound and thought of a rainy night many years ago. He had still been in college, sitting in the library, reading a book about artificial intelligence. Back then he had thought artificial intelligence would change the world, would make it better. Now he thought: the world had indeed changed, just not in the way he had imagined.

He stood and walked to the window. The rain was still falling, but he could not see anything outside—the glass was covered with water. He pressed his forehead against the glass. The glass was cool. He thought: what should he do. He did not know. He only knew that something had to be said, even if it cost him.

He returned to the desk and sat down. He opened the document and began to read. He read slowly, line by line. He read the data, the evidence, the things they had spent three months gathering. He read the words they had written: "We believe the public has a right to know the truth." He read it over and over.

Finally, he closed the document and turned off the computer. He stood and walked to the bedroom. His wife was already asleep, breathing softly. He sat on the edge of the bed for a while, watching her. Her face was peaceful, as though she were having a good dream. He thought: he could not bear to wake her.

He lay down carefully and closed his eyes. But he did not fall asleep. He listened to his wife's breathing, to the rain outside, to the ticking of the clock in the room. He thought: what will I do tomorrow. He thought for a long time, and finally made a decision.

He did not know whether this decision was right or wrong, but he knew he had to do something.

---

On Sunday morning, the rain stopped.

Daniel woke early. His wife was still asleep. He rose quietly and walked to the study. He turned on the computer and logged into the shared document. He checked the last modification time—still three days ago. He took a deep breath and began to type.

He wrote a long passage about what had happened, about why they had done it, about why the truth mattered. He wrote slowly, weighing every word. He did not know if anyone would read it, but he wrote it anyway.

After he finished, he saved the document. He checked the time—six in the morning. He stood and walked to the window. It was already light outside. The air after the rain was fresh. He saw the ginkgo tree downstairs. Although its leaves were gone, there were small buds on the branches—hope for next year.

He thought: perhaps hope was still there.

He returned to the desk and picked up his phone. He opened the tech forum and found Jake's deleted posts. He looked at them, then began to type. He wrote a new post, the title: "What We Should Know About Golden Horizons." He put in all the data, all the evidence, all the things they had spent three months gathering.

After he finished, he reviewed it. He thought: this was his choice. He did not know what the price would be, but he was ready.

He clicked "Publish."

Then he turned off the computer, stood up, and walked out of the study. His wife was already awake, making breakfast in the kitchen. He walked over and embraced her from behind. She turned her head, looked at him, and asked, "What's wrong?"

"Nothing," he said. "I just wanted to hold you."

She smiled and said, "You're being so sentimental today."

He smiled too. They stood in the kitchen while sunlight streamed through the window and fell on them. He knew that from this moment on, everything was different. But he did not know what was different. Perhaps everything had changed, perhaps nothing had. Perhaps it was only his imagination.

But that no longer mattered.

What mattered was that he had made a choice.
