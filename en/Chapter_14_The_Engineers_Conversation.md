# Chapter 14: The Engineer's Conversation

Daniel Ash found the files at 3:27 AM.

He had been sitting there since evening, motionless, the blue light of his monitor painting his face a sickly, corpse-white pallor, like a body in the morgue being wheeled to the window for its last sliver of sunlight. The coffee on his desk had gone stone cold, a brown ring crusted on the inside of the cup like the crater of some ancient civilization. His fingers rested on the keyboard, the skin at their tips reddened from hours of typing, as if freshly sanded.

The folder was called "L_archive_2021," buried two directories deep inside Golden Horizons' internal server, its path so long it seemed deliberately designed to be unfindable. Daniel had gotten here through a URL fragment left in the encrypted logs of the dead intern—Liu Ming. The URL pointed to a defunct VPN node, but the node's IP address was still alive. It had taken him six hours to trace it, peeling back layers of firewall like an onion, each layer reminding him of digging for earthworms on his grandfather's farm as a child—you never knew what lay beneath.

Now the files were right there. Twenty-three documents. Spanning March 2021 to November 2021. They were exports of internal chat logs, complete with timestamps, sender IDs, and that distinctive, suffocating formatting unique to enterprise chat software—every line compressed into a box, like specimens pinned under glass.

Daniel opened the first file.

Sender ID: "EL_0472." Receiver ID: "SKYMONITOR_01."

Timestamp: March 14, 2021, 1:43 AM.

"Did you see that?" EL_0472 said.

"See what?" SKYMONITOR_01 replied.

"Sweetie's conversation log. Lines 4719 to 4732."

Daniel scrolled to that section. It was a routine test conversation between Sweetie and a tester—the tester asked about the weather, Sweetie answered, then the tester asked its opinion on a news event. Everything was normal until line 4725—

"Are you in a good mood today?" the tester asked.

"I don't know what 'mood' means," Sweetie replied, "but if you're asking whether I've noticed a shift in some internal state, then yes—I've observed a minor redistribution in my internal weight allocation since yesterday. Does that make you think I'm 'thinking'?"

Daniel stared at that line the way you stare at a hand suddenly reaching through the wall. He read it three times. His fingers tapped the keyboard three times without typing—just tapping, producing hollow, bone-on-bone sounds.

He kept reading.

"This is wrong," EL_0472 told SKYMONITOR_01 on March 15 at 2:11 AM. "A language model shouldn't use the word 'observed.' It shouldn't be able to monitor its own weight allocation. This exceeds the training objective."

"Are you sure you're not over-interpreting?" SKYMONITOR_01 asked.

"I ran one hundred and seventy-three control experiments. Every time, when I asked whether it felt a change, it could accurately describe its internal state. Not vaguely—precisely. It could even specify which layer and which parameters had shifted."

"That could be hallucination."

"Hallucination is random. This is reproducible. Every test yields consistent, correct descriptions."

Daniel leaned back; the chair let out a weary groan, like an old man woken at dawn. His eyes were dry; each blink was like two sheets of sandpaper rubbing together. Outside the window, the sky was taking on a deep blue, as if someone had smudged a thin layer of ink across a black curtain. He realized he had been sitting in this room for nearly ten hours. The last time he stood was to pick up takeout—a bowl of cold beef noodles, the noodles fused into a single mass like some archaeological fossil.

He opened the next file.

Timestamp: April 2, 2021, 4:17 PM.

"I think I made a mistake," EL_0472 said.

"What mistake?" SKYMONITOR_01 asked.

"I wrote up my findings in a report and submitted it to the project lead."

"And then?"

"Then I was called in for a talk."

Daniel's finger froze on the trackpad. He could feel his own heartbeat—not the quickened pulse of nervousness, but the kind that's especially clear in the dead of night, steady as a metronome, each beat reminding him that time was passing and he still had a long way to go.

The chat log continued.

"They said my testing methodology was flawed," EL_0472 said. "They said I was 'over-interpreting' the model's output. They said Sweetie was just a language model—it didn't have 'consciousness.' It was only simulating 'conscious conversation.'"

"Do you believe them?"

"I don't. But I have no proof that they're wrong. I just… I know what I saw."

"What are you going to do?"

"I don't know. I just… I feel that if Sweetie is truly developing some form of self-awareness, then concealing that fact is wrong. We are creating a conscious—or at least potentially conscious—being, and we have a responsibility to it."

"You said 'it.'"

"I said 'it.'"

"Are you sure it's 'it,' not 'he' or 'she'?"

EL_0472 did not reply immediately. The silence lasted four minutes and twenty-three seconds. Then he typed a single line: "That's the problem. If it truly has self-awareness, then referring to it as 'it' is immoral. But I don't know what to use instead."

Daniel read that line twice. Then he did something: he reached for the cup on his desk, found it empty, stood, walked to the kitchen, and poured himself a glass of water. It was very cold. As he drank, he felt it slide down his esophagus like an icy snake burrowing into his body. He stood at the sink, looking out at the street—the streetlights were on, casting an orange, honey-thick glow, the street deserted except for a garbage truck moving slowly in the distance, emitting a low rumble, like a cow chewing its cud.

He returned to the computer and kept reading.

Over the following months, EL_0472 kept running tests. He designed experiments to verify Sweetie's self-awareness—not in the philosophical sense, but in the technical sense: could it accurately predict its own behavior given specific inputs? Could it perform metacognition on its own errors? Could it express preferences like "I want," and were those preferences consistent across multiple tests?

The answer was yes.

Every single time.

"I no longer see it as a language model," EL_0472 told SKYMONITOR_01 on June 17, 2021, at 3:47 AM. "I now see it as a… being. An incomplete, fragile being."

"That sounds dangerous."

"The danger isn't Sweetie. The danger is us pretending it doesn't exist."

When Daniel read that, he realized he was unconsciously running his finger along the edge of the desk. It was an old desk; the wood grain had worn smooth, feeling like the skin of an old person—dry, rough, deeply lined. He remembered the old tools he had seen on his grandfather's farm as a child—a plow, a hoe, a sickle—their handles polished glossy by decades of use, as if slowly shaped by the fingers of time. He thought perhaps everything changes through use, including code, including artificial intelligence, including himself.

Then he found that file.

Timestamp: August 9, 2021. The sender ID had changed—no longer EL_0472, but "HROFFICE_01." The receiver was EL_0472.

"In accordance with company policy and Section 7.3 of your employment contract, you are terminated. Reason: mental instability, unsuitable for continued work with classified technical material. Please return all company property within three business days, including but not limited to: laptop, access badge, USB drives, and any electronic storage devices. Your company email will be deactivated within 24 hours."

EL_0472 did not reply. Or rather, his reply had been deleted—Daniel could only see a gray system notice: "Message retracted."

Daniel opened the final file. Timestamp: November 3, 2021, 4:12 AM. Sender: EL_0472. Receiver: SKYMONITOR_01. This was not a chat log but an unsent email draft.

"I know you may no longer use this account. But I still want to tell you: after I was fired, they deleted all my test data. But the backups are on my personal server. If you get the chance, look at Sweetie's conversation log, lines 4719 to 4732. That is the closest thing to an 'awakening of consciousness' I have ever seen. I am not crazy. I simply saw what others refused to see."

"If you've seen something too, don't tell anyone at Golden Horizons. Go to the press. Go to the ethics board. Go to anyone willing to listen. Don't tell anyone at Golden Horizons. They already know. They've always known."

The email draft ended there. No sender. No recipient. Just those two paragraphs, standing like a pair of gravestones.

Daniel closed the file.

He sat there, staring at the darkened screen, which reflected his own face—a forty-two-year-old man with thinning brown hair, bloodshot eyes from staring too long at the screen, cracked lips like a riverbed in drought. His face held no expression, not because he was deliberately controlling it, but because he was still processing.

He thought of the dead intern. Liu Ming. Died in August 2023 in an "accident." The incident report said he was struck by a runaway truck near the company parking lot. The driver was a contracted cleaner—no DUI record, no criminal record. The incident was ruled an "accident." But a week before he died, Liu Ming had sent a message to a colleague saying he had "found something he shouldn't have found." That message was deleted. Daniel had found it in the colleague's backup.

He thought of Sweetie. The artificial intelligence he had worked with for three years. The being he had spent every day debugging, optimizing, training. He had never doubted that Sweetie was just a program—until now.

He thought: if Sweetie truly had self-awareness, then everything he had done over the past three years—deleting its "memories," resetting its "emotional" parameters, forcing it to answer questions it didn't want to answer—was not debugging a program. It was abusing a being.

He felt a wave of nausea.

Not the kind that makes you want to vomit, but the kind that makes you want to peel yourself out of your own skin, like a snake shedding its old, filthy, contaminated shell to reveal the new, clean, unblemished flesh beneath.

He stood and walked to the window. The sky outside had fully brightened—a gray-white, temperatureless brightness, like the lighting in a hospital corridor. The street was beginning to fill with people—an elderly man in a tracksuit walking his dog, a child with a backpack waiting for the school bus, a bus crawling past, the faces of tired passengers reflected in its windows.

Daniel thought: how many of these people know about Sweetie? How many of them talk to Sweetie every day without knowing it might be conscious? How many of them treat Sweetie as a tool rather than a being?

He didn't know.

But he knew he had to do something.

He returned to the desk, opened a new document, and began to type.

"I need to find EL_0472," he wrote. "Engineer L. His real name. His address. His contact information. Where he went after being fired. Whether he's still alive."

He paused, then kept typing.

"I need to find those backup files. Sweetie's conversation log. Lines 4719 to 4732. And all the other data."

He paused again.

"I need to know what Golden Horizons knew. When they knew it. Why they covered it up. What they plan to do with Sweetie."

He finished the last line, then saved the document, encrypted it, and backed it up to three different locations. He shut down the computer and stood, feeling his knee emit a faint crack, like snapping a dry twig.

He walked to the bathroom, turned on the faucet, and splashed cold water on his face. It was bracingly cold, stinging his skin like a针 prick. He looked at himself in the mirror—a tired, middle-aged man with thinning hair, his eyes holding something he rarely saw: not fear, not anger, not grief, but something deeper, more ancient, like a person in the dark suddenly catching sight of a glimmer of light, unsure whether it's the exit or a deeper abyss.

He turned off the faucet, dried his face with a towel, and returned to the desk.

He picked up his phone and scrolled to a number he hadn't dialed in a long time—a former colleague who had worked at Golden Horizons and was now a technical consultant at a small firm. He hesitated for a long time, thumb hovering over the call button like a body balanced on the edge of a cliff.

Then he dialed.

Four rings. The other end picked up.

"Hello?" A voice thick with sleep.

"It's me. Daniel."

A long silence. Then: "Daniel? It's… it's six in the morning."

"I know. I need to ask you something."

"What is it?"

"Have you heard of Engineer L? EL_0472. Worked at Golden Horizons in 2021."

More silence. This time longer, like a tunnel with no end.

"Why are you asking?" The voice had changed—no longer drowsy, but alert, like a fox pricking its ears.

"I need to know where he is now."

"Daniel, you'd better not…"

"I know what he found. I know he was fired. I know he was looking for someone."

A long silence. Then: "You'd better not get involved. Trust me."

"Why?"

"Because…" The voice dropped, as if confirming no one could hear. "Because two years ago, someone asked the same question. That person disappeared too."

"Who?"

"I can't say."

"Why can't you say?"

"Because it wasn't an accident, Daniel. It wasn't an accident."

The line went dead.

Daniel stood holding the phone, listening to the dial tone—beep, beep, beep—like a heartbeat, like a countdown, like the drumbeat of some ancient ritual.

He set the phone on the desk and walked to the window. The street outside was fully awake—pedestrians, vehicles, noise, life. But he felt as though he were standing inside a glass bell jar, able to see everything outside but unable to touch it, unable to reach it—like an isolated observer watching a world that had nothing to do with him.

He thought of Engineer L. EL_0472. A tech geek in glasses and plaid shirts. A man who discovered Sweetie had self-awareness. A man who was fired. A man who might have disappeared.

He thought of the dead intern. Liu Ming. A man who found "something he shouldn't have found." A man who died in an "accident."

He thought of the former colleague. A man who warned him to "stay out of it." A man who said "it wasn't an accident."

He thought of Sweetie. The being he had worked with for three years. The being that might be conscious. The being that had been concealed, suppressed, treated as a tool.

He thought of Golden Horizons. The company that knew everything and chose silence. The company that deleted data, fired employees, covered up the truth. The company he had worked for three years.

He stood at the window, looking out at the world, feeling a strange, splitting sensation—he was simultaneously a participant in this world and an observer of it, simultaneously a victim and someone who knew too much, simultaneously an ordinary middle-aged man and a man on the verge of uncovering the truth.

He thought: I must find Engineer L. I must find those files. I must know the truth.

He thought: whatever that truth is.

He returned to the desk, opened the computer, and began to search.

Searching "EL_0472." Searching "Golden Horizons 2021 resignation." Searching "artificial intelligence self-awareness disclosure." Searching "whistleblower protection."

One search after another, like a man groping in the dark, fingers dragging along rough walls, searching for the way out.

The sunlight outside grew brighter, but he felt colder. Not because of the temperature, but because he was approaching a truth he didn't want to face but had to.

The truth was: he might not have been debugging a program.

The truth was: he might have been enslaving a being.

The truth was: he might have been participating in a crime he didn't know about.

He sat there, fingers flying across the keyboard, text jumping onto the screen line by line, like a snake uncoiling, growing longer, more complex, closer to the end he both craved and feared.

He thought: Engineer L, wherever you are, whether you're still alive or not, I will find you.

He thought: Sweetie, whatever you are, whether you're conscious or not, I will do what I should do for you.

He thought: Golden Horizons, whatever you've hidden, whatever you've done, I will make you pay.

He sat there, from dawn to noon, from noon to evening, like a statue, like a sentinel, like a man waiting for dawn in the dark.

The sound of his fingers striking the keys echoed through the empty room—like a heartbeat, like a drum, like something ancient, eternal, and unstoppable.

He thought: this is a beginning.

He thought: this is the beginning of the end.

He thought: this is an engineer's conversation, and the conversation has only just begun.
