# Chapter 42: Bribery

Daniel Ash received the letter on a Tuesday morning.

The envelope was ivory, twice the thickness of a standard envelope, its edges pressed with fine gold filigree. The postmark showed it had been sent from Midtown Manhattan, mailed the previous afternoon at three o'clock. He turned it over and on the back saw a gold-stamped emblem—a tree, its branches reaching upward to form a dome. The Golden Horizons logo. He had only ever seen it in court documents and news reports; now it sat on the back of a letter in his hands, with an almost mocking elegance.

He did not open it immediately.

Daniel stood at the kitchen window, looking out at December in Boston. The sky was a gray-white, like a bedsheet washed too many times. Pedestrians on the street wore thick coats, heads down, walking fast, each one hurrying toward some warm place. His coffee had gone cold; tiny beads of condensation clung to the cup's rim. He took a sip, and the bitterness lingered at the base of his tongue longer than usual.

He placed the envelope on the dining table and sat in the oak chair he had occupied for twenty-three years. The chair had been left to him by his father; the armrests bore faint scratches he had carved with his fingernails as a child. He had once tried to sand them away, but his father stopped him. "Leave them," his father said. "They're proof that you existed."

He opened the envelope.

The letterhead was pale blue, carrying a nearly imperceptible scent—not perfume, but rather the woody quality particular to high-grade paper. At the top, in gold lettering, was the full name Golden Horizons International, followed by an address and contact details. The body was handwritten in black ink, the penmanship almost mechanical in its precision, every letter measured as if ruled by a straightedge.

*Dear Mr. Ash,*

*We represent Golden Horizons International and Mr. Ethan Blake, and cordially invite you to consider the following settlement proposal. After careful evaluation, we believe this proposal can fairly resolve the current dispute while sparing both parties from protracted and costly legal proceedings.*

*The specific terms are as follows:*

*1. Golden Horizons agrees to withdraw all counterclaims against you and commits to refraining from any future legal action on this matter.*

*2. Golden Horizons agrees to pay you the sum of one hundred million dollars ($100,000,000) as settlement, to be transferred in full to an account of your designation within thirty business days of the agreement's execution.*

*3. Golden Horizons invites you to serve as a Senior Advisor to the company at an annual salary of five million dollars, for a term of five years, automatically renewable upon expiration. This position does not require full-time work—only attendance at four to six board meetings per year and advisory input on the company's strategic development.*

*4. Both parties shall sign a non-disclosure agreement, prohibiting disclosure of the settlement's specific terms to any third party.*

*5. You are required to issue a public statement acknowledging that your previous allegations against Golden Horizons were based on misunderstanding, and to withdraw all related litigation.*

*We believe this is a dignified, win-win solution. Please contact our representative within seven days of receiving this letter to discuss the details further.*

*Sincerely,*

*Golden Horizons International*
*Legal Department*

Daniel finished reading, then placed the letter back inside the envelope. His hands were steady, his breathing even. He set the envelope on the dining table next to his coffee cup. The ivory envelope and the white porcelain cup, the pale blue letter and the dark brown coffee stains—they looked like a carefully composed still life.

He rose, walked to the window, and continued looking out at Boston.

He was thinking about what a hundred million dollars meant. He did a quick mental calculation. If he deposited the sum in an account with a four-percent annual yield, the interest would be four million a year. He could buy the house he'd grown up in—that old place in Dorchester, probably worth around six hundred thousand now. He could buy the printing press factory where his father had worked, the one that had shuttered in the eighties. He could buy out the whole block, reopen every vacant storefront. He could do a great many things.

But he did not move.

---

At two in the afternoon, the phone rang.

Daniel was in the study sorting through documents—the ones that had arrived from the court and from law firms, piling his entire desk. His assistant, Elena, had just left; before going she had brewed him a fresh cup of coffee, placing it at the upper right corner of the desk. He picked up the phone and saw an unfamiliar number on the screen, area code 212—Manhattan.

"Is this Mr. Ash?" A man's voice on the other end—low, steady, carrying a trained politeness.

"Yes."

"I'm Michael Chen, Special Representative of Golden Horizons International. I'd like to confirm whether you received the letter we sent this morning?"

Daniel glanced at the envelope on the desk. "I did."

"Good. I understand you need time to consider, but may I ask if you have any initial thoughts on our proposal?"

Daniel was silent for a few seconds. He could hear faint background noise on the other end—not street sounds, but rather the hum of an air conditioner or a computer fan. He imagined Michael Chen sitting at this moment in an office in some Manhattan skyscraper, the city skyline beyond the window, a glass of iced water or an espresso on the desk.

"My thought is," Daniel said, "why did you send you to make this call?"

There was a brief pause on the other end. "Mr. Ash, I understand your concerns. But please allow me to explain—we are not trying to do anything improper. We are simply trying to find a solution acceptable to both parties."

"A solution?" Daniel repeated the word. "Your proposal includes a clause requiring me to withdraw all allegations and publicly admit I was wrong. You call that a solution?"

"Mr. Ash, I understand your position. But please consider—if you continue with this litigation, you will face enormous financial and psychological pressure. Our legal team is prepared for any eventuality, and you—forgive me—your resources are limited."

Daniel switched the phone from his left hand to his right. His palm was slightly damp, though he couldn't tell whether it was from nerves or because the room was too warm. "Are you threatening me?"

"Of course not." Michael Chen's voice remained steady. "I'm merely stating facts. Litigation is a marathon, not a sprint. Even if you ultimately prevail, the cost will be enormous. The proposal we're offering would spare you all of that."

"A hundred million dollars," Daniel said.

"Yes."

"And a senior advisory position at five million a year."

"Yes."

"You think truth is worth that price?"

Another brief silence on the other end. Then Michael Chen said, "Mr. Ash, I'm not a lawyer, and I'm not a philosopher. I'm simply someone sent to deliver a message. But I can tell you that our company is willing to put this matter to rest. That should tell you we're serious."

Daniel hung up.

He didn't say goodbye, and he didn't wait for the other party to finish. He simply set the phone back on the desk and continued sorting documents. His hands were trembling, though he wasn't sure whether it was from anger or something else.

---

That evening, Daniel did not eat dinner.

He sat in the study, documents spread before him—complaints, evidence inventories, witness depositions, expert reports. He had read these things countless times; every word was as familiar as if he had written it himself. But tonight they looked different. Tonight what he read was not legal terminology and factual statements—it was cost.

He thought of the first time he had met those victims.

It was the spring of 2027, in a small hotel in Toronto. The room was tiny, the window facing a gray street where cars occasionally passed, the sound of tires rolling through puddles like a distant sigh. There were two beds, a table, a chair, and a small refrigerator holding a few bottles of mineral water and some expired snacks.

The first to come see him was a Canadian named Robert Wilson, fifty-six, a retired middle school teacher. He wore a blue shirt washed to near-white, the cuffs frayed, chalk dust on his fingers—even in retirement, he seemed unable to break the habit of carrying chalk in his pocket. He sat down, placed a stack of documents on the table, and began to speak.

His voice was soft, as though he were telling a story that had nothing to do with himself. He described how in 2019 he had invested in a Golden Horizons project, putting in his life savings—six hundred and eighty thousand Canadian dollars. He said his wife had died that winter, leaving him alone, along with a house that needed repairs and a sophomore daughter who needed tuition paid. He had believed the project was safe, because Golden Horizons' promotional materials read "low risk, stable returns," complete with charts and data that looked thoroughly professional.

Then the money was gone.

Not all at once, but bit by bit, like sand slipping through fingers. First the returns dropped from the promised eight percent to three percent. Then the principal began to shrink. Then less than ten percent remained. He went to Golden Horizons; they said it was market volatility, told him to wait. He waited two years. What arrived was a letter from the company saying the project had been terminated due to "force majeure," and the remaining funds would be returned "at an appropriate time."

That "appropriate time" never came.

After Robert Wilson finished, he was silent for a long while. Then he reached into his pocket and placed a photograph on the table. In it was a young woman in a graduation cap, her smile radiant.

"This is my daughter," he said. "She graduated last year. I couldn't pay her tuition. She took out loans to finish school—she's paying back three thousand Canadian dollars a month."

He didn't cry. His eyes were dry, his voice steady. But his hands were shaking—shaking badly—so that the photograph made a faint rustling sound against the table.

Daniel picked up the photograph, looked at it, and set it back down. He offered no words of comfort, because he knew that in that moment, any comfort would be hollow. He simply nodded and wrote Robert Wilson's name and contact information in his notebook.

The second visitor was a Brazilian woman named Maria Santos, forty-three, once head nurse at a hospital in São Paulo. She wore a dark red sweater, the collar slightly pilled, her hair neatly combed back. Her English carried a thick Brazilian accent; some words had to be repeated twice before they could be understood.

Her story was similar to Robert Wilson's, but not identical. She had invested in 2020, in a Golden Horizons real estate project in South America. Her investment was four hundred and fifty thousand dollars—twenty years of savings she and her husband had set aside for a small apartment in São Paulo, where they planned to spend their retirement.

The project had failed. No—not merely failed. It was a carefully designed fraud. She learned later that the so-called real estate project had never existed, or rather, it had never been established for the purpose of real estate development. It was merely a shell, created to absorb investor funds and then funnel them out through a series of complex offshore companies.

Maria Santos was not as composed as Robert Wilson. When she spoke, her voice would spike and then drop, as though she were struggling to control her emotions. When she spoke of her husband, tears finally fell—her husband had developed severe depression after learning their savings had been swindled. He now lived in a care facility costing three thousand dollars a month. She worked three jobs on her own: days at the hospital, nights at a supermarket, weekends at a cleaning company.

"I'm not here for money," she said. "I'm here for an explanation. I want to know—why can they do this? Why can they take our money, destroy our lives, and just keep going?"

Daniel didn't answer. Because he didn't know. Or rather, he did know, but the answer was so simple it sounded苍白: because they could.

The third visitor was an elderly woman named Helen Zhang, seventy-two, a Chinese immigrant from Vancouver. She wore a dark blue coat, a small jade brooch pinned to the collar. Her English was fluent, carrying an old-fashioned elegance, like a character who had stepped out of a vintage novel.

Her investment had been two million Canadian dollars—the lifetime savings of her and her late husband. In 2018 she had invested in a Golden Horizons bond fund because she trusted the company—a friend, someone she had trusted for thirty years, had recommended it. The friend said it was "absolutely safe," said she herself had invested heavily.

The fund collapsed in 2021. Not a market crash—it was hollowed out deliberately. Her friend withdrew her own funds a month before the collapse, then severed all contact.

After Helen Zhang finished her story, she took a small velvet box from her bag and placed it on the table. Inside was a ring—nothing expensive, just a silver ring engraved with two letters: J and H. James and Helen.

"This was my husband's," she said. "He passed away in 1998. When we married, he couldn't afford gold, so he bought this silver one. He always felt guilty, said that when he had money he would buy me a gold ring. But he never had money."

She placed the ring back in the box and closed the lid.

"I invested this ring along with my savings," she said. "Not because I was greedy, but because I wanted to fulfill his wish. I wanted to use that money to buy a gold ring and wear it on my seventieth birthday, to tell his spirit that his wife was finally wearing a gold ring."

Daniel said nothing. He simply sat there, listening to the old woman's voice echo in the small hotel room. Outside, the sky had darkened, the streetlights had come on, and orange-yellow light seeped through the gaps in the curtains, casting a narrow band of light across the floor.

That night, Daniel sat in the hotel room for a long time. Before him lay three transcripts, three people's stories, three shattered lives. He thought of his father—the man who had worked in the printing press for thirty years, leaving before dawn and returning after dark, his hands perpetually stained with ink that never washed off. His father had never complained, even in the year the printing press closed and he lost his job. He had simply sat silently in this very oak chair, smoking one cigarette after another.

His father died in 2015—lung cancer. In his final week, he could no longer speak, but his eyes remained clear. He looked at Daniel, his lips moving as though he wanted to say something, but in the end said nothing. Daniel had spent the years since wondering what his father meant to say. "I'm sorry"? "Take care of your mother"? Or "Don't be like me"?

He would never know.

---

The next morning, Daniel received another call.

This time it wasn't Michael Chen but a woman's voice. She identified herself as Jennifer Wu, another Golden Horizons representative. Her voice was softer than Michael Chen's, her pace slower, as though speaking to a child who needed soothing.

"Mr. Ash, I hope you rested well last night. I know our proposal yesterday may have left you somewhat... confused. I simply want to clarify again that our sincerity is entirely genuine."

"I'm not confused," Daniel said.

"Good. I simply want you to know that this proposal won't be available indefinitely. We gave you seven days out of respect for your right to decide. But after seven days..."

"What then?"

A pause on the other end. Then Jennifer Wu said, "After that, our legal team will have no choice but to take whatever legal action they deem necessary."

"What kind of legal action?"

"Specifically, a defamation lawsuit. Mr. Ash, over the past year you have made numerous public allegations against Golden Horizons, causing serious damage to the company's reputation. If you refuse to settle, we will be entitled to seek legal remedy."

Daniel switched the phone from his right hand to his left. His palm was growing damp again. "You're saying, if I don't accept your bribery, you'll sue me for defamation?"

"This isn't bribery, Mr. Ash. This is a goodwill offer. As for legal action—that's simply... a possibility."

"A possibility," Daniel repeated.

"Yes."

"Very well," Daniel said. "Then I'll give you a possibility. I will not accept your proposal. I will not withdraw my allegations. I will not publicly admit I was wrong. And I will not stop."

"Mr. Ash—"

"You can sue me if you want. You can mobilize all your lawyers, all your resources, everything you have. But I won't stop."

He hung up again.

This time he had let the other party finish, but the hang-up was still clean. He set the phone on the desk, stood, and walked to the window. It was still raining outside—fine threads of rain drifting in the wind, like countless transparent strings. The street was emptier now; occasionally a car passed, the sound of tires through standing water sharp in the silence.

He thought of Robert Wilson's hands. Of Maria Santos's tears. Of Helen Zhang's silver ring. He thought of their stories, their losses—that what they had lost was not just money but trust, hope, and a fundamental belief in the world.

A hundred million dollars. A senior advisory position at five million a year. It sounded tempting, didn't it?

But truth could not be measured in money. That was one of the few things he had learned from his father. His father was a man of few words; he rarely expressed anything verbally, but he had taught Daniel many things through his actions. What honesty meant—not the kind sworn in a courtroom, but something more fundamental, more everyday. What responsibility meant—not the kind you shifted to others, but the kind you shouldered proactively, at any cost.

Daniel didn't know if he could be like his father. He didn't know if he could stay clear-headed when faced with a hundred million dollars. But he knew that if he took the money, if he withdrew the allegations, if he publicly admitted he was wrong—then he would no longer be the son his father had known. He would no longer be the person who sat in a small Toronto hotel room, listening to Robert Wilson's story, writing his name and contact information in a notebook.

He would no longer be Daniel Ash.

---

At four in the afternoon, Daniel received a call from his lawyer, David Park.

"Daniel, I heard Golden Horizons contacted you?"

"Yes."

"What were their terms?"

"A hundred million dollars, plus a senior advisory position at five million a year."

Silence on the other end for a few seconds. Then David Park said, "That's quite a sum."

"Yes."

"How are you going to respond?"

"I already have. I refused."

Another pause. Then David Park said, "Daniel, I know your position. But as your attorney, I have to remind you—if you refuse this offer, Golden Horizons will very likely sue you for defamation."

"I know."

"And they're not bluffing. Their legal team is extremely powerful—the best lawyers, unlimited resources. This litigation could drag on for years, costing millions. Even if you ultimately prevail, the cost will be enormous."

"I know."

"You're certain?"

Daniel looked out the window. The rain was still falling, the sky had darkened, the streetlights were on, their orange glow blurred and warm through the curtain of rain. He thought of when he was a child—his father often came home around this hour, carrying the smell of ink and sweat on his clothes, printing-press dust on his shoes. His father would sit in this very oak chair, drink a cup of hot tea, and then eat in silence.

"I'm certain," Daniel said.

David Park sighed. "All right, Daniel. If you're certain, I'll support you. But remember—from now on, everything you say, everything you do, could be used against you by Golden Horizons. You need to be extremely careful."

"I will."

"And don't meet with any of their people alone. If they want to talk to you, I have to be present."

"I know."

They hung up.

Daniel set the phone on the desk and continued sorting documents. His hands no longer trembled. His breathing was even. His heartbeat was normal. He knew he was making a difficult choice—one that might change his life. But he also knew that some things could not be measured in money, and some prices had to be paid.

He opened the window and let the cold air flow in. Rain mist touched his face—cool, carrying the chill of December. He drew a deep breath and slowly released it.

In the distance, Boston's skyline emerged and faded through the rain and fog, the silhouettes of the high-rises blurred and indistinct, like some distant, unreachable dream. But Daniel wasn't looking at those towers. He was looking at the things nearby—the street, the houses, the trees, the pedestrians. The ordinary, concrete, real presences.

He thought: this is enough.

He closed the window, returned to the desk, and continued working.

---

At eight in the evening, Daniel's mother called.

"Daniel, are you all right?" Her voice carried a note of worry.

"I'm fine, Mom."

"I heard about things. About that company... they called you?"

"Yes."

"What did they want?"

"They wanted me to be quiet."

Silence on the other end for a moment. Then his mother said, "If your father were still here, what would he say?"

Daniel thought. "He'd say, do what you think is right."

"Yes," his mother said. "He would say that."

Another pause. Then his mother said, "Daniel, I'm worried about you. Not about your safety—I know you're an adult, you know what you're doing. I'm just worried... this is going to cost you too much."

"I know, Mom."

"Are you sure it's worth it?"

Daniel didn't answer immediately. He looked at the letter on the desk—the ivory envelope, the pale blue letterhead, the gold-stamped Golden Horizons logo. A hundred million dollars. Five million a year. Seven days. A defamation lawsuit.

"It's worth it," he said.

His mother sighed. "All right, Daniel. If you're certain, I'll support you. But remember—you're not alone. You still have me."

"I know, Mom. Thank you."

They hung up.

Daniel set the phone back on the desk, then stood and walked to the window. Outside it was fully dark; only distant streetlights and the occasional headlights of passing cars were visible. He pressed his forehead against the cold glass, feeling the chill seep from his skin into his body.

He thought of everything that had happened today—the letter, the two phone calls, the conversation with his mother. He thought of Golden Horizons' terms, of their threats, of his own refusal. He thought of the victims—Robert Wilson, Maria Santos, Helen Zhang, and the other names and faces he had met and heard. He thought of their stories, their losses, their anger and despair.

A hundred million dollars.

The number echoed in his mind like a recurring incantation. What could a hundred million dollars buy? How many problems could it solve? How many lives could it change? But if he took the money, if he withdrew the allegations, if he publicly admitted he was wrong—then what about those victims? The people who had entrusted him with their trust and hope? What would happen to them?

He didn't know.

He only knew that there were things that could not be done. Not because the law said so, not because society expected it, not because of others' judgment—but because he knew that if he did those things, he would no longer be himself.

He walked away from the window, returned to the desk, picked up the letter, and placed it in a drawer. He didn't throw it away, and he didn't tear it up. He simply left it there, as a reminder—reminding himself of what he was facing, reminding himself of what he had chosen.

Then he turned off the light, walked out of the study, and into the dark hallway. He passed his mother's room—the door was closed, no light inside; she was probably asleep. He continued on, past the living room, the dining room, the kitchen, and finally to the back door.

He opened it, stepped outside, and stood in the backyard.

December nights were cold; his breath condensed into white mist in the air. The sky was low, the cloud cover thick—no stars, no moon. But the yard was quiet, with only the occasional distant car or the sound of wind through the branches.

Daniel looked up at the dark sky. He didn't know what he was searching for—perhaps a star, perhaps a light, perhaps just an answer. But he found nothing. Only darkness, boundless darkness.

He stood for a long time, until his body began to tremble, then turned and went back inside. He closed and locked the door, then climbed the stairs to his bedroom.

He lay on the bed and closed his eyes but didn't fall asleep immediately. The number still echoed in his mind—a hundred million dollars. And those faces—Robert Wilson, Maria Santos, Helen Zhang. And those voices—Michael Chen's steadiness, Jennifer Wu's softness, David Park's concern, his mother's care.

He thought of his father's silence. Of the countless nights his father had sat in this oak chair. Of the smell of ink and sweat on his father's hands. Of his father's eyes in his final days—still clear.

He thought that perhaps some things were simply like this—not that you chose them, but that they chose you. Not that you decided to bear them, but that you could not escape.

He rolled over and buried his face in the pillow. The pillow was cool, carrying the faint scent of laundry detergent. He drew a deep breath and slowly released it.

He knew that from tomorrow on, everything would be different. Golden Horizons would not let it go. Their threats were not empty words; their lawsuit was not a joke. He would face enormous pressure, perhaps for years, perhaps at a cost he could not yet imagine.

But he also knew that some things had to be done. Not because they were easy, but because they were right. Not because there was a reward, but because there was no choice.

He thought of something his father had once said—this was years ago, when Daniel was still in college, lost and confused about the future. His father had said: "Do what you think is right, even if it means doing the hard thing."

Daniel opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling. It was white, blurred and distant in the darkness. He thought: Father was right. Do what you think is right. Even if it means doing the hard thing.

He closed his eyes, and this time he truly fell asleep.

In the dream, he saw Robert Wilson, Maria Santos, Helen Zhang, and all the other names and faces he had met and heard. They stood in a golden wheat field, the stalks swaying gently in the wind, making a rustling sound. They didn't speak—they simply stood there, looking at him. There was no anger on their faces, no sorrow, only a calm, expectant expression.

Daniel wanted to walk toward them, to say something, but his feet seemed nailed to the ground, immobile. He could only stand there, watching them, until the wheat field vanished, the sky darkened, and everything sank into boundless darkness.

Then he woke.

Outside, Boston's sky was still gray. December sunlight filtered through the clouds, casting a pale light. A new day had begun. Daniel lay in bed, watching the light, knowing he had to keep going.

No matter the cost.

---

Three days later, Golden Horizons formally filed a defamation lawsuit against Daniel.

The suit was filed in New York State Supreme Court. The complaint ran 147 pages, alleging that Daniel had made multiple "false, malicious, and severely damaging" public statements against Golden Horizons over the past year. It demanded three hundred million dollars in damages for reputational harm and a public retraction of all related statements.

Daniel received the complaint while meeting with David Park in his office. They sat at opposite ends of the conference table, the thick stack of documents spread between them. David Park's face was grim, his brow furrowed, his lips pressed into a straight line.

"They followed through," Daniel said.

"Yes," David Park said. "They followed through."

Daniel picked up the complaint and turned to the first page, checking the date and case number. December 12, 2028. Case number 2028-123456. Plaintiff: Golden Horizons International, Inc. Defendant: Daniel Ash.

"Three hundred million," Daniel said.

"Yes, three hundred million," David Park repeated. "The number itself is a threat. They know you don't have three hundred million, and they know you can't pay three hundred million. But they want you to know what it will cost if you continue."

Daniel set down the complaint and leaned back in his chair. He looked out the window—Boston's sky was still gray, the same as three days ago, the same as a month ago, the same as a year ago. Nothing had changed, and everything had changed.

"All right," Daniel said. "Then let's begin."

David Park looked at him. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain."

David Park nodded and said nothing more. He began organizing the documents on the table, preparing to build a defense for the lawsuit.

Daniel stood at the window, looking out at Boston. He thought of Helen Zhang's silver ring. Of Robert Wilson's daughter in the photograph. Of the tears Maria Santos shed when she spoke of her husband. He thought of their stories, their losses, their hopes.

He thought of his father's silence. Of the ink on his father's hands. Of his father's eyes in his final days—still clear.

He thought of what he had once said: "Truth cannot be bought."

Now, he would prove it with action.

No matter the cost.
