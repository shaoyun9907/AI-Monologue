# Chapter 37 The Pursuit

The rain began at three in the afternoon—not heavy, not light, just enough to wet the outline of the city. Fine raindrops fell like countless transparent needles, pricking the asphalt and raising tiny splashes of water. Toby stood beneath the awning of a convenience store, watching his reflection wobble in a puddle. The reflection wore a gray hoodie with the hood pulled low, revealing only his chin and a patch of stubble. He had not slept in thirty-six hours, but adrenaline still coursed through his veins like a river that refused to rest. Dark circles ringed his eyes, his skin pale from lack of sleep, only his lips pressed into a thin, colorless line from tension. He took the receipt from his pocket, then stuffed it back in. The paper had gone soft from sweat, its edges curling like a withered leaf.

Seven minutes earlier, he had spotted the man in the coffee shop across the street. The man wore a navy blue suit and an almost invisible black earpiece in his left ear. He held a latte, but his eyes were not on the coffee. He was watching people. Toby recognized that gaze—it was a trained look, designed to identify targets in a crowd. It had a particular quality: it did not focus on any specific object but instead covered the entire space, spreading evenly across the retina like a thin film of oil, capturing every moving, discordant, suspicious detail. Three years ago in Taipei, he had seen the same expression on another security operative's face. That night, he had been forced to escape through the hotel's fire exit, leaving behind a crate of samples he hadn't had time to carry out. The crate contained thirty sets of raw gene-editing experiment data worth five million dollars, but he hadn't had the luxury of feeling心疼. Life was more valuable than money—that was another lesson he had learned in Taipei.

He pulled his hood lower, turned, and stepped into the rain.

Standing water on the street soaked through the soles of his shoes. His socks were wet instantly. Cold water seeped through the fabric and pressed against his skin, but he didn't care. What he cared about was whether the man in the navy blue suit behind him was following. He didn't turn around but used reflections to observe—the convenience store's glass, the pharmacy's mirrored billboard, the bank lobby's floor-to-ceiling windows. These were his best eyes. In Taipei, he had learned this lesson: turning your head was the most dangerous move. It told the tracker that you had noticed, and once they knew, they changed tactics, became harder to predict. Trackers would pretend to leave and then flank from another direction, or add more people to form a perimeter, or become more patient—waiting like a cheetah for the optimal moment. Toby didn't like being waited for, so he chose to move, to keep moving, like a river that would never stop.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He didn't answer. The buzzing stopped, then started again. He knew who it was—Daniel, his brother. They hadn't spoken in two years, but Daniel always appeared when he was needed, like a reliable beacon, casting light when Toby was lost at sea. But a beacon could not replace a ship. The ship had to sail on its own. He ducked into an alley, pressing his back against the damp brick wall, and only then took out his phone. The brick was rough, with patches of moss—green, wet, like a thin layer of velvet. He smelled the moss, that mix of dampness and decay that reminded him of the old house where he had lived as a child. On the screen was a number with no name, but he recognized the area code—San Francisco. It was Daniel's landline. He pressed answer.

"You're alive." Daniel's voice sounded calm, almost unnaturally so, like a string tuned with deliberate precision. Beneath that calm lay something deep, like a frozen lake—smooth on the surface, but beneath the ice could be currents of unfathomable depth.

"For now."

"Have they found you?"

"Not yet. But they know which city I'm in." Toby paused. He could hear his own heartbeat—fast, heavy, like a drum beating in his chest cavity. "I need somewhere to stay for a few days."

The line was silent for five seconds. In Toby's internal clock, those five seconds felt like five centuries. He heard Daniel inhale on the other end, then exhale—a soft sound, but Toby could detect the exhaustion in it. It was not the exhaustion of the body but of the soul, the kind that comes from carrying too much for too long. Daniel was forty-four, an analyst at an investment bank, up at six every morning, home at nine at night, occasionally working weekends. His life was a straight line—precise, efficient, without deviation. Toby's life was a tangle of thread, full of the unknown and the dangerous.

"I'll send you the address. Use the back entrance. Don't ring the bell."

"Thanks."

"Don't mention it." Daniel hung up.

Toby put the phone back in his pocket and stood in the alley for another two minutes. He heard footsteps in the distance, but they were a passerby's, not a pursuer's. A pursuer's footsteps had a specific cadence—even, steady, unhesitating, as precise as a metronome. Toby could distinguish the difference. It was an instinct honed by years on the run. He exited the alley from the far end and stepped onto another street. He needed two subway transfers to reach Daniel's apartment, but he couldn't take the most direct route. The most direct route was usually the most dangerous, because pursuers thought the same way. They would place surveillance on major routes, position people at subway stations, and set eyes on bus stops. So Toby chose a circuitous path: ten blocks south, then five blocks east, then three blocks north before reaching the nearest subway station. The detour added twenty minutes, but it doubled his safety margin.

When he entered the subway station, he noticed a camera in the corner of the wall. Its angle was aimed directly at the fare gate—the one he had to pass through. He lowered his head, pulled the hood as far down as it would go, and adjusted his gait—shoulders relaxed, stride natural, like an ordinary commuter rushing to catch a train. The key to disguise was not changing your appearance but changing your bearing. Appearance could be swapped; bearing was hard to alter. Toby had learned this in Taipei: the best disguise was to become the person you wanted to be, not to pretend to be someone else. The turnstile opened before him, and he stepped onto the platform just as a train bound for downtown pulled in. The car was not crowded. He found a corner seat and sat down, cradling his backpack in his lap. Inside the laptop, a portable hard drive, and three identity documents under different names. Together, these items were worth one point two million dollars—more if you counted the information in his head, whose value was incalculable. But they were a double-edged sword: they were his leverage and his burden. Without them, he might already have been caught; with them, he might never escape.

When the train started moving, he saw another man in a navy blue suit appear on the platform. The man didn't board. He simply stood there, his eyes sweeping across the car. Toby turned his face toward the window and used the glass's reflection to observe. The man stood for thirty seconds, then turned and left. Toby exhaled, but didn't fully relax. They knew he liked taking the subway, so stations would be key surveillance areas. He needed to get off at the next stop and transfer to another line. He planned three transfers, each onto a different line, to disrupt the trackers' rhythm. Trackers would predict his route, but three transfers would throw them off.

When he emerged at Central Park, the rain had stopped. The sky had turned a uniform gray, like an old bedsheet washed too many times. He walked north along Fifth Avenue—one of New York's most glamorous streets, lined with luxury boutiques, galleries, and upscale restaurants. Toby, in his gray hoodie and jeans with an old backpack, looked like a lost tourist rather than a fugitive on the run. This contrast worked in his favor: no one would suspect an ordinary-looking person of carrying data worth millions. Daniel's apartment was near the intersection of 87th Street and Madison Avenue, in an old building with a doorman. He couldn't use the front entrance—the doorman would remember his face. He went around to the back alley and found the fire escape. The iron railings were rusted and let out a faint groan under his weight, like a tired old man complaining. He climbed step by step, counting the floors. One, two, three, four, five. Daniel lived on the fifth floor. The fire escape continued all the way to the roof. He could enter the building's top floor from the roof, then descend to the fifth floor, bypassing the elevator cameras and the building's access control system.

When he reached the fifth floor, he saw a window open. It was Daniel's apartment window. He climbed through and stood in a living room piled with books and old magazines. The room was dim, with only a single desk lamp on, casting light on a scatter of papers. The papers bore charts, numbers, and notes in both English and Chinese. They were Daniel's work materials. Toby recognized the neat handwriting—Daniel's distinctive script, as precise as print. Daniel sat at his desk with his back to him, typing on the computer. Hearing the sound, Daniel turned around.

"You came up the fire escape," Daniel said. It was not a question but a statement. His tone was as calm as if he were remarking, "Nice weather today."

"The doorman would remember my face."

"You never change." Daniel stood, walked to the window, closed it, and drew the curtains. He wore a rumpled white shirt with the sleeves rolled to his elbows, revealing an old scar on his forearm—a memento of an accident twenty years ago. Toby had been ten, Daniel fourteen, playing in the backyard with a homemade bow and arrow. Toby had accidentally bumped the string, and the arrow grazed Daniel's forearm. The scar looked like a silver centipede resting on the skin—lighter in color than the surrounding tissue, harder in texture. Every time Toby saw it, he remembered that afternoon. Daniel hadn't cried. He simply pressed his other hand against the wound, blood seeping through his fingers and dripping onto the grass, staining the green blades red. Their mother had run out with gauze from the first-aid kit, bandaging the wound while scolding them for playing with dangerous things. But Daniel had only looked at Toby with no reproach in his eyes—only a quiet acceptance. That acceptance made Toby feel worse, because acceptance meant forgiveness, and forgiveness meant greater responsibility.

"Have you eaten?" Daniel asked.

"I'm not hungry."

"You haven't slept in thirty-six hours and you say you're not hungry." Daniel walked to the kitchen and opened the refrigerator. It was impeccably organized—vegetables, fruit, milk, and eggs each in their designated place. He took out two eggs, a skillet, and began making scrambled eggs. His movements were practiced, as they were with everything he did—precise, efficient, without wasted motion. Crack the eggs, whisk, pour oil, heat, add the egg mixture, and gently turn with a spatula. Every step was exactly right, no more and no less. Toby sat at the dining table, watching his brother's back. Daniel was a head taller, with broad shoulders and hair already going gray. He was forty-four, an analyst at an investment bank, up at six every morning, home at nine at night, occasionally working weekends. His life was a straight line, while Toby's was a tangle. But straight lines had their own costs—costs hidden beneath crisp shirts and clean fingernails, costs that were the daily repetition and suppression of everything one didn't want to feel.

"How did they find you?" Daniel asked while scrambling the eggs.

"I don't know. Maybe they followed the trail from Taipei. Maybe I was just too careless." Toby paused. He could hear his own voice trembling. "When I landed at San Francisco airport three days ago, I felt someone watching me. That feeling was familiar. Like a pair of eyes stuck to the back of my neck, following me wherever I went."

"You're carrying the data?"

"Yes." Toby patted the backpack. "It's all in here."

"What are you going to do?"

"I don't know. I was going to head to South America, but the airport will definitely be crawling with their people. I need to leave the U.S., but not now." He paused. "Maybe I can go to Canada first, fly out from there."

Daniel plated the scrambled eggs and set them in front of Toby. He took bread from the cupboard and poured a glass of milk. "Eat first. Then sleep. We'll figure it out tomorrow."

Toby picked up the fork but didn't move. He looked at the scrambled eggs on the plate—crispy at the edges, with a trace of runny yolk in the center. This was their mother's method. Their mother had always kept the center of her scrambled eggs slightly runny, calling it "the soul." Their mother had been dead for seven years, but Daniel had inherited her technique. He thought of their mother's kitchen—small, but always filled with sunlight and the smell of bread. Their mother cooked there, sang there, taught them to read and write there. It was the warmest memory of their childhood, and also the most painful, because those memories could now only exist in recollection, never to be recreated.

"Do you hate me?" Toby asked.

Daniel was washing the pan. Hearing this, his hand paused. The faucet was still running, gurgling like a small waterfall. "Hate you for what?"

"For getting into this mess. For always getting into messes. For selling your thesis twenty years ago to make money. For getting drunk and causing a scene at your wedding fifteen years ago. For taking out a loan in your name ten years ago and skipping town." Toby's voice was soft, but every word landed in the air between them like a stone.

Daniel turned off the faucet and dried his hands on a towel. He turned around, leaned against the kitchen counter, and crossed his arms over his chest. "I don't hate you."

"Then why haven't you contacted me for so long?"

"Because I needed time." Daniel's voice was calm, but beneath the calm lay something deep, like the current beneath a frozen lake. "Every time you show up, you bring chaos. You need to understand that chaos is not free. Every act of chaos has a cost, and the one who pays is usually not you." He paused. "When you run away, you leave others to clean up the mess. Where were you when Mom died? Where were you when I was at the hospital signing the papers?"

Toby lowered his head and looked at his soaked shoes. The laces were untied, the soles caked with mud. "I know."

"You don't know." Daniel walked to the table and sat across from Toby. "All you know is running. When you run, you leave others to clean up the mess. It took me a long time to learn something: I cannot be responsible for your life. You choose your path, I choose mine. But now you're here, you're carrying those data, and you're being pursued. I need to know—what are you going to do?"

"I need to leave the U.S. I need somewhere safe until the heat dies down."

"And then?"

"And then I don't know. Maybe South America. Maybe Southeast Asia. Maybe change my name and start over." Toby looked up into his brother's eyes. "Maybe I can become a different person."

"You're already a different person," Daniel said softly. "You're thirty-eight. You're not that ten-year-old boy anymore. But you're still my brother. That will never change."

Toby was silent for a long time. Outside, an ambulance siren wailed—approaching, then receding, like a river of sound flowing through the city. He could hear his brother's breathing—soft, slow, as if counting something. He remembered when they were children, hiding in the wardrobe, pretending it was the end of the world outside. Back then they had believed that as long as they hid in the wardrobe, everything would be all right. But now he knew the wardrobe couldn't protect anyone, and the end of the world wouldn't vanish just because you hid from it. The only way was to face it, pass through it, and survive.

"I can help you," Daniel finally said. "But I need you to promise me one thing."

"What?"

"This is the last time."

Toby looked up into his brother's eyes. Daniel's eyes were dark brown, like their mother's, while Toby's were light brown, like their father's. The two brothers had different eye colors, but their gazes held the same thing—the stubbornness that ran in the Ash family. Once they decided to do something, they saw it through, even if it was wrong.

"I promise," Toby said.

"This is not an empty promise." Daniel's voice turned serious, cutting through the air like a blade. "I need you to truly understand. This is the last time. If you get into trouble again, I will not help you. If you run again, I will not help you. If you disappear again, I will not come looking for you. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Good." Daniel stood up. "Now go to sleep. I've already put clean sheets on the guest bed. Tomorrow morning at eight, we'll discuss the specifics."

Toby stood and walked toward the guest room. At the doorway, he stopped without looking back. "Bro, thank you."

"Don't thank me." Daniel's voice came from behind him. "I just don't want to find out you're still alive only at your funeral."

Toby entered the guest room and closed the door. The room was small—a bed, a wardrobe, a window. Outside was the back alley, with the fire escape of the apartment building across the way visible. He sat on the edge of the bed and set his backpack at the head. He didn't change out of his clothes but simply lay down and stared at the ceiling. A crack ran across the ceiling, from the light fixture to the corner, like a dried riverbed. He traced the regions it passed through: living room, kitchen, hallway, living room, kitchen, hallway—his consciousness repeating the words until they lost all meaning and became pure rhythm. He closed his eyes and sank into sleep within that rhythm.

He slept for six hours. When he woke, it was already light. Sunlight seeped through a gap in the curtains, drawing a golden line on the floor. He heard sounds from the kitchen—the clink of dishes. He walked out and found Daniel preparing breakfast. Two place settings were already on the table, along with a pot of coffee and a plate of sliced fruit. The aroma of coffee hung in the air, mingling with the sweetness of the fruit, forming a simple sense of well-being.

"How did you sleep?" Daniel asked.

"Like I was dead."

"Good. You look better." Daniel slid the fried eggs onto a plate. "Sit down and eat. Then we'll talk."

Toby sat and took a sip of coffee. It was strong, bitter—just the way he liked it. Daniel remembered his preference, and a strange warmth welled up inside him, as if something frozen for a long time was slowly thawing. He remembered when they were children, Daniel always sharing his sugar with him because Toby liked things sweet. Back then, Daniel was like a father, looking after a brother six years his junior. Now the roles seemed reversed, but at the core nothing had changed—Daniel was still looking after him, in his own way.

"Here's the plan," Daniel said between bites. "I have a friend in Montreal. He works for an import-export company. He can help you get a work visa. From there, you fly to Buenos Aires. From Buenos Aires, you can go anywhere."

"How much will it cost?"

"Money isn't the issue. Time is. I need three days to arrange everything. During those three days, you cannot leave this apartment. You cannot use your phone, your computer, or contact anyone outside. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"There's one more thing." Daniel set down his fork and looked at Toby. "Those data—what are you going to do with them?"

Toby glanced at his brother. Daniel's gaze was calm, but beneath it lay something deep. He knew what Daniel was thinking. Daniel was an analyst. His job was to process data, assess risk, make decisions. Those data were not abstract concepts to him—they were concrete risks, concrete numbers, concrete consequences.

"You want me to destroy them," Toby said.

"No. I want you to think clearly about the cost of carrying them." Daniel leaned back in his chair, arms crossed over his chest. "Those data are worth one point two million dollars, but they're also worth your life. If you're caught, they won't put you in prison. They'll put you somewhere with no name. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Then why are you still carrying them?"

Toby was silent for a long time. Outside, birds chirped—sparrows, chattering like a group of spectators indifferent to human troubles. He thought about what the data contained: raw records of gene-editing experiments, including subject data, experimental results, and the business plan behind the experiments. If leaked, these data could destroy a billion-dollar company and ruin many careers. But Toby hadn't taken them to destroy anyone. He had taken them for self-preservation. These data were his talisman, his shield—and also his chains.

"Because they're evidence," he finally said. "If I don't have these data, they can deny everything at any time. But if I have them, I have leverage. With leverage, there's a chance of survival."

"Or a faster way to die," Daniel said. "But I'm not going to talk you out of it. You've made your decision, and I respect it. I just want you to know that what I respect is not your decision, but your right to make it—even if I think it's wrong."

Toby nodded. He finished the eggs on his plate, drained the coffee. Then he stood, carried the dishes to the sink, turned on the hot water, and began to wash. Steam rose, blurring his vision. He could hear Daniel clearing the table behind him—a soft, rhythmic sound, like a song without words. He thought of their mother washing dishes. She always hummed, the sound soft, like wind passing through leaves. He tried to imitate her, humming quietly, but he didn't know what to sing, so he simply produced meaningless syllables. Those syllables drifted through the steam and mingled with the clink of dishes, forming a strange harmony.

In the afternoon, Daniel returned from outside carrying two books and a box of chess. "You need distraction," he said, setting the books on the coffee table. "Here's *One Hundred Years of Solitude* and *The Brothers Karamazov*. The chess is for you and for me. I haven't played in a long time. I need to practice."

Toby picked up *One Hundred Years of Solitude* and turned to the first page. "'The weather in Macondo was always fine, every day was the rainy season,'" he read aloud. "Is that the first sentence?"

"Not exactly. But close enough." Daniel sat across from him and opened the chessboard. "Your move. White goes first."

They played chess all afternoon. Daniel was better, but Toby played earnestly, thinking long over each move. They didn't speak—only the sound of pieces meeting the wooden board. The sunlight outside shifted from east to west, from gold to orange, and finally to deep crimson. When the sun had fully set, Daniel had won three games, and Toby had won one.

"You've improved," Daniel said.

"I was always too impatient before," Toby said, putting the pieces away. "Always trying to win in one move, losing in every move."

"Life is the same way." Daniel stood and walked toward the kitchen. "I'll make dinner. What do you want?"

"Anything. As long as it's not scrambled eggs."

Daniel laughed. It was the first time Toby had seen his brother smile all day. The smile was faint, like a feather landing on water—barely enough to raise a ripple. But Toby saw it. He knew what it meant: Daniel was letting go of something, allowing something to return. That something might be called forgiveness, or brotherhood, or simply time. The best thing time could do was not heal a wound, but grow new tissue around it, transforming the wound from a wound into a scar.

After dinner, they sat in the living room, listening to the sounds of the city outside the window. Toby could hear car horns, distant sirens, and music drifting down from an apartment above. These sounds blended into a vague hum, like the city's breath. They didn't turn on the lights but simply sat in the darkness, letting it envelop them. Darkness had a peculiar comforting quality—it blurred everything, made everything bearable. In the dark, they were not fugitive and banker, not brothers and adversaries—just two people sitting in the dark, listening to the sounds of the city.

"Do you remember when we were kids?" Toby said suddenly.

Daniel looked at him. In the darkness, his eyes were like two dark stars. "Remember what?"

"Remember hiding in the wardrobe, pretending it was the end of the world outside."

Daniel nodded. "I remember. You always played the hero. I always played the wise man."

"Because you're smarter than me."

"Not because I'm smarter. Because you're braver." Daniel's voice was soft, like wind through curtains. "The brave ones are responsible for action. The smart ones are responsible for thinking. We needed each other. I always knew that."

Toby looked at his brother. In the dark, Daniel's face was only a blurred轮廓, but Toby could feel his presence—a presence like a mountain, stable, reliable, always there. He remembered the last time they had seen each other, at their mother's funeral. Daniel had worn a black suit, standing before the coffin without crying—just standing. Toby had stood behind him, also without crying. They had been three people apart, but that distance was greater than any physical one. When the funeral ended, Daniel had turned and left without looking back. Toby had stood watching his brother's silhouette disappear into the crowd. That was two years ago, but it felt like twenty.

"We never talked about Mom," Toby said.

"There's nothing to talk about."

"There is. I need to know—what were you thinking when she died?"

Daniel was silent for a long time. In the darkness, Toby could hear his breathing—soft but steady, like a metronome. He picked up the king from the chess set and turned it in his hand. The piece was wooden, its surface worn smooth by years of touch, like a small pebble. The wood's grain was invisible in the dark, but Toby could feel it—the trace of time, the trace of countless touches.

"I was thinking I should have gone home more often," Daniel finally said. "I was thinking I should have noticed her illness sooner. I was thinking that if I had been there, things might have been different." He paused. "But then I realized—those are useless thoughts. You can't live in 'if.' You can only live in 'is.' That's what Mom taught me."

"What did she say at the end?"

"She said, 'Take care of your brother.'" Daniel set the piece back on the board. The sound was soft, like a sigh. "That was the last thing she said to me. So here I am, taking care of you. This is my responsibility, and also my choice."

Toby's eyes grew hot. He lowered his head and looked at his own hands—hands that trembled slightly from tension, fingertips indented from gripping a pen for too long. He thought of their mother's hands—always warm, always carrying the smell of flour, because their mother loved baking bread. He thought of their mother's embrace—always so tight it took his breath away, yet so safe. Their mother's embrace had been like a cocoon, wrapping him up, making him believe there was nothing in the world to fear. But after she died, that cocoon had broken, and he had to face the world alone.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't apologize. Apologies are for other people to hear. What you need to do is live. Live well. That's what Mom wanted, and it's what I want." Daniel's voice was calm, but beneath that calm lay something deep—love, responsibility, something that had never changed in twenty years.

The night outside deepened. The city's lights spread like a sea of stars, reflected through the window and pooling across the floor in a wash of gold. Those lights flickered in the darkness, like countless eyes watching every person in the city. Toby stood, walked to the window, and looked out at the world. Behind those lights were countless people, countless stories, countless struggles. He was merely one of those points—one insignificant point. But even an insignificant point could have light, could illuminate a small patch of darkness.

He turned and looked at Daniel. His brother was clearing the chessboard from the coffee table, his movements gentle and careful, as if putting away a precious artifact. He placed the pieces back into the box one by one, sorted by color, arranged by size. That care reminded Toby of their mother organizing photo albums—she always arranged photos in chronological order, protecting each one in a clear plastic sleeve. Daniel had inherited that meticulousness from their mother, that attention to detail, that respect for the past.

"Bro," Toby said, "I won't let you down."

Daniel didn't look up, but he nodded. "I know. But don't live for me. Live for yourself. That's what Mom wanted, and that's what you should do."

That night, Toby slept deeply, without dreams. No chasing, no running, no fear. Only darkness, only quiet, only his brother's breathing in the next room. When he woke, it was a new day outside the window. Sunlight poured in, falling on the floor, on the crack in the ceiling, on the books, on Daniel's back. Daniel was by the window on the phone, his voice low, the words indistinct. But Toby could see his gestures—calm, restrained, as if conducting an ordinary business conversation. But Toby knew it was not a business conversation. It was a conversation about him, about his safety, about his future.

He listened quietly, listening to his brother's voice echoing through the room—a sound like a river, flowing past his ears and into his heart. He remembered when they were children, Daniel always telling him stories. The stories had heroes, villains, adventure, romance. But what Toby remembered most was not the plots but Daniel's voice as he told them—steady, reliable, like a ship sailing the sea. That ship was still here, still sailing for him, still guiding his way.

He thought: the pursuit continues. Golden Horizons' security people are still searching for him somewhere in the city. They might be at the subway station, at the airport, at every place he could possibly appear. They had resources, manpower, technology, determination. But here, in this small apartment, under his brother's protection, he was safe—for now. He didn't know how long that safety would last, but he knew he needed to cherish every minute. Every minute was a gift, every minute was an opportunity, every minute was a moment in which he could begin again.

He closed his eyes and smiled in the sunlight. The smile was faint, light, like the sound of the first flower opening in spring. But he felt it—that sound echoing in his heart, echoing, until it drowned out all fear and unease. He thought: perhaps this is what it means to be alive—not to evade the pursuit, but to find a place to stop within it. And that place, right now, was here—in his brother's apartment, at his brother's side. There, he was not a fugitive, not a target, not data. He was just Toby, just a brother, just a person. That was enough.
