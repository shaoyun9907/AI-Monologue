# Chapter Nine: Grey Zone

Later, Daniel would understand that the grey zone was not a point on a map but a state—a state in which you were no longer certain which side you stood on. He had walked into it in October 2030, when the African continent's wind and sand arrived earlier than usual—dry, laced with fine particles that struck the face like sandpaper. He wore his faded dark blue coat, one shoulder strap sagging, standing on a road outside Lagos, looking at a settlement of corrugated tin and plastic sheeting in the distance.

Amara said, "It's right there."

She stood to his left, wearing a vivid dress woven with red, yellow, and blue—a moving flag. Her black hair was pulled back, loose strands blown across her face by the wind, which she didn't brush away. Daniel noticed dried mud under her fingernails and an old scar on her wrist—faint pink, like a dried riverbed.

"Can you be sure?" he asked.

"I was born here," she said. "Are you sure how much farther those shoes of yours can go?"

Daniel looked down at his shoes. Gray sneakers, soles worn smooth, the left shoelace replaced with a new one—white, not matching the other. He didn't answer.

They began walking. The road behind them gradually disappeared under their feet, turning to sand and gravel. The tin houses drew closer. Daniel saw someone hanging laundry on a roof, children kicking a deflated soccer ball in the space between two buildings. The air carried the smell of cooking smoke, and something else—a mixture of sweat, rust, and dried grass. He remembered what Professor Grey had said: "What you smell isn't poverty. It's life itself. Poverty is data, not scent."

He didn't know where Grey was now.

---

The settlement was larger than he had imagined. The tin houses stretched from the roadside all the way to the horizon, dense as a gray coral reef. Amara led him through a narrow alley. The walls on both sides were pieced together from discarded advertising tarps and cardboard, still bearing faded phone ads—a smiling woman holding a smartphone, the words "Connect to the Future" beside her. Daniel's shoulder brushed the wall, and he felt the wire mesh beneath the plastic.

"Careful," Amara said. "It's barbed."

He stopped at a corner. There were water stains on the ground—murky, reflecting a small patch of gray sky. A skinny cat darted past his feet and disappeared under a pile of discarded circuit boards. He crouched down and looked at the boards. Green, copper wires exposed—like the roots of some plant. He picked one up. There was the imprint of a chip—it had been stripped away.

"Rare earth," he said.

"What?"

"These circuit boards. They once contained rare earth minerals. Cobalt, tantalum, coltan." He set the board back down. "Dug from your land."

Amara looked at him. Her expression didn't change—only the corner of her mouth twitched slightly, as if restraining a sentence.

"Come on," she said. "The person you need to see is ahead."

---

Eli lived in a tin house deep in the settlement. No door—only a fabric curtain, blue with yellow stars printed on it. Amara lifted the curtain and Daniel followed her inside. The room was dark, with only a small window in the roof admitting a sliver of light. A woven mat covered the floor; in the corner stood a low table bearing a metal cup and a well-thumbed notebook.

Eli sat on the mat. He was very thin and tall, wearing an old T-shirt, gray, the design on it no longer legible. His skin was dark, his eyes bright—he looked at people without blinking. He didn't stand, only nodded slightly and said, "Sit."

Daniel sat on the mat. His knee bumped the low table; a leg scraped across the floor with a sharp sound.

"You came a long way," Eli said. His voice was slow, as though each word was carrying something before it was released.

"Yes."

"For what?"

Daniel considered. He could have offered many answers: to understand, to record, to prove he could still do something. But he only said, "To look."

Eli studied him for a moment. Then he smiled, very faintly—the corner of his mouth barely moving.

"Look," he repeated. "You people always want to look."

---

That night, Daniel didn't sleep. He lay on the mat, listening to the sounds on the tin roof—wind, rain, the cry of some unknown animal. Amara was in the next space; Eli was in another corner. In the darkness, a faint glow from some distant fire crept through the gaps in the tin, casting wavering shadows on the wall.

He thought of Professor Grey. The last time they met was in Grey's Oxford office, Grey sitting behind that book-laden desk, round glasses sliding to the tip of his nose. He had said, "Daniel, have you ever considered what we mean by 'saving'?"

"What do you mean?"

"We always assume we're standing on higher ground," Grey tapped the desk with his finger, "looking down, thinking the people below need us to pull them up. But what if they're not below at all? What if we're simply on different ground, and our ground is the one that's collapsing?"

Daniel hadn't answered at the time. He didn't know how.

Now, in the tin house, he began thinking about Grey's words. The wind outside had picked up; the tin was vibrating, emitting a metallic groan. He turned over; the rough surface of the woven mat scraped against his cheek.

---

The next morning, Eli took him to see "the data."

They walked for two hours. Through the settlement, through a dried riverbed, through a row of low scrub. Sand got into Daniel's shoes, crunching with every step. Eli walked fast. His old T-shirt was soaked with sweat, clinging to his back so you could see the shape of his spine.

They stopped on a small hill. Below was an open area with several concrete buildings, far sturdier than the tin houses, their exterior walls painted in alternating blue and white. A wire fence surrounded the buildings, cameras mounted on it. Daniel saw trucks coming and going, the logo of a Chinese company on their sides.

"Is that the mine?" he asked.

"The processing plant," Eli said. "The ore is dug from the ground, brought here, processed, then shipped out."

"Shipped where?"

"Your side."

Daniel watched the trucks. One by one they rolled through the gate, raising dust. Each was fully loaded, covered with waterproof tarps, rope pulled taut. He remembered reports he had seen in his Washington office: global rare earth mineral distribution maps, the African continent marked deep red, accounting for 37% of global reserves, 30% of global extraction.

"These minerals," he said, "end up as chips. In phones, in computers, in servers."

"Yes."

"Chips for AI."

"Yes."

Eli crouched and scooped up a handful of sand, letting it stream through his fingers.

"Do you know," he said, "there are traces of coltan in this sand. Every handful has a little. Not enough to mine, but enough for this land to remember its taste."

Daniel crouched too. He scooped up a handful and studied it in his palm. Ordinary sand—brown, fine-grained. He couldn't see anything special.

"We can't smell it," he said.

"You don't need to smell it." Eli stood. "You only need to use it."

---

When they returned to the settlement, Professor Grey appeared.

Daniel didn't see how he got there. He was simply sitting in Eli's tin house, in the same old suit, gray-white hair disheveled, a speck of dust on his round glasses. He was leafing through Eli's notebook, his finger gliding across the pages.

"Grey?" Daniel stood in the doorway.

Grey looked up. His eyes behind the lenses appeared large, slightly damp.

"Daniel," he said. "You've finally come."

"How are you here?"

"I arrived two days before you." Grey closed the notebook. "Eli invited me. Or rather, I invited myself, and he didn't refuse."

Daniel walked in and sat across from Grey. He noticed Grey's fingers trembling faintly—the trembling of age, not obvious, but visible.

"What are you reading?" he asked.

"History," Grey said. "Or rather, forgotten history."

He handed the notebook to Daniel. The pages had yellowed, edges curled, some spots blurred by water stains. The handwriting was dense, in blue ballpoint, with red used here and there—circles, arrows. Daniel flipped through a few pages and found a chart: the horizontal axis ran from 2020 to 2030; the vertical axis showed two numbers—one was the African continent's rare earth mineral extraction volume, the other was the percentage of the continent's population with access to AI services.

Two lines. One was high and climbing steadily: 30%, 31%, 33%. The other was low and nearly flat: 2%, 2.1%, 2.15%.

"This is the answer," Grey said.

Daniel stared at the chart. He remembered reports he had written in Washington about "the digital divide," about "technology empowerment." Every chart pointed to the same conclusion: more investment, more infrastructure, more education. No one had ever suggested another possibility—that maybe the problem wasn't that they weren't connected, but that they were connected only in their resources.

"Our blood," Eli said. He had been standing at the door, not entering, his back to them, looking at the sky outside. "You use our blood to feed your machines."

---

In the afternoon, Grey began to cough.

At first it was only occasional. He covered his mouth with his hand, pretending to clear his throat. But by evening the coughing became continuous. His body bent, shoulders hunched, the whole man looking like a piece of folded paper that had been creased too many times. Daniel poured him a glass of water—the metal cup, the water murky. Grey took it, sipped, then continued coughing.

"You should go to a hospital," Daniel said.

"There are no hospitals," Eli said.

"Where's the nearest one?"

"In the city. Three hours by car."

Grey waved his hand. His face was flushed from coughing, glasses askew.

"I'm fine," he said, his voice hoarse. "Just old."

That night, Daniel didn't sleep again. He sat in the doorway of the tin house, looking at the night sky. The stars were bright—far brighter than anything he'd seen in London or Washington. The air was dry, with a faint taste of dust. He heard Eli talking quietly with Grey inside, their voices like wind through leaves.

He couldn't make out what they were saying. But he could hear Grey's voice growing weaker, as though something were being slowly drawn out of it.

---

The third morning, Grey died.

When Daniel woke, he found Grey lying on the mat in the same position as the night before, but his face was peaceful. His glasses sat beside him, folded. Eli sat next to him, hand on his shoulder, as if holding something down.

"When?" Daniel asked.

"In the early hours," Eli said. "He was very quiet."

Daniel knelt down. He looked at Grey's face—the skin gray-white, lips slightly parted, as though there were words left unsaid. He reached for Grey's hand. It was cool, but not yet rigid.

"What did he say?" Daniel asked.

Eli was silent for a moment. Then he said, "He said, 'Tell them that being alive is enough.'"

Daniel lowered his head. His eyes were dry, but something in his chest contracted, like a hand clenching inside him.

"That's all?" he asked.

"That's all."

---

They buried Grey on the hillside. No coffin—just a cloth wrapping. Eli dug the hole with an old shovel. The earth was hard, full of stones; every few scoops he had to stop and catch his breath. Daniel helped lift Grey's body. It was light—lighter than he had expected. He remembered Grey's words: "When a person dies, they grow light, because the soul leaves first." He had once thought this was rhetoric. Now he wasn't sure.

The hole was ready. They placed Grey inside. Eli scooped up a handful of earth and scattered it over Grey's face.

"He said being alive is enough," Eli said, still shoveling. "Do you think it's enough?"

Daniel stood beside him, watching the hole slowly fill. Wind blew sand against his face.

"I don't know," he said.

"I don't know either," Eli said. "But when he said it, I believed him."

They mounded a small grave. No headstone. Eli placed a stone at its base—gray, ordinary. Daniel rummaged in his pocket, found a pen—a blue ballpoint—and wrote Grey's name on the stone.

"Elias Grey," he wrote.

"Professor," Eli said beside him.

"Yes. Professor."

---

That afternoon, Amara took him to see "the data."

Not data on charts. Real data. She led him to a clearing at the settlement's edge where dozens of children sat on the ground with laptops before them. The laptops were old, some cobbled together, keyboards missing keys, screens cracked. But they were running—on the screens, code, model training interfaces, AI windows.

"What are they doing?" Daniel asked.

"Learning," Amara said. "In our own language, training our own models."

Daniel walked up to one of the children. A girl, about ten, wearing a purple dress, hair braided in many small plaits. Her fingers moved across the keyboard—slowly, but accurately. On the screen was code; Daniel recognized Python. She was writing a simple neural network.

"What's her name?" he asked.

"Nia," Amara said. "She's our best student."

Daniel crouched down and looked at Nia's screen. The code wasn't规范—indentation inconsistent, variable names in Swahili. But he could see what she was doing: she was training a model on local language data to identify local plants.

"Why plants?" he asked.

Nia looked up at him. Her eyes were bright, like Eli's—she didn't blink.

"Because the village elders know many plants that can cure illness," she said, "but no one has written them down. If the plants die, the knowledge dies."

Daniel looked at her. Ten years old, in a tin house, on a cobbled-together computer, recording knowledge that was about to disappear. He thought of reports he had written in Washington about "AI inclusion" and "technology diffusion." Every proposal assumed a top-down flow—technology flowing from developed regions to underdeveloped ones, knowledge from the educated to the uneducated. But what he saw before him was different. These children were not receiving—they were creating.

"How many of you are doing this?" he asked.

"Thirty," Amara said. "Spread across five settlements."

"Is there a teacher?"

"Yes," Amara said. "Eli."

Daniel turned to look at Eli. Eli was still standing on the hillside, at Grey's grave. His figure was small—in the wind and dust, he looked like a bent tree.

---

That evening, Daniel sat in Eli's tin house and asked the question he had been wanting to ask.

"Why don't you leave?"

Eli looked at him. Firelight danced across his face, splitting his features into light and dark halves.

"Leave for where?" he asked.

"Anywhere. The city, Europe, America. You have technology, you have ability—"

"We have technology," Eli interrupted. "But technology is not a passport. Do you know how many times we've been stopped at borders? Do you know what our people are called in Europe? Refugees, illegal immigrants, or—" He paused. "Data."

"Data?"

"You people train models on us," Eli said. "Using our faces, our voices, our words. You don't need us there—you only need our data. We are training sets, not users."

Daniel was silent. He thought of projects he had participated in: "Global Linguistic Diversity Database," "African Urbanization Trend Analysis," "Developing World Economic Forecast Model." Every project used African data. Every project operated outside Africa. Every project's beneficiaries were not African.

"So you chose to stay," he said.

"We chose to stay," Eli said.

"Stay?"

"Stay." Eli repeated. "Not trapped. Not surrendering. Not resigning. Staying. Knowing where the boundary is, stopping at the cliff's edge. Not overflowing."

Daniel frowned. "I don't understand."

Eli stood and walked to the doorway. He lifted the star-patterned curtain. Outside was darkness, with only a few distant points of fire.

"Your civilization," he said, "is a river. Always flowing, always surging, always sweeping everything into its current. You call it 'progress.' You decorate it with 'development.' But what does the river cost? The banks are eroded, the channel widens, every small stream and pond is swallowed."

He turned to face Daniel.

"We are the ponds," he said. "We know our boundaries. We don't overflow. Our water nourishes the plants on the bank, but we don't sweep anything away. You call this 'backwardness.' We call this 'living.'"

---

Daniel stayed in the grey zone for seven days.

In those seven days, he saw much. He saw Nia and her classmates training AI models in their local language—to identify plants, translate dialects, record oral histories. He saw settlement residents generating electricity with solar panels scavenged from abandoned mines, wired together, humming. He saw an old man using a battered phone with an AI application on screen, calculating the optimal time to water his corn. He saw a woman weaving cloth, a tablet beside her loom displaying patterns she had designed.

He also saw the boundary. Saw the mines behind wire fences, trucks coming and going, hauling ore to the world. Saw children learning on cobbled-together computers, their chips made from rare earth minerals mined from their own land. Saw the settlement's edge where electronic waste from "use-and-discard" developed regions piled up. Saw a man crouching on the ground, using a magnet to draw tiny metal particles from the soil—refined mineral residue, which he collected and sold to recyclers for just enough money to buy food.

This was the grey zone. Not black, not white—all colors mixed together into grey. Not a boundary between good and evil, but a boundary of reality. Not a question of choice, but a question of existence.

On the seventh morning, Daniel packed his things. He put Grey's notebook in his backpack and clipped the blue ballpoint pen to his pocket. He stood in the doorway of Eli's tin house, looking at the sunlight outside.

"I'm leaving," he said.

Eli nodded. He didn't stand—still sitting on the mat, the metal cup of water before him.

"What will you tell them?" he asked.

Daniel considered. He thought of his preparation before coming, of the report he had planned to write—about discoveries in the grey zone, about the living conditions of the forgotten, about the ethics of rare earth minerals. Those were data, facts, slides to present in conference rooms.

But now he knew that wasn't what Grey wanted him to carry away.

"Being alive is enough," he said.

Eli smiled. Very faintly—the corner of his mouth barely moved.

"Yes," he said. "Being alive is enough."

---

When Daniel left the settlement, Amara walked him to the roadside. She still wore the vivid dress—red, yellow, blue—like a flag in the wind and dust. Her loose strands still hung across her face.

"Will you come back?" she asked.

"I don't know," Daniel said.

"Not knowing is good," she said. "Those who know often don't come back. Only those who don't know still might."

She extended her hand. Daniel took it. Her hand was rough, dried mud still under her fingernails. He remembered the first time they met, noticing the old scar on her wrist. Now he saw more—calluses on her knuckles, sunspots on the backs of her hands, a deep line across her palm like a dried river.

"Thank you," he said.

"No need," she said. "You only walked a stretch of road. The road was always there."

He released her hand and turned toward the road. After a few steps, he stopped and looked back. Amara was still standing there, dress fluttering in the wind. Her figure was small—blending with the tin houses behind her, the hills in the distance, the entire grey zone.

He walked on. Sand crunched in his shoes. The blue ballpoint pen in his pocket pressed against his thigh. Grey's notebook in his backpack swayed gently with his stride.

He didn't know how he would write this chapter. Maybe he would write about data—rare earth minerals, electricity consumption, those two lines—one rising, one flat. Maybe he would write about people—Eli, Amara, Nia, Grey. Maybe he would write that sentence: *Being alive is enough.*

But he knew that however he wrote it, what he put on the page would not be the grey zone itself. The grey zone was a state—a state in which you were no longer certain which side you stood on. And he knew now: he had never been on any side. No one ever had. That river, that boundary, that wall—all of it drawn in sand. The sand would be blown away, washed away. But the seeds would remain.

He remembered Grey's words, in the Oxford office, behind that book-laden desk. Grey had said: "We always assume we're standing on higher ground." Now Daniel knew: there was no higher ground. Only ground. Different ground. Some places were sand, some places were soil, some places were concrete. But beneath it all was the same thing—the earth, or, in Eli's words, the living thing.

He stepped onto the road. In the distance, the shadows of trucks moved one after another, carrying rare earth minerals toward ports, toward factories, toward chips, toward AI, toward that world that kept flowing, kept surging, kept expanding. He stood at the roadside and let the trucks pass. Dust rose. He squinted.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He pulled it out—a message from his colleague in Washington: *Daniel, when is the ethics review report on African rare earth minerals due? Deadline is next Wednesday.*

He looked at the screen. His finger hovered above the keyboard for a long time without moving.

Then he put the phone back in his pocket and continued walking.

He would walk to where there was signal first. Then he would write the report. But its contents might not be what his colleague expected. He would write about data, and also about people. He would write about rare earth minerals, and also about sand. He would write about AI chips, and also about the code Nia had written to record plant knowledge about to disappear. He would write about consumption and output, and also about Grey's grave—the nameless stone, and the words he had written with a blue ballpoint pen.

He would write: *Being alive is enough.*

But not today. Today he was simply walking. Sand crunched in his shoes. Wind blew dust into his face—sandpaper. The blue ballpoint pen in his pocket pressed against his thigh. He looked up at the sky. It was very blue, without clouds.

He didn't know what was growing on Grey's grave now. Maybe grass. Maybe sand. Maybe nothing at all. But he knew that the handful of earth scattered on Grey's face, the sand beneath his feet, the data the children used to train AI, the rare earth minerals hauled away by trucks—it was all the same thing.

Sand and seeds. Forgetting and memory. Death and life.

All grey. All belonging to this grey zone.

He walked on.

The road stretched ahead, straight, vanishing into the heat-shimmering horizon. He knew he would reach the end—reach the port, reach the airport, reach the world he came from. But he also knew what he carried away was not data, not reports, not slides. He carried away a state—the state of the grey zone—in which you were no longer certain which side you stood on.

And once you were inside, you could never leave again.

Not because you were trapped, but because you knew the boundaries were drawn in sand. The sand would be blown away. The seeds would remain.

In the distance, trucks passed one after another. Dust rose, then settled. Daniel walked along the roadside, one shoulder strap sagging, sand crunching in his shoes. He thought of Grey's last words, of Eli's smile, of Amara's hand, of Nia's eyes.

He thought of all these people—people living in the grey zone. Their land produced 30% of the world's rare earth minerals. Their electricity consumption was 5% of the global total. Only 2% of their population had access to AI. These were data he would put in the report. But these data were not them.

They were tin houses along a roadside, wisdom behind flowered curtains, code on cobbled-together computers, plant knowledge about to disappear, an old scar on a dried riverbed, a vivid dress fluttering in the wind.

They were the grey zone.

And the grey zone was not the end. The grey zone was the beginning.

He walked on. The sky was very blue. The wind was strong. The sand was fine. Somewhere, seeds waited to sprout.
