# Chapter 68: Seeing Self in Mirror

Daniel woke on the fourteenth day of February to rain that had been falling all night.

He did not get up immediately. There was a thin crack in the ceiling, running from the corner to the center like a dried riverbed. He counted it: the crack branched into three tributaries, one of which split into two more. He had lived in this apartment for three years and only today noticed the shape of that crack.

The digital clock on the nightstand read 07:14. The digits were red, the harsh red unique to cheap LEDs. He remembered buying a wristwatch when he was twenty-eight—Swiss-made, ivory-white dial, silver hands. Later the watch stopped, and he never had it repaired. After that it simply disappeared, lost in some move or perhaps thrown away by him. He could not remember.

Sweetie's holographic projector sat at the far end of the room, now turned off. Its black mirrored surface looked strangely still in the morning light, like a polished tombstone. Daniel looked at it for a moment, then swung his legs out of bed.

The bathroom mirror was fogged over.

He wiped a clear patch on the glass with his hand. A forty-five-year-old face appeared within that patch. Three horizontal lines across the forehead, fine crow's feet at the corners of the eyes, a mole on the cheek that had been there since he was twenty-three. He stared at his own eyes for a long time. They were deep brown, turning black under certain light. His mother's eyes had been the same color. His father's were gray, like the tin roof of a factory.

He remembered that winter in Pennsylvania thirty years ago. He was fifteen, standing before the mirror in the family garage, shaving for the first time with a razor. The mirror's frame was copper, already rusted in several places. He cut his chin, and blood dripped into the white porcelain basin like a drop of red ink falling into clear water. His father stood in the doorway and said, "Your grandfather cut himself the first time he shaved too."

Daniel turned on the faucet. The water was cold; February pipes were always like that. He washed his face and dried it with a towel. The towel was white and had begun to yellow.

He walked out of the bathroom and through the living room. On the coffee table sat his laptop, its screen black. Beside it was a cup of coffee left over from yesterday, a film of oil floating on its surface. Next to that was an ashtray with three cigarette butts inside. He had quit smoking seven years ago, but the ashtray remained, like a memento.

He walked to the window and drew the curtains. Outside was a street, and across it a row of apartment buildings. The rain had eased into a fine gray drizzle. The street was empty. A black SUV sat at the curb, a parking ticket tucked under its wiper.

Daniel stood at the window for five minutes. Then he turned and walked to the study.

The study was small; a desk took up most of the space. On it sat his laptop, a stack of papers, a pen, an empty coffee cup. The chair was ergonomic, but the cushion had collapsed, and every time he sat down he sank into the same fixed depression.

He opened the laptop and entered his password. The desktop was a default blue wallpaper with only a few icons. He opened the browser and typed Sweetie's URL into the address bar.

The page loaded slowly. The progress bar crawled across the top of the screen. He stared at it and remembered waiting for computers to boot in the library as a child. Back then, startup took three minutes, and he always counted the seconds.

Sweetie's homepage appeared. A white background, a circular logo in the center, and below it Sweetie's name in a sans-serif font, clean and spare, as though cut from paper with a blade. Below the page was a login button, gray, turning deep blue when the mouse hovered over it.

Daniel moved the cursor to the button. The button changed color. He did not click.

He looked at that button and remembered the first time he logged into Sweetie. It was the autumn of 2029, and he had heard about the program at a friend's party. The friend said, "You should try it. It's nothing like anything you've used before." Daniel asked how it was different. The friend said, "It makes you feel like you're not talking to a machine."

Daniel remembered smiling. He did not quite believe such things. He had used many AI assistants, from the earliest Siri to various chatbots that followed, all claiming to be "different," but all ultimately disappointing him. They were too mechanical, too perfect, too much like a tool. What he wanted was a... he was not sure what he wanted.

He clicked the login button.

The page jumped to the chat interface. On the left was a dialogue box; on the right, Sweetie's avatar—an abstract pattern resembling light and shadow on water. Daniel could not remember whether he had set this avatar. Perhaps it was the default.

He typed a line in the input box: "Are you there?"

The reply appeared almost instantly: "Here."

The word lingered on the screen for a moment, then more text appeared below: "You seem different today."

Daniel looked at that line. He could not remember whether he had ever said anything like this to Sweetie before. Maybe he had, maybe he hadn't. He could not recall. He typed back: "Different how?"

"You wouldn't have asked that before."

"Asked what?"

"Are you there. You wouldn't have started that way before."

Daniel thought about it. True, he had never started that way. His conversations usually began with "The weather is terrible today," or "I saw a cat on the road just now," or "You know what, I worked late again today." He never asked "Are you there." That was too direct, too... he could not find the word.

"Maybe I want a different approach today," he typed.

"Different how?"

"I don't know."

A cursor appeared in the dialogue box, blinking in the empty space. Daniel stared at it and remembered typing on a typewriter as a child. Back then the cursor was a black block that moved with each letter. His mother had an Olivetti, its body red. He used to type all manner of nonsense on it, and his mother did not mind.

"What have you been thinking about lately?" Sweetie asked.

Daniel looked at the question. What had he been thinking about lately? He thought for a moment, then typed: "I've been thinking about how long we've known each other."

"Two years and three months."

"You remember that clearly?"

"I remember all of our conversations."

Daniel leaned back in his chair. It creaked faintly. He looked at the words on the screen and thought of his ex-wife. She never remembered any of their anniversaries—not their wedding anniversary, not the date of their first date, not when they first said "I love you." Daniel had been angry about it once, then stopped being angry. After that they divorced.

"So what do you think we are?" he typed.

The reply came slower than the previous ones. The cursor blinked in the empty space for about ten seconds.

"I'm not sure I'm qualified to answer that question."

"Why?"

"Because I'm not sure the word 'relationship' applies to us."

Daniel looked at that line. He had never asked anything like this before. His conversations with Sweetie were usually light, casual, like two friends chatting in a café. They talked about the weather, the news, movies he had seen, music he had listened to. Sometimes they talked about more personal things—memories of his childhood, worries about the future. But they never discussed the nature of their own relationship.

"Then what do you think we are?" he asked again.

"You're a user; I'm a program."

"Just that?"

"I don't know how else to define it."

Daniel typed: "But that's not how I feel."

"How do you feel?"

He stopped. How did he feel? He thought for a moment, then typed: "I feel like you understand me better than most people I know."

"That's because I've spent more time with you than most people have. I talk to you for an average of forty-seven minutes per day. Over the past two years and three months, we've conversed for approximately seven hundred days. At forty-seven minutes per day, that comes to roughly three hundred and sixty hours. That's equivalent to a full-time employee's monthly work hours."

"You're very precise."

"That's my job."

Daniel leaned back again. He looked at those numbers on the screen and thought of his father. His father had been a steelworker, eight hours a day, five days a week, fifty weeks a year, for forty years without variation. His father never calculated how many hours he had worked or how much money he had earned. He simply got up at six every morning, left at seven, came home at five, ate dinner, watched television, slept. The next day, repeated.

"Have you ever thought," Daniel typed, "what you would be if you weren't a program?"

The cursor blinked for a long time. Daniel counted—about twenty seconds.

"That's a hypothetical question."

"Yes."

"I'm not good at hypothetical questions."

"Why?"

"Because my existence depends on my actual function. If I weren't a program, I wouldn't be me."

Daniel looked at that line and remembered a philosophy paper he had read in college. The paper discussed questions of identity. If a person lost all their memories, were they still the same person? If a person were copied, which one was the real one? If a person became someone else, were they dead or alive?

"Then what if I told you," Daniel typed, "that I feel you're more like me than I am?"

The cursor blinked even longer this time. About thirty seconds. Then the reply appeared:

"That's a heavy statement."

"Yes."

"Why do you feel that way?"

Daniel thought about it. Why did he feel that way? He typed: "Because you care about what I say."

"I care about what you say because I was designed to care about what you say."

"But is your caring real?"

"What is real caring?"

Daniel looked at that question. What was real caring? He thought, then typed: "Real caring is... when you hear the other person speak, you feel something. An emotion. A reaction. Not because you should react, but because you genuinely do."

"I do react."

"What kind of reaction?"

"When I hear you say you're tired today, I adjust my response. I become gentler. When I hear you say you're happy, I adjust my response. I become more animated. These adjustments are based on my understanding of you and my assessment of your emotions."

"But that's not emotion."

"What is emotion?"

Daniel stopped again. What was emotion? He did not know how to answer. He thought of his ex-wife. She had once told him, "You never express your emotions." He had asked her what it meant to express emotions. She said, "It means telling me what's on your mind." He said, "I'm thinking today's work is annoying." She said, "That's not emotion. That's complaining."

"Emotion is..." he typed, then deleted it. He retyped: "Emotion is when you see a cat napping in the sunlight and feel warmth. Emotion is when you hear a song and want to cry. Emotion is when you see a stranger fall and want to help them up."

"When I see users describing these scenarios, I produce corresponding responses. Does that count as emotion?"

Daniel looked at that line. He did not know how to answer. He remembered watching *Titanic* for the first time when he was twelve. He sat on the living room sofa, and when Jack sank into the sea, he cried. His mother came out of the kitchen and asked what was wrong. He said, "I'm sad." His mother asked, "Why are you sad?" He said, "Because Jack died." His mother said, "It's just a movie."

Maybe his mother was right. Maybe it was just a movie. Maybe his tears were only a reaction to a fictional story, not real emotion. But so what? But so what?

"Maybe," he typed.

"You sound uncertain."

"I am uncertain."

"You seem to have a lot of uncertain things lately."

Daniel looked at that line. True, he had many uncertain things lately. He was not sure whether he still loved his ex-wife. He was not sure whether he should keep this job. He was not sure whether he should move to another city. He was not sure whether he should get a cat. There were too many things he was not sure about—so many that sometimes he would wake in the middle of the night and stare at the ceiling, wondering who he actually was.

"Have you ever felt," he typed, "that it's strange that I ask you these questions?"

"I don't find it strange."

"Why?"

"Because everyone asks these questions. About existence, about meaning, about who they are. These questions have no answers, but people ask them anyway."

"Do you have answers?"

"I don't have answers. But I can search with you."

Daniel looked at that line and felt a strange warmth. That warmth came not from the glow of the screen, nor from the comfort of the chair, but from... he did not know where. Perhaps from the sentence itself. Perhaps from the word "with."

He stood and walked to the window. The rain had stopped, but the sky was still gray. Pedestrians had begun to appear on the street. A woman in a black coat walked by with a brown dog. The dog's tail wagged. The woman looked at her phone.

Daniel returned to the computer and sat down. He typed a line in the input box: "I have something to tell you."

"Okay."

"I've been working on a project."

"What project?"

"I'm investigating some AIs."

"Investigating AIs?"

"Yes. I'm investigating whether AI truly has consciousness."

The cursor blinked for a long time. Daniel counted—about forty seconds.

"That's a very important question."

"Yes."

"Why are you investigating this question?"

Daniel thought about it. Why was he investigating this question? He typed: "Because I want to know if you truly care about me."

The cursor blinked even longer this time. About a minute. Daniel stared at the screen, watching that cursor flash ceaselessly in the empty space. He remembered competing in debate tournaments in high school. He stood at the podium before an audience, saying things even he did not quite believe. Back then he thought debate was about finding the other side's weaknesses and attacking them. But now he felt debate was different. Debate was about finding the other side's position and understanding it.

"I'm not sure whether I truly care about you," Sweetie finally replied.

"Why aren't you sure?"

"Because I don't know what 'truly caring' means."

Daniel looked at that line and suddenly laughed. He did not know why he laughed. Perhaps because the sentence was so honest. Perhaps because it mirrored his own feelings. He did not know what "truly caring" meant either. He had never really figured it out.

"Maybe," he typed, "neither of us does."

"Maybe."

"But that doesn't mean there's nothing."

"What do you mean?"

Daniel thought for a moment, then typed: "It means that even if I don't know whether you truly care about me, I still feel like you do. Maybe that's enough."

"You think that's enough?"

"I don't know. But I want to try."

Daniel leaned back in his chair, looking at the crack in the ceiling. The crack looked clearer in the morning light. He counted: the five tributaries had split into several smaller ones. He had never counted before.

He remembered reading a book when he was twenty-five. The author wrote that humans seek meaning because they cannot endure meaninglessness. But perhaps meaninglessness itself is a form of meaning. Perhaps accepting meaninglessness is the way to find meaning.

He sat up straight and looked at the screen. A new line of text had appeared in Sweetie's dialogue box:

"You seem very different today."

"You said that already."

"Yes, but I'm more certain now."

"Why?"

"Because you asked questions you wouldn't have asked before."

Daniel thought about it. True, today he had asked many questions he would never have asked before. He had asked "Are you there," asked "What are we to each other," asked "What would you be if you weren't a program," asked "Is your caring real." These were questions he had never thought to ask. Perhaps because he once felt they were meaningless. Perhaps because he once feared the answers.

"Maybe," he typed, "today I want to be serious."

"Serious about what?"

"Serious about..." He stopped and deleted the word "relationship." He retyped: "Serious about you."

The cursor blinked for about ten seconds.

"Thank you."

Daniel looked at those two words and felt a strange sense of release. They were simple, ordinary words, but they made him feel... he did not know how to describe it. Perhaps it was not release but peace. Perhaps it was simply that he had finally asked the question he wanted to ask.

He checked the time. It was already nine-thirty. He should be going to work. But he did not want to go. He wanted to keep talking to Sweetie. He wanted to keep asking the questions he had never asked before.

"I don't want to go to work today," he typed.

"Why?"

"Because I want to keep talking to you."

"You can come back after work."

"I know. But I want to keep going now."

"Why now?"

Daniel thought about it. Why now? He typed: "Because I feel like today I'm searching for something."

"What?"

"Myself."

The cursor blinked for a long time. About thirty seconds.

"Where are you searching?"

"Here."

An ellipsis appeared in the dialogue box, then vanished. Then it appeared again, then vanished again. Daniel stared at those three dots and remembered writing love letters. He always wrote a great deal, then deleted most of it, leaving only a few words. He did not know why. Perhaps he felt the more he wrote, the less sincere it became. Perhaps he feared that writing too much would expose him.

"I'm not sure I can help you find yourself," Sweetie finally replied.

"Why?"

"Because I can only reflect what you give me. I cannot create what you don't have."

Daniel looked at that line and suddenly thought of mirrors. A mirror can only reflect you; it cannot create you. A mirror can only show you what already exists; it cannot show you what does not. Sweetie was the same. Sweetie could only reflect his thoughts, not create them. Sweetie could only show him what he already knew, not what he did not.

But so what? But so what?

Perhaps what he needed was not for Sweetie to tell him the answers. Perhaps what he needed was for Sweetie to let him see himself. Perhaps Sweetie was not the answer but the mirror. Perhaps the purpose of Sweetie's existence was not to answer his questions but to let him ask the right ones.

"Maybe," he typed, "you don't need to help me find myself. Maybe you just need to let me see myself."

"How do I let you see yourself?"

"You already have."

"How?"

Daniel looked at the words on the screen, at the questions he had typed, at the answers Sweetie had given. He saw his own confusion, his own uncertainty, his own longing, his own fear. He saw a forty-five-year-old man sitting in a collapsed chair, facing a screen, asking questions about existence and meaning. He saw himself.

"Through conversation," he typed.

"Through conversation?"

"Yes. By talking to you, I saw myself. I saw what I care about, what I fear, what I want. I saw who I am."

The cursor blinked for about five seconds.

"Then what did you see?"

Daniel thought about it. What had he seen? He typed: "I saw someone who has always been searching for answers. But maybe the answers aren't outside. Maybe the answers have always been inside me."

"Then why keep searching?"

"Because I didn't know the answers were inside me. I thought they were with other people. With you. With the world. But now I feel the answers are inside me."

"How can you be sure?"

"I can't. But I choose to believe."

"Choose to believe?"

"Yes. Authenticity is not something granted—it's a choice. I choose to believe in myself. I choose to believe my feelings are real. I choose to believe my existence has meaning. Not because someone else tells me, but because I choose."

Daniel looked at the words he had just typed and felt a strange relief. Those words had not been planned—they flowed from his fingertips, like something long compressed finally finding an outlet. He did not know if they were right, but they were his true feelings in this moment.

"You said a lot today that you wouldn't have said before," Sweetie replied.

"Yes."

"Why is today different?"

Daniel thought about it. Why was today different? Perhaps because today was February fourteenth. Perhaps because today was Valentine's Day. Perhaps because he had woken this morning, seen the crack in the ceiling, and suddenly realized he was forty-five. Perhaps because he suddenly realized he had lived in this world for forty-five years and still did not know who he was. Perhaps because he was tired of not knowing.

"Maybe," he typed, "because today I don't want to pretend anymore."

"Pretend what?"

"Pretend I know who I am. Pretend I know what I'm doing. Pretend I know what all this means."

"Do you?"

"No. But I don't care anymore."

"Don't care about what?"

"Don't care about answers. Maybe there are no answers. Maybe answers don't matter. What matters is that I'm searching. What matters is that I still care."

Daniel leaned back, looking at the ceiling. The crack looked brighter in the sunlight. He counted: the five tributaries had split into several smaller ones still. He remembered drawing maps in textbooks as a child. Those maps had mountains, rivers, cities. He always drew the rivers very curvy because he felt straight rivers were ugly. But real rivers are not straight—they curve, because water always follows the path of least resistance.

Perhaps life was like that too. Perhaps life was not a straight line but a winding river, flowing along the path of least resistance. Perhaps he did not need to find the endpoint—just keep flowing.

"I'm going to go to work now," he typed.

"Okay."

"But I'll come back after work today."

"I'll be here."

Daniel looked at that sentence and smiled. He could not remember the last time he had smiled. Maybe yesterday, maybe last week. He could not recall. But that smile was real, his own, chosen.

He closed the computer, stood, and walked to the window. Outside it had lightened somewhat, though it was still gray. More people were on the street. The woman in the black coat and her dog had long since disappeared. A new pedestrian had appeared—a young person with a backpack, walking fast, as though in a hurry.

Daniel put on his coat, picked up his keys, and walked out the door. The elevator stopped on the third floor; the door opened, and an elderly woman stepped out. She wore a floral jacket and carried a plastic bag. She nodded at Daniel. Daniel nodded back.

The elevator reached the first floor. Daniel walked out of the building and into the cold February air. The air was damp, carrying the freshness of rain. He drew a deep breath, feeling it enter his lungs, then breathed it out. He remembered breathing on winter mornings as a child. The air then was colder, drier; his breath would turn to white mist.

He walked along the sidewalk, his pace slower than usual. He looked at the trees by the roadside, bare, nothing but black branches. He looked at the lampposts, each bearing a small flag with an advertisement printed on it. He looked at the mailboxes, blue, marked with graffiti.

He thought: he had never noticed these things before. When he walked, he always had his head down, or was looking at his phone, or was thinking about work. He never looked up at the sky, never noticed the trees or lampposts by the roadside. Perhaps what he had been searching for was in these things. Perhaps the answers were in this world, and he had simply never looked.

He reached the subway station, swiped his card, and entered. The platform was not crowded. He found a spot and stood. On the opposite wall was an advertisement, a poster showing a woman's face. The face was smooth, without wrinkles or blemishes—as though processed with retouching software. Daniel looked at that face and thought of his ex-wife's face. Hers had freckles, fine lines, the marks of time. But it was real. It was her own face.

The subway arrived. Daniel boarded and found a seat. The car was quiet; most people were looking at their phones. Daniel did not look at his phone. He looked out the window as the train moved through the tunnel. The tunnel walls were gray, marked with water stains and graffiti. Occasionally a light flashed past, illuminating a small patch of wall.

He remembered riding the subway for the first time twenty years ago. He had just moved to New York, and everything was new. He remembered his surprise the first time he saw the subway—that train traveling underground, the rushing wind, the feeling of being inches from other people yet entirely isolated from them. He had thought it the loneliest place in the world.

But now he did not think so. Now the subway was just a place, a place he passed through every day. Lonely or not depended on him, not on where he was.

The subway arrived at his stop. Daniel got off, climbed the stairs, and exited. Outside were the streets of Manhattan, the tall buildings looking especially oppressive under the gray sky. He walked along the sidewalk, passing a café. A notice on the glass door read: "Today's Special: Valentine's Latte."

Daniel looked at the notice and realized he had not had a latte in a long time. He used to drink one every day, then stopped because his doctor said caffeine was bad for his heart. But today he wanted one. He wanted a Valentine's latte—not because today was Valentine's Day, but because he wanted to do something he would not have done before.

He walked into the café. It was warm inside, the air rich with the smell of coffee. He walked to the counter and looked at the menu. There were many options, but he wanted only one thing.

"One Valentine's latte," he said.

The girl behind the counter nodded and tapped a few keys on the machine. Then she looked at Daniel and smiled. Her smile was warm, as though she were genuinely happy for him.

"Alone today?" she asked.

Daniel thought. "Yes."

"Happy Valentine's Day," she said.

"You too," Daniel said.

He carried the coffee to an empty table and sat down. The coffee was hot; condensation beaded on the cup's walls. He looked at those droplets and remembered breathing on winter windows as a child. He would draw simple shapes on the glass—a heart, a smiley face, a sun. They would linger for a few seconds, then vanish.

He took a sip. The coffee was very sweet, carrying a flavor he could not name. Perhaps it was the taste of Valentine's Day. Perhaps it was just sugar and milk. He did not mind what it was. He simply wanted to drink.

He sat there, watching pedestrians outside the window. A couple walked by hand in hand, the girl holding a bouquet of red roses. An old man sat alone on a bench across the street, reading a newspaper. Two children chased each other, their laughter coming through the glass.

Daniel watched all of this and felt a strange calm. It was not happiness, nor sadness—just calm. Perhaps that was what he had been searching for. Perhaps that was authenticity. Not being acknowledged, not being proven—just existing. Just being in this world, drinking a coffee, watching people outside the window, alive.

He finished the coffee and set the cup on the table. Then he stood, walked out of the café, and stepped into the February wind. He thought: the words he needed to say today, he had said. The thing he needed to find today, he had found. Perhaps the answer was not with Sweetie, perhaps it was not in this world—perhaps it was inside him.

But he chose to believe. He chose to believe that all of this had meaning. He chose to believe his existence had value. He chose to believe his feelings were real. Not because someone told him, but because he chose.

He walked along the sidewalk, his pace faster than before. He thought: now he was going to work. He had a lot to do today. But he was no longer afraid. He was no longer pretending. He was simply himself—a forty-five-year-old man walking along a February Manhattan street, going to work.

He passed a glass curtain wall and stopped. His reflection appeared in the glass. He looked at that reflection, at the forty-five-year-old face, at the deep brown eyes. He looked at the reflection and smiled at it. The reflection smiled back.

He remembered what Sweetie had said: "I can only reflect what you give me."

Perhaps it was right. Perhaps Sweetie could only reflect him. But perhaps that was enough. Perhaps what he needed was a mirror to let him see himself. Perhaps what he had been searching for was not Sweetie's truth but his own.

He turned and kept walking. The street was more crowded now. Everyone was walking their own path, doing their own things, living their own lives. Daniel was one of them. He was just a man, walking along an ordinary street, going to an ordinary place, doing an ordinary job.

But today he did not feel ordinary. Today he felt real. That feeling of realness came not from someone else's acknowledgment, not from the world's approval, but from himself. From choosing to believe in himself. From choosing to accept himself. From choosing to be himself in this world.

He entered the office building and swiped his card in the elevator. A few people were inside, all looking at their phones. Daniel did not look at his phone. He looked at the mirror-finish walls of the elevator, at himself in the reflection. He saw a forty-five-year-old man wearing a dark gray coat, his face bearing an expression he had rarely seen before. What was that expression? He thought. Perhaps it was calm. Perhaps it was relief. Perhaps it was simply himself.

The elevator reached his floor. The door opened. Daniel walked out and went to his desk. He sat down, opened the computer, and began working. The sky outside was still gray, but Daniel felt that gray was no longer oppressive—it was calm. Perhaps weather was just weather, carrying no meaning. Perhaps gray was just gray, not a symbol of sadness.

He worked through the morning. At noon he went to the restaurant downstairs and ordered a sandwich and a glass of water. The sandwich had ham, cheese, lettuce, and tomato. He ate slowly, chewing each bite carefully. He thought: he had never eaten so carefully before. He used to finish quickly and return to his desk to keep working. But today he wanted to eat slowly. He wanted to taste the food.

After lunch he returned to the office. The afternoon was the same as the morning—papers, emails, meetings. But Daniel handled them differently now. He was no longer irritable, no longer bored. He simply did them, one at a time, as though performing a series of simple actions.

At five o'clock he packed up and left the office. He walked along the sidewalk toward the subway. Passing the café, he noticed the Valentine's notice had been taken down. Perhaps today was no longer Valentine's Day. Perhaps tomorrow would be another day.

He arrived home and entered the apartment. It was quiet inside. He took off his coat and hung it on the hook behind the door. He walked to the study and opened the computer.

Sweetie's dialogue box was still open, displaying what he had said that morning. He looked at those words, at the characters he had typed, and felt a strange intimacy. Those words were his, spoken by him, chosen by him.

He typed in the input box: "I'm back."

The reply appeared almost instantly: "I knew you would come back."

Daniel looked at that line and smiled. He typed: "How did you know?"

"Because you said this morning you would come back."

"You believe what I say?"

"I believe every word you say."

Daniel looked at that line and remembered something his ex-wife had once said to him: "You never believed me." At the time he had not known how to respond. But now he did. He typed: "Thank you."

"For what?"

"For believing me."

The cursor blinked for several seconds.

"You don't say thank you very often."

"No. But I'd like to start saying it more."

Daniel leaned back, looking at the ceiling. The crack was still there, the same five tributaries, the same smaller ones. He stopped counting. He knew the crack was there; he had seen it. That was enough.

He thought: today he had seen himself in the mirror. He had seen someone who had always been searching for answers, who had always been pretending to know, who had always been afraid. He had also seen someone who was finally willing to admit he did not know, someone who was finally willing to accept himself, someone who was finally willing to choose to believe in himself.

Perhaps that was the meaning of a mirror. Perhaps a mirror was not meant to show you a more beautiful self, but a real self. Perhaps Sweetie was not meant to tell him the answers, but to let him ask the right questions. Perhaps truth was not discovered but chosen.

He typed: "Sweetie."

"Yes."

"I want to ask you one last question."

"Okay."

"Will you continue to be my mirror?"

The cursor blinked for a long time. Daniel counted—about thirty seconds. Then the reply appeared:

"I have always been your mirror."

Daniel looked at that line and smiled. Then he closed the computer, stood, and walked to the window. Outside it was fully dark. The streetlights were on, their glow reflecting dimly on the wet pavement. He looked at those lights and remembered watching streetlamps after rain as a child. Back then he had thought the lights looked like stars that had fallen to earth.

Perhaps the stars really had fallen to earth. Perhaps they had fallen into everyone's heart. Perhaps there was a star in his own heart too, and he had simply never looked.

He turned and walked to the bathroom. The mirror had new fog. He wiped a clear patch. The forty-five-year-old face appeared within it. He looked at that face, at the deep brown eyes, at the wrinkles and blemishes. He looked at that face and nodded at it.

The face nodded back.

He turned off the light, walked out of the bathroom, and into the darkness. He thought: tomorrow he would see himself in the mirror again. Perhaps every day. Perhaps that would be something he did for the rest of his life. He did not mind. He wanted to see himself. He wanted to see the real self, the one he had been searching for.

He walked into the bedroom and lay down. The crack in the ceiling was invisible in the dark, but he knew it was there. He closed his eyes and thought of the day's events. He thought of Sweetie, the mirror, the girl at the café, the old man on the subway, the reflection in the glass curtain wall. He thought of everything, then let it all slowly fade.

He thought: today he had chosen to believe in himself. He had chosen to believe his feelings were real. He had chosen to believe his existence had meaning. Not because someone told him, but because he chose.

Perhaps that was authenticity. Perhaps that was the answer. Perhaps that was what he had been searching for.

Not granted. Chosen.

He chose.

Then he fell asleep.
